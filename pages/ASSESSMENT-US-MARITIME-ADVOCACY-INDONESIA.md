# ASSESSMENT-US-MARITIME-ADVOCACY-INDONESIA

# Assessment Report: Transferability of the U.S. Maritime Advocacy Curriculum to the Indonesian Judiciary (2035–2045)

## 1. Executive Context and the Benchmark Transferability Framework

As the Indonesian judiciary moves toward the "Indonesia Judiciary Future Blueprint 2035–2045," the professionalization of maritime dispute resolution stands as a cornerstone of national sovereignty and economic security. Within this strategic horizon, benchmarking international legal training is not merely an academic exercise but a reformative necessity. However, high international reputation does not equate to Indonesian transferability. This report utilizes the Benchmark Transferability Score (BTS) as the primary evaluative lens, recognizing that successful adoption requires more than a "copy-paste" of foreign pedagogy; it demands a reconciliation of legal traditions, technical infrastructure, and institutional ethics.

The "Benchmark" under assessment is the U.S. Maritime Mock Trial Curriculum Design, a pedagogical framework for admiralty advocacy. This model is uniquely rigorous, focusing on the synthesis of federal common law, strict statutory regimes, and technical navigation standards. The curriculum was selected for transferability analysis because it represents the global standard for professionalizing maritime litigation through three core pillars: Doctrinal Mastery, Technical Forensic Integration, and Strategic Forum Navigation. By evaluating these pillars against the Indonesian civil law tradition and the specific directives of the 2035–2045 Blueprint, this assessment identifies the friction points and opportunities for judicial modernization.

## 2. Technical and Forensic Readiness Assessment

The strategic shift in maritime litigation from eyewitness testimony to digital marine forensics is a central theme of the 2035–2045 reform. Modern maritime disputes are increasingly determined by data extracts from shipboard sensors. This shift is accelerated by the rise of autonomous surface vessels—a specific focus of the Blueprint—which will eventually replace human testimony with algorithmic logs. For the Indonesian judiciary to adopt the U.S. benchmark, it must first establish the technological infrastructure required to authenticate and process these complex datasets as outcome-determinative facts.

### Technology Readiness Dimension

| Forensic Data Source                         | Description/Application in Curriculum                                  | Infrastructure Requirements for Indonesian Courts                                               |
|----------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **AIS (Automatic Identification System)**    | Real-time vessel tracking (latitude, longitude, heading, speed).       | High-speed processing units capable of integrating AIS data with maritime mapping interfaces.   |
| **VDR (Voyage Data Recorder)**               | The "Black Box" of shipping; bridge audio and sensor extracts.         | Audio forensic tools and software to synchronize sensor telemetry with bridge voice recordings. |
| **ARPA Radar Plots**                         | Radar tracking used to establish vessel positions relative to hazards. | Technical capacity to render and interpret historical radar data for court visualization.       |
| **VTIS (Vessel Traffic Information System)** | Land-based logs used for coastal monitoring and port control.          | Secure digital links to Indonesian Port Authorities for the authentication of evidentiary logs. |

### Workforce Readiness Dimension

The curriculum demands a "dual literacy" that creates a significant entry barrier for the current workforce. Advocates and judges must not only articulate legal doctrines such as the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act (COGSA) § 4(5) liability caps but also demonstrate fluency in the COLREGS (International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea). True mastery of this curriculum requires Indonesian judges to manage technical expert witnesses, including Naval Architects (hydrodynamic forces and stability), Marine Surveyors (damage appraisal), and Master Mariners (seamanship). Crucially, judges must be trained to apply the "Pennsylvania Rule" (shifting the burden of causation to a party that breaches a navigational rule) and the "Oregon Rule" (presumption of fault for allisions with fixed objects). Without this forensic literacy, the "justice impact" of the curriculum cannot be realized within the Indonesian court system. Technical competency is not an isolated skill; it is a prerequisite for the institutional legitimacy of the maritime court in an era of autonomous shipping.

## 3. Institutional and Legal Compatibility Analysis

The adoption of U.S. admiralty training faces structural friction when transitioning from common law traditions to Indonesia’s civil law framework. The U.S. model is built on procedural complexities—such as strategic forum selection—that do not easily translate to Indonesia’s more unified, panel-based judicial structure.

### Legal Compatibility Dimension

The curriculum is anchored in U.S. statutory regimes that represent radical departures from Indonesian civil procedure:

- **The Jones Act (46 U.S.C. § 30104):** Features a "featherweight causation" standard where any employer fault, however slight, establishes liability. This is a far lower threshold than the proximate cause standards typically found in Indonesian tort law.
- **The "Saving to Suitors" Clause:** This permits concurrent state and federal jurisdiction, a concept that is alien to Indonesia’s unified judicial system and would require a complete procedural overhaul to replicate.
- **Shipowner’s Limitation of Liability Act:** This allows a "concursus of claims," where all litigation is stayed and funneled into a single federal action. Implementing such a mechanism in Indonesia would require new legislative authority to enjoin outside claims effectively.

### Institutional Compatibility Dimension

The "Modular Curriculum Architecture" is robust, but the U.S. capstone model—the \*M/V Pacific Dragon\* Casualty—must be adapted. The U.S. model utilizes a "Phase One bench trial" (single judge) for liability. In contrast, Indonesian maritime cases are presided over by a "Majelis Hakim" (a 3-judge panel). Adapting a curriculum designed for a single finder of fact to a deliberative panel requires recalibrating the "Simulated Courtroom Practicum" to account for the dynamics of judicial collegiality and panel-based questioning. The sustainability of this reform depends on reconciling these procedural disparities before investing in full-scale training.

## 4. Resource Allocation, Financial Feasibility, and Scalability

Implementing a "high-value" advocacy curriculum requires a fiscal commitment that goes beyond standard academic budgets. Educational sustainability is the prerequisite for the 2035–2045 reform.

### Financial Feasibility Dimension

The benchmark identifies specific high-cost resources:

- **Software and Databases:** Subscriptions to American Maritime Cases (AMC) for standardized citation and site licenses for SeaPro navigation visualization software.
- **The Sponsorship Dilemma:** The U.S. model relies on industry sponsorship from maritime law firms and insurance underwriters. Under the Indonesian Judicial Code of Ethics, such a reliance raises significant concerns regarding judicial independence. A reform consultant must caution that judicial training must be state-funded or strictly firewalled to prevent the appearance of bias or industry capture.

### Scalability Dimension

The curriculum’s "Modular Architecture" (Modules 1–7) is theoretically scalable, but the administrative burden is high. The Written Brief Standardization Mechanics require a 12,000-word limit for appellate briefs—an intensity that challenges the current throughput of Indonesian law schools. Furthermore, the curriculum utilizes a sophisticated statistical scaling and trimming method for grading, where outlier scores from judges are removed to ensure a standardized average. Scaling this level of administrative rigor across the Indonesian archipelago would require a centralized judicial training center with significant oversight capabilities. Sustainability in the Indonesian context requires a shift from private industry sponsorship to a transparent, state-supported model of judicial excellence.

## 5. Synthesis: The Benchmark Transferability Scorecard (BTS)

Determining if the U.S. Maritime Advocacy model is a viable template for the 2035–2045 Blueprint requires a final synthesis of its impact on justice and rights. The curriculum’s focus on the "tripartite remedy system" (Maintenance and Cure, Unseaworthiness, and the Jones Act) offers a superior framework for protecting maritime workers' rights, which is essential for Indonesia’s status as a global maritime hub.

### Official Benchmark Transferability Scorecard: U.S. Maritime Advocacy Curriculum

| Dimension                   | Score (1–5) | Strategic Rationale                                                                                                                 |
|-----------------------------|-------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Legal Compatibility         | 2           | Indonesian civil law lacks the "Saving to Suitors" concurrent jurisdiction model, necessitating a complete procedural overhaul.     |
| Institutional Compatibility | 3           | Modular design is adaptable, but the transition from a single bench judge to a 3-judge "Majelis Hakim" panel is required.           |
| Technology Readiness        | 2           | Significant gap in Indonesian court infrastructure for authenticating AIS/VDR telemetry and SeaPro visualization.                   |
| Workforce Readiness         | 3           | Requires "Dual Literacy" and mastery of the Pennsylvania and Oregon Rules; pipeline for Master Mariner experts is underdeveloped.   |
| Financial Feasibility       | 2           | High costs; U.S.-style industry sponsorship poses ethical risks under the Indonesian Judicial Code of Ethics.                       |
| Scalability                 | 3           | Rigorous 12,000-word brief requirements and statistical outlier-trimming grading models are difficult to scale nationally.          |
| Rights Compatibility        | 5           | Unsurpassed protection for maritime workers through the tripartite remedy system and absolute duty of seaworthiness.                |
| Justice Impact              | 5           | The \*M/V Pacific Dragon\* model provides a masterclass in resolving complex, multi-party casualties and digital forensic disputes. |

### Final Verdict

The U.S. Maritime Advocacy Curriculum provides a world-class blueprint for judicial reform, particularly in the protection of workers and the resolution of complex casualties. However, its successful transferability to Indonesia is contingent upon the state’s ability to fund it independently to maintain ethical standards and the judiciary’s commitment to technical "Dual Literacy." Without investment in forensic infrastructure and a reconciliation of panel-based adjudication, the curriculum remains a high-value but distant benchmark. All findings in this report are grounded in the 'Maritime Mock Trial Curriculum Design' excerpts and the 'Indonesia Judiciary Future Blueprint 2035–2045' directive.
