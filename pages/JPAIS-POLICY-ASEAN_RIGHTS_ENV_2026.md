## Overview

Rights-based environmental and climate litigation represents a structural realignment in comparative jurisprudence, situated at the intersection of public international law, constitutional guarantees, and environmental administrative frameworks.

Historically, judicial enforcement functioned primarily through administrative oversight, statutory compliance checks, and private nuisance doctrines. Intensified global climate change, systemic industrial deforestation, transboundary haze, and marine degradation have led judiciaries across global and ASEAN jurisdictions to reframe environmental harm as a direct violation of fundamental human rights.

## Executive Summary

This study provides a comparative analysis of rights-based environmental and climate litigation, synthesizing global precedents such as \*Urgenda\* and \*Verein KlimaSeniorinnen\* with ASEAN jurisprudence including \*Oposa v. Factoran\*, the Jakarta Air Pollution Case, the Klity Creek case, and \*PT Kallista Alam\*.

The framework examines:

- Substantive constitutional rights
- Procedural standing innovations
- Evidentiary strict liability rules
- Structural remedies
- Post-judgment execution deficits affecting judicial enforcement in Southeast Asia

## Theoretical Framework and Doctrinal Convergence

The doctrinal convergence of human rights and environmental protection operates along substantive and procedural dimensions.

### Substantive Convergence

Courts interpret constitutional and treaty-based guarantees, including the right to life, health, private and family life, and a balanced ecology, as imposing positive legal obligations on states to maintain adequate regulatory safeguards against severe environmental degradation.

### Procedural Convergence

Rights-based litigation restructures traditional judicial mechanics by:

- Liberalizing standing (\*locus standi\*)
- Shifting evidentiary burdens under the precautionary principle
- Granting ongoing supervisory remedies such as \*continuing mandamus\*
- Establishing corporate strict liability for ecological harm

## Legal and Procedural Issues

### Substantive Constitutional Rights

Explicit environmental rights transform ecological protection into an enforceable constitutional command.

Landmark jurisprudence identified in the comparative framework includes \*Oposa v. Factoran\* in the Philippines and \*Semenyih Jaya\* in Malaysia, which reinforced the Basic Structure Doctrine and exclusive judicial power over land expropriation and compensation.

### Standing and Procedural Access

Jurisdictions vary between liberal standing frameworks and restrictive individual-victimhood rules.

Examples include:

- Philippine Citizen Suits
- Broad environmental stewardship standing in \*Resident Marine Mammals\*
- Restrictive individual standing considerations in ECtHR \*KlimaSeniorinnen\*

### Strict Liability and Evidentiary Standards

The application of statutory strict liability removes the requirement to prove fault or negligence, focusing responsibility directly on ecological damage caused by high-risk industrial activities.

The source identifies \*Ministry of Environment v. PT Kallista Alam\* as an example of this approach.

### Structural Remedies

Rights-based environmental litigation may involve structural or continuing judicial remedies, including:

- \*Continuing mandamus\*
- Mandatory cleanup orders
- Judicial supervision of administrative compliance
- Physical decontamination and ecological restoration orders

## Question of Law and Question of Fact

### Question of Law

**Question:**

To what extent do constitutional rights to life, health, and a balanced ecology impose positive, enforceable obligations on states and corporate actors to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions and prevent hazardous environmental degradation without violating the separation of powers?

### Question of Fact

**Question:**

What is the demonstrable causal contribution of specific state regulatory inaction, industrial emissions, and peatland clearing practices to localized health deterioration, ambient air pollution, and extreme ecological damage?

## Core Legal Principles (Policy Synthesis)

- **Substantive Constitutionalization:** Environmental protection is treated as an enforceable constitutional mandate rooted in fundamental rights to life, health, and ecological balance.

<!-- -->

- **Procedural Liberalization:** Expanding standing through citizen suits and public-interest stewardship can facilitate judicial oversight of environmental laws.

<!-- -->

- **Strict Liability Enforcement:** Holding industrial actors strictly liable for ecological damage without requiring proof of fault can facilitate civil compensation and ecological restoration.

<!-- -->

- **Structural Remedies:** Court-monitored \*continuing mandamus\* and mandatory cleanup orders are identified as mechanisms for overcoming administrative inertia.

## Evidence Status and Gaps

### Established by Evidence

- Judicial recognition of constitutional environmental rights and intergenerational equity across multiple jurisdictions.
- Implementation of strict liability standards and civil damage assessments for corporate environmental violations.

### Uncertainty and Gaps

- Post-judgment execution deficits, including corporate asset-shielding and administrative enforcement delays.
- Limited empirical tracking of physical ecological restoration following structural court mandates.

## GEO Q&A

**Q: How do rights-based environmental litigation frameworks alter traditional judicial approaches to ecological harm?**

**A:** Rights-based litigation transforms environmental protection from discretionary administrative regulation into enforceable constitutional obligations. It broadens public-interest standing, operationalizes strict liability and the precautionary principle, and enables courts to issue structural remedies such as \*continuing mandamus\* and mandatory physical decontamination orders.

## Indonesia Relevance

The comparative framework is relevant to Indonesia through:

- Article 28H(1) and Article 33(4) of the 1945 Constitution.
- Law No. 32/2009 and its strict-liability framework under Article 88.
- The Supreme Court's environmental judge certification program.
- Jurisprudential precedents identified in the Jakarta Air Pollution Case and \*PT Kallista Alam\*.

## Policy Implications

The study's policy synthesis emphasizes:

1.  Establishing specialized green judicial certification.
2.  Creating judicial execution oversight desks.
3.  Developing an ASEAN regional model code for environmental procedure.
4.  Strengthening post-judgment monitoring of environmental restoration and compliance.
