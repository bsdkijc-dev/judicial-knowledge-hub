# Judicial-policy-traber-unequalrepresentation-2021

**Social status, political priorities and unequal representation** is an empirical comparative political science and public policy research paper authored by Denise Traber (University of Basel), Miriam Hänni (SFIVET), Nathalie Giger (University of Geneva), and Christian Breunig (University of Konstanz), published in the *European Journal of Political Research* (EJPR) in 2021 (print edition Vol. 61, pages 351–373, May 2022).

## Summary

This study analyzes how social status (measured by occupational and income group: high, middle, and low status) shapes citizens' political priorities at the agenda-setting stage of policymaking, and tests whether European governments are systematically more responsive to issues considered important by high-status citizens. Combining Eurobarometer survey data (2002–2016) from 10 European countries with legislative output data from the Comparative Agendas Project (CAP), the study demonstrates that **when a priority gap exists, governments significantly prioritize the legislative agenda of high-status citizens and neglect the key concerns of low-status citizens**.

## Background

Representative democracy models assume that all citizens have an equal voice. Earlier literature confirmed that governments favor the preferences of wealthy citizens over poor citizens. However, prior research focused almost entirely on final policy preferences rather than the earlier stage of policymaking — agenda setting. If the problems of low-status citizens are denied entry onto the parliamentary agenda ("agenda denial"), they are effectively excluded from having their problems addressed by the state.

## Objectives

1\. Test whether high-status and low-status citizens hold divergent priorities ("priority gap") regarding which issues require government attention. 2. Test whether governments and parliaments across 10 European countries pass more laws in the policy domains prioritized by high-status citizens. 3. Evaluate the robustness of agenda bias across different government systems and cabinet ideological orientations.

## Scope

The research covers a sample of 10 European countries (Austria, Belgium, Denmark, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Netherlands, Spain, and the United Kingdom) over the period 2002–2016, along with in-depth time-series analyses for Germany, Spain, and the United Kingdom.

## Key Provisions / Empirical Findings

- **Existence of the Priority Gap:** Low-status citizens prioritize **unemployment, crime, inflation, and social housing**, while high-status citizens prioritize **the general economic situation, education, health, and the environment**.
- **Agenda Responsiveness Bias:** Government legislative output is positively and significantly driven by high-status priorities (β = +5.05, p \< 0.001), while low-status priorities show a negative relationship once high-status demands are controlled for (β = -3.80, p \< 0.001) \[53, 58–60\].
- **Magnitude of the Bias:**
  - When there is no priority gap between groups, roughly 5% of adopted laws fall within that policy domain.
  - When an issue is 20 percentage points more important to high-status citizens than to low-status citizens, the share of adopted legislation jumps to **~15%** (a 300% increase).
  - Conversely, as an issue becomes more important to low-status citizens, government legislative responsiveness actually declines.
- **Independent of Cabinet Ideology:** The bias toward affluent groups' agenda occurs under governments across the entire ideological spectrum, whether left-wing or right-wing.

## Implementation

The methodology was implemented through fractional logit regression models with country and year fixed effects, and a lagged time structure (matching year-t survey priorities to laws adopted from August of year t through July of year t+1). The research was funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation (Ambizione Grant PZ00P1_174067/1), the DFG (Grant 414/16), and the ERC (Advanced Grant No. 741538).

## Policy Issues & Causal Mechanisms

Three primary causal mechanisms drive the agenda bias \[24–28\]: 1. **Unequal Political Participation:** Lower turnout and lower rates of contacting officials among low-income citizens reduce their visibility to politicians. 2. **Influence of Money & Business Lobbying:** Wealthy campaign donors and business-dominated interest groups act as privileged sources of information for lawmakers. 3. **Socialization of Political Elites:** Professional politicians disproportionately come from affluent backgrounds, leading them to instinctively prioritize issues that match their own life experiences.

## Reform / Implications

1\. Strengthening collective representation and lobbying access for less-affluent groups through labor unions. 2. Reforming candidate recruitment mechanisms within political parties to bring in more working-class candidates (analogous to institutional gender quotas).
