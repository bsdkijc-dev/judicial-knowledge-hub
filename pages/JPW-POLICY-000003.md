# JPW-POLICY-000003

The **Indonesian Contract Law Reform and Obligations Framework** (Stable ID: `JPW-POLICY-000003`) is a national statutory and policy reform initiative designed to overhaul Indonesia's law of contracts and obligations. Developed by the National Law Reform Agency (*Badan Pembinaan Hukum Nasional* - BPHN) in collaboration with the Institute of Developing Economies (IDE-JETRO), the policy establishes the *Academic Draft for a Bill on the Law of Obligations* (*RUU Tentang Perikatan*). The framework aims to replace the 1848 Dutch colonial Civil Code (*Burgerlijk Wetboek* / KUHPerdata Book III) with a modern, unified national contract law harmonized with the UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts.

## Summary

The framework addresses the severe legal pluralism and obsolescence inherited from Dutch colonial legislation. It outlines a transition from rigid colonial codification to an "Open System" of contract law that incorporates statutory protections against gross disparity, hardship, and unconscionable standard contracts. The draft comprises 140 articles covering general obligations, contract formation, performance, non-performance, torts (*perbuatan melawan hukum*), voluntary management (*zaakwaarneming*), and undue payment.

## Legal Basis

The framework draws authority from constitutional and statutory sources:

- **Constitutional Basis:** 1945 Constitution of the Republic of Indonesia (Arts. 5(1), 20(1), and Article II Transitional Regulation).
- **Colonial Civil Code:** Civil Code 1848 (*Burgerlijk Wetboek* / KUHPerdata Book III, Arts. 1233–1850).
- **Population Classification Law:** Netherlands Indies State Law 1855 No. 2 (*Staatsblad* 1855 No. 2, Arts. 131 and 163).
- **Modern Economic Legislation:** Act No. 1 of 1995 (Limited Liability Companies); Act No. 32 of 1997 (Futures Trading); Act No. 5 of 1999 (Anti-Monopoly); Act No. 8 of 1999 (Consumer Protection); Act No. 42 of 1999 (Fiduciary Securities).

## Background & Policy Problems

### Background

Under Dutch colonial administration, Indonesian contract law was divided among population groups: Europeans (governed by the Civil Code), Foreign Orientals, and Indigenous Indonesians (governed by Adat law). Following independence in 1945 and the Foreign Investment Law of 1967, the Civil Code was extended to indigenous business transactions, creating a confusing mix of Civil Code rules, Adat law, and Anglo-American corporate clauses. The post-1998 *Reformasi* movement catalyzed efforts to establish the supremacy of law, transparency, and clean business governance.

### Policy Problems

- Lack of *stare decisis* and regular judicial digests leading to legal unpredictability.
- Lopsided foreign investment contracts shifting operational risks onto local partners.
- Proliferation of unconscionable standard contracts by corporate monopolies.
- Executive overreach via delegated legislation (Ministerial Decrees overriding Parliamentary Acts).

## Objectives

1\. Replace outdated 1848 Dutch colonial rules with a unified national Law of Obligations. 2. Align national contract law with international commercial standards (UNIDROIT Principles). 3. Protect socio-economically weaker parties against gross disparity and abusive standard terms. 4. Provide legal certainty for foreign and domestic investment without overriding regional autonomy and Adat customs.

## Scope & Implementation

### Scope

Applies across all civil and commercial obligations in the Republic of Indonesia, encompassing general contracts, international trade, standard-form contracts, government procurement, and e-commerce.

### Key Provisions

- **Definition of Obligation & Contract:** Obligations stem from agreement or legislation (Art. 1 CC / Draft Art. 1); contract defined as an act creating obligations (Art. 1313 CC / Draft Art. 106).
- **Freedom of Contract & Open System:** All legally concluded agreements operate as law for the parties (Art. 1338 CC / Draft Art. 117), subject to mandatory public policy, morals, and good faith.
- **UNIDROIT Harmonization:** Incorporation of doctrines on Gross Disparity (UPICCs Art. 3.10), Hardship (UPICCs Art. 6.2.1–6.2.3), Force Majeure (UPICCs Art. 7.1.7), and *Contra Proferentem* (UPICCs Art. 4.6).
- **Torts & Undue Payment:** Torts (*perbuatan melawan hukum*, Art. 1365 CC / Draft Art. 131) and Undue Payment (*pembayaran tidak wajib*, Art. 1359 CC / Draft Art. 125).

### Implementation

- **Academic Drafting:** Formulated by a BPHN research team chaired by Prof. Dr. C.F.G. Sunaryati Hartono, SH, with drafting chaired by Setiawan, SH.
- **Legislative Pipeline:** Submission from BPHN to the Directorate General for Legislation, integration into the Parliamentary Legislative Program (*Prolegnas*), DPR debate, and Presidential enactment.
- **Judicial Integration:** Supreme Court application of good faith to currency fluctuation risks (*S.T. Silalahi* case 1988) and undue influence / *misbruik van omstandigheden* (*Luhur Sundoro* case 1985).

## Reform

- Parliamentary passage of the Bill on the Law of Obligations.
- Statutory enactment of specialized acts for standard contracts, e-commerce, and government procurement.
- Institutionalization of regional conflict-of-law rules (*Hukum Antar Adat / Hukum Antar Daerah*) under regional autonomy.
