# JPW-WORK-JIPLP-ED-2006-JPL178

**"The spy who came in from the cold"** is a legal editorial written by [Jeremy Phillips](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Jeremy_Phillips), editor of the *[Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Journal_of_Intellectual_Property_Law_%26_Practice)*, published by [Oxford University Press](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Oxford_University_Press) in Vol. 1, No. 13, p. 813 (Advance Access, 27 October 2006), DOI: 10.1093/jiplp/jpl178.

## Summary

The editorial examines the long-running dispute over the royalties earned by [George Blake](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/George_Blake) — a former officer of the [Secret Intelligence Service](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Secret_Intelligence_Service) who spied for the Soviet Union — from his 1990 autobiography *No Other Choice*. It uses the litigation to make a procedural point rather than a substantive one: the English proceedings took roughly nine years and two months to reach a final result, and the [European Court of Human Rights](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights) subsequently held that this breached Blake's right under Article 6(1) of the [European Convention on Human Rights](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights) to a hearing within a reasonable time. Phillips draws practical lessons for intellectual property litigants from that outcome.

## Background

Blake was convicted in 1961 and sentenced to 42 years' imprisonment; he escaped in 1966 and settled in Moscow. His autobiography *No Other Choice* was published in 1990. Ordinary copyright law gave the Crown no proprietary claim to the book: it was not written in the course of Blake's official duties, and by the time of publication the information it contained was no longer confidential. The Attorney General therefore brought civil proceedings on the Crown's behalf to strip Blake of the royalties by another route.

## Course of the Litigation

- ***Attorney General v Blake*** — the claim failed at first instance in the Chancery

` Division, and the Court of Appeal dismissed the public-law claim but raised the possibility`  
` of a restitutionary remedy. The House of Lords ([2000] UKHL 45, [2001] 1 AC 268) allowed the`  
` Crown's appeal and ordered an account of profits for breach of contract.`  
` `*`ID:`*``  `JPW-CASE-AG-V-BLAKE-2000` —  ``**`CORRECTED`**` (the source described a uniform run of`  
` wins for the Attorney General at every level; the procedural history was not uniform)`

- **Duration** — approximately nine years and two months from the issue of proceedings to

` the final domestic judgment.`

- ***Blake v United Kingdom*** (App. No. 68890/01, judgment of 26 September 2006). The

` Strasbourg court found a violation of Article 6(1) on length of proceedings and awarded`  
` €5,000 in respect of non-pecuniary damage. `*`ID:`*``  `JPW-CASE-BLAKE-V-UK-2006` ``

## Reasonable-Time Criteria

In assessing whether the length of proceedings is unreasonable, the Strasbourg court applies the circumstances of the case by reference to established criteria: the complexity of the case; the conduct of the applicant and of the relevant authorities; and what was at stake for the applicant in the dispute. *(The source rendered the second and third limbs loosely as "scale of the issue" and "conduct of the parties"; the standard formulation is given here.)*

## Lessons for Litigants

The editorial's practical suggestions include simplifying legal argument, favouring jurisdictions with a single tier of appeal, and considering arbitration or mediation — forums which do not attract the Article 6(1) length guarantee in the same way as public litigation.

## Policy Issues

- **Judicial creativity** — the reasoning by which the House of Lords reached an account of

` profits for breach of contract was novel and has been read as an exceptional, fact-specific`  
` extension rather than a general rule.`

- **Substantive against procedural justice** — tension between preventing a wrongdoer from

` profiting from his wrongdoing and the individual's procedural right to timely adjudication.`

- **Remedial gap** — where copyright confers no Crown proprietary interest, the state is

` driven to construct a remedy from private law, with the uncertainty and delay that follows.`

## Interpretation and Inference

- **Interpretation** — the Strasbourg award gave Blake a measure of personal vindication,

` although €5,000 is a small fraction of the royalties he lost.`

- **Inference** — protracted public litigation carries its own human-rights exposure, which

` strengthens the case for arbitration or mediation where the parties can choose them. This is`  
` the editorial's own suggestion drawn from the facts, not a holding of any court.`
