# Judicial-policy-greek-ejustice-2024

# **'Quality of Court Services and ICT: The Case of Greek Justice' (Chatziioannou & Giannakourou, 2024)**

### **Wiki Entity Page**

**The Greek Judicial ICT & Service Quality Reform Framework** is a judicial administration policy in the Hellenic Republic focusing on the integration of Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) to automate court procedures, reduce disposition time, and improve legal service quality based on European Union indicators \[1, 2, 11, 18, 33–35\].

## Summary

The Greek judiciary faces case backlog issues and trial durations exceeding the EU average. Through participation in the e-CODEX system and the operation of the OSDDY/PP system, the Greek Government aims to transition from manual, paper-based procedures to digital administration. Qualitative evaluations based on the SERVQUAL model indicate that reliability is the most crucial dimension expected by court service users.

## Legal Basis

- **Article 26 of the Greek Constitution**: Governs the separation of powers and the exercise of judicial power by regular courts staffed by independent judges.
- **EU e-CODEX Framework**: The foundation for Greece's participation in the electronic submission of European Payment Orders (EPO).
- **CEPEJ Evaluation Guidelines & EU Justice Scoreboard**: Standard periodic assessment of judicial efficiency, independence, and digitalization levels \[18–20\].

## Background

The Greek judicial system traditionally operates under a slow bureaucratic, paper-based scheme. According to World Bank data, the average disposition time for civil cases reaches 1,580 days (~4 years). This delay undermines legal certainty for citizens and hinders economic investment.

## Objectives

1\. Maximize efficiency and transparency in the judicial process. 2. Reduce case disposition time to meet European Union standards. 3. Eliminate reliance on paper-based filing systems. 4. Improve court staff's e-skills and public transparency.

## Scope

This policy applies to all Greek judicial bodies, divided into three main pillars \[40–45\]:

- **Civil and Criminal Courts**: Supreme Court (*Areios Pagos*), 19 Courts of Appeal, 63 Courts of First Instance, 154 Magistrate Courts, and 41 Police Courts.
- **Administrative Courts**: Council of State, 9 Courts of Appeal, and 30 Administrative Courts of First Instance.
- **Court of Audit**: State financial and pension oversight.

## Key Provisions

- **Digitalization of Caseflow**: Implementation of e-filing, digital document exchange, and automated case registration through the OSDDY/PP system.
- **e-CODEX Interoperability**: Integration of the national judicial system with the EU e-Justice network.
- **SERVQUAL Standards**: Measurement of court service quality based on 5 dimensions: Tangibles, Reliability, Responsiveness, Assurance, and Empathy.

## Implementation

- **Ioannina Empirical Study (2024)**: An ICT evaluation in the Prefecture of Ioannina found an average service quality score from lawyers of 2.89/5 and from court staff of 3.81/5.
- **ICT Adoption Rate**: Both lawyers and court staff gave low ratings to the availability and adoption of digital tools (averaging 2.16 and 2.14, respectively).

## Policy Problems

- **Low ICT Index**: Greece has a General ICT Index of 3.98 (out of a max 8.23) in the CEPEJ report.
- **E-Skills Deficit**: 70.0% of lawyers assess that court staff lack adequate digital skills and knowledge.
- **Data Fragmentation**: ICT systems were built separately without inter-departmental interconnection (creating data silos).

## Reform / Change

- Prioritization of judicial investment based on the SERVQUAL hierarchy: Reliability (29-31%), Responsiveness (19-20%), Assurance (18%), Tangibles (17-18%), and Empathy (14%).
- Provision of continuous ICT training for court staff and drafting comprehensive electronic communication regulations.

## Relationships with Other Entities

- **Ministry of Justice of Greece**: Manager of ICT administration and budget policies.
- **CEPEJ & EU Commission**: Performance evaluators and providers of judicial scoreboard frameworks \[18–20\].
