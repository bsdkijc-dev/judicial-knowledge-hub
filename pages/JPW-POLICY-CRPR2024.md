## Policy Analysis

### Background & Context

Over the last two decades, climate-induced flooding, sea-level rise, and coastal erosion have forced communities across the United States to plan relocations and resettlements. Federal disaster management relies predominantly on post-disaster property acquisition programs—commonly known as voluntary buyouts—which treat relocation as a market-rate transaction between a selling homeowner and a purchasing government entity.

### Core Problems & Policy Gaps

Conventional buyout programs reduce complex social, cultural, and ecological relationships into individualized property transactions. Market-rate acquisitions externalize historical patterns of redlining and infrastructure disinvestment into depressed home valuations, leaving low-income property owners with insufficient funds to purchase replacement housing outside floodplains. Furthermore, renters, mobile home park residents, and informal landholders (precarious possessors) are routinely excluded from buyout compensation and relocation planning.

Federal law suffers from a core misalignment between individual property rights and collective community survival. Current statutory frameworks—such as the Stafford Act and the Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act of 1970 (URA)—fail to provide dedicated mechanisms or funding streams for whole-community relocation. Additionally, administrative regulations rely on rigid cost-benefit analysis (BCA) ratios that prioritize high-value real estate over human risk reduction and rural community viability.

### Legal Basis & Statutory Baseline

- **[Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Fifth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) (Takings Clause):** Mandates that private property shall not be taken for public use without "just compensation".
- **[Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Stafford_Act) (42 U.S.C. § 5121 et seq.):** Regulates federal disaster relief and hazard mitigation grants, granting administrative immunity to agencies under § 5148.
- **[Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act of 1970](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Uniform_Relocation_Act) (42 U.S.C. § 4601 et seq.):** Establishes mandatory moving assistance and replacement housing payment floors for displaced persons.
- **[Housing and Community Development Act of 1974](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Housing_and_Community_Development_Act_of_1974):** Authorizes HUD Community Development Block Grants for Disaster Recovery (CDBG-DR) and Mitigation (CDBG-MIT).
- **[General Allotment Act of 1887](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/General_Allotment_Act_of_1887) (Dawes Act) & [Indian Removal Act of 1830](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Indian_Removal_Act_of_1830):** Historical statutory precedents that weaponized property individuation and voluntary land exchange formulas to dispossess Indigenous nations.

### Conceptual Typology & Key Provisions

Legal scholarship identifies four structural contradictions embedded within the U.S. property regime during climate resettlement:

| Dimension ! Contradiction Focus ! Statutory / Legal Impact |

| **1. Ethic of Land Rights vs. Expropriation**              |
| **2. Individual Rights vs. Communal Action**               |
| **3. Development Rights vs. Protection**                   |

### Implementation & Policy Cases

Managed by local municipal or county pass-through governments using federal grant funds (FEMA HMGP, BRIC; HUD CDBG-DR). Acquisitions require appraisals, voluntary owner consent, demolition, and deed restrictions converting acquired land into perpetual open space.

- **Isle de Jean Charles, Louisiana:** State real estate frameworks converted a Tribal community-led relocation into individual property transfers, restricting collective governance.
- **Kinston, North Carolina:** Post-Hurricane Floyd buyouts led to the fragmentation and displacement of the historically African American neighborhood of Lincoln City.
- **Alaska Native Villages (Newtok, Akiak, Shishmaref):** Appraisal formulas yield market valuations (\$85,000–\$240,000) far below actual Arctic replacement home construction costs (\$475,000–\$600,000).

### Reforms & Unresolved Issues

Legal scholarship recommends 14 structural reforms:

1.  Eliminating local matching fund requirements for impoverished hazard mitigation grant applicants.
2.  Utilizing Community Land Trusts (CLTs) for collective land stewardship.
3.  Monitoring inclusionary zoning mechanisms in receiving communities.
4.  Expanding legal pathways for communal land ownership in federal grants.
5.  Revising cost-benefit analysis (BCA) to incorporate non-market social and cultural values.
6.  Eliminating inequities in federal hazard mitigation subsidy allocations.
7.  Subsidizing flood insurance rates for low-income coastal residents.
8.  Expanding affordable public housing inventory outside floodplains.
9.  Protecting renters and precarious possessors under expanded URA rules.
10. Adopting "replacement value" instead of "fair market value" for buyouts.
11. Applying reparative justice principles to historically neglected neighborhoods.
12. Conducting long-term post-relocation monitoring and social impacts assessments.
13. Establishing community-based advocacy groups with formal representational authority.
14. Directly funding Indigenous self-determined climate adaptation and land management.

Unresolved legal issues include balancing municipal tax-base preservation with development restrictions, resolving state agency "maximum feasible deference" discretion, and clarifying government "passive takings" liability for failing to protect coastal property from climate risks.

What primary federal statutes and constitutional provisions govern flood buyouts and relocation in the United States?  
The Fifth Amendment Takings Clause; the Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act (42 U.S.C. § 5121); the Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act of 1970 (42 U.S.C. § 4601); and the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974.

Which federal and state agencies oversee climate adaptation, hazard mitigation grants, and disaster recovery?  
The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA); the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD); the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE); and state pass-through agencies such as the Louisiana Office of Community Development (OCD).

<!-- -->

Who are the lead authors of the 2024 source study on property law contradictions and climate resettlement?  
Alessandra Jerolleman, Elizabeth Marino, and Nathan Jessee (first authors), alongside Liz Koslov and Chantel Comardelle (second authors).

<!-- -->

How do current federal buyout policies impact renters and other "precarious possessors"?  
Renters and non-title holders are largely excluded from buyout negotiations and property compensation. Temporary assistance under the URA is capped (\$7,200 max for rental assistance), often pushing renters into unaffordable or high-risk housing.

<!-- -->

What are the four core contradictions of U.S. property law identified in climate resettlement processes?  
\(1\) Ethic of right to land vs. settler state expropriation; (2) Privileging individual property rights vs. discouraging communal action; (3) Privileging economic development rights over collective protective measures; and (4) Law as a historic tool of violent displacement vs. a mechanism for harm reduction and rights restoration.

<!-- -->

Why does "fair market value" fail as a measure of "just compensation" in rural Alaska Native village relocations?  
Rural Alaska lacks a standard real estate market, resulting in low appraised market values (\$85,000–\$240,000) that fall drastically short of the cost required to construct new Arctic replacement homes (\$475,000–\$600,000).

<!-- -->

How did the state of Louisiana's real estate framework conflict with the Jean Charles Choctaw Nation's resettlement vision?  
The Tribe envisioned resettlement as collective stewardship via a Community Land Trust; the state re-framed the project as individual real estate transactions, restricting Tribal ownership of common areas and subjecting unclaimed lots to public auction.

<!-- -->

How could Community Land Trusts (CLTs) and the Scottish "Community Right-to-Buy" model transform U.S. climate relocation?  
CLTs separate land ownership from structure ownership using ground leases to maintain housing affordability and collective land stewardship. The Scottish model permits community bodies to force the sale of neglected or environmentally vulnerable land for sustainable community development.

<!-- -->

What evidence shows that federal hazard mitigation grants disproportionately benefit wealthy, white communities?  
Empirical studies indicate higher home values correlate with protective shoreline infrastructure, while lower-income communities of color are targeted for buyouts. In 2022, over half of U.S. states allocated the majority of FEMA home elevation grants to affluent white neighborhoods due to local matching fund requirements.
