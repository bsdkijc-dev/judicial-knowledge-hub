# Judicial-policy-macedonia-administrative-justice-2006

# 'Efficiency of the Judicial System in Protecting Citizens against Administrative Judicial Acts: The Case of Macedonia' (Pelivanova & Dimeski, 2011/2013)

### Wiki Entity Page

**Macedonian Administrative Judicial System & Reforms** is a specialized judicial reform framework in the Republic of Macedonia established under the Law on Administrative Disputes (2006) and its 2010 amendments. This reform transitioned the administrative oversight system from an "Anglo-Saxon Model" (oversight by the Administrative Chamber of the Supreme Court) to a "European-Continental Model" based on specialized dual-instance courts (the *Administrative Court* which commenced operations on December 5, 2007, and the *Higher Administrative Court* established in 2010/2011). The reform aimed to resolve systematic delays, severe case backlogs, and the erosion of public confidence in the legal protection of citizens.

## Summary

Based on the empirical study by Dr. Natasa Pelivanova and Dr. Branko Dimeski of St. Kliment Ohridski University (Bitola), the transition from the Supreme Court to a specialized Administrative Court has not successfully resolved the backlog problem. Although the number of judges was increased from 22 in 2008 to 25 in 2009, the number of unresolved cases actually increased from **9,154 cases (2008)** to **10,340 cases (2009)**. The primary causes of these delays include spatial and IT technology constraints, a crisis in judicial assistance (only 18 assistants for 25 judges in 2009), and the lack of practical experience in administrative law among the appointed judges.

## Legal Basis

- **Constitution of the Republic of Macedonia (1991):** Article 50(2) (guaranteeing judicial review of administrative acts), Amendment 15 (independent judicial power), Amendment 21 (right to appeal), and Amendment 101 (status of the Supreme Court).
- **Law on Courts (Official Gazette No. 58/06, 35/08, 150/10):** Articles 22 and 34 defining the court hierarchy and the jurisdiction of the Administrative Court.
- **Law on Administrative Disputes (2006 & Amending Act 2010):** The foundational basis establishing the Administrative Court and the Higher Administrative Court.
- **Constitutional Court Decision U.No. 231/2008-0-1 (2009):** Mandated the availability of two-instance judicial protection in administrative disputes.

## Organization & Six Specialized Departments

The Administrative Court of Macedonia organizes its caseload into 6 specialized departments and 8 chambers/councils: 1. **Department I:** Real Estate, Cadastre, and Education. 2. **Department II:** Denationalization, Expropriation, and Transformation. 3. **Department III:** Urbanism/Construction, Water, Agriculture, Economy, Transport, and Broadcasting. 4. **Department IV:** Pensions, Disability Insurance, Social Welfare, Health Insurance, and Labor Rights. 5. **Department V:** Public Procurement, Unemployment Rights, Taxes, Competition, and Public Information Access. 6. **Department VI:** Customs, Levies, Passports/Travel, Weapons, Energy, Concessions, and Intellectual Property.

## Key Problems & Causes of Delay

1\. **Escalating Case Backlogs:** Unresolved cases rose from 9,154 in 2008 to 10,340 in 2009. 2. **Shortage of Judicial Assistants:** In 2008, the court had **0 judicial assistants**, and in 2009, there were only 18 assistants for 25 judges (less than 1 assistant per judge). 3. **Lack of Administrative Law Specialization:** Most appointed judges had backgrounds in civil, criminal, or private law without prior practical experience in administrative law. 4. **Inadequate Infrastructure & IT:** Deplorable spatial constraints and outdated technical equipment impaired court workflow.

## Comparison with the French Model

The French Model (European-Continental Model) relies on specialized education at the *École Nationale d'Administration* (ENA), career mobility between courts and the bureaucracy, and consultative functions that accelerate case resolution. Pelivanova and Dimeski recommend continuous training, budget increases, hiring more judicial assistants, and reducing political intervention in judicial appointments.
