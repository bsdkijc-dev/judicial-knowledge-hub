# INTL-MARITIME-LAW-ENFORCEMENT-INDONESIA

# International Maritime Law Enforcement: A Strategic Transferability Assessment for the Indonesian Judiciary

## 1. Introduction to the Benchmark Transferability Score (BTS) Framework

As the Indonesian judiciary maneuvers toward the Indonesia Judiciary Future Blueprint 2035–2045, the implementation of the "Benchmark Transferability Score" (BTS) is a strategic necessity for institutional evolution. This framework acknowledges a critical caution: a high international reputation for a legal regime does not equate to automatic domestic applicability. Indonesia’s unique geography and legal tradition require a rigorous filter to ensure that imported "best practices" do not destabilize the existing sovereign architecture.

The BTS evaluates reform viability across eight distinct dimensions:

- **Legal:** Compatibility with existing Indonesian statutes, the Penal Code (KUHP), and constitutional mandates.
- **Institutional:** The degree of alignment with current agency command hierarchies and the organizational structures of BAKAMLA, KKP, and TNI-AL.
- **Technology:** Requirements for specialized digital infrastructure, including satellite surveillance and forensic tools for maritime data.
- **Workforce:** The specialized skills and training levels required for both enforcement personnel and judicial personnel, specifically judges and prosecutors.
- **Financial:** An assessment of long-term budgetary commitments and the cost-benefit analysis of maritime trial and transport logistics.
- **Scalability:** The ease with which pilot enforcement or judicial models can be expanded across the diverse archipelagic geography.
- **Rights:** Alignment with human rights protections, including the principle of non-refoulement and international fair trial standards.
- **Justice Impact:** The measurable potential to enhance deterrence, close jurisdictional vacuums, and ensure successful criminal convictions.

A strategic evaluation posits that a high international reputation does not automatically imply high Indonesian transferability; consequently, reform must be pursued with archipelagic sensitivity. Before applying this scoring mechanism, we must first establish the baseline of the multilateral treaties that govern the global maritime domain.

## 2. Analysis of the Multilateral Legal Landscape

The international maritime legal framework is a "complex mosaic" of conventions and suppression treaties that establish the spatial zoning and jurisdictional authority required to maintain order at sea. For a leading archipelagic state, navigating this mosaic is essential for asserting sovereign authority over vast maritime reaches.

### The UNCLOS-SUA Nexus

The primary tension in maritime enforcement exists between the spatial "zonal" logic of the 1982 UNCLOS and the crime-specific suppression logic of the SUA Framework.

| Feature                  | 1982 UNCLOS                                                 | 1988/2005 SUA Framework                                                      |
|--------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Primary Objectives**   | Codifies spatial oceans regime and zonal authority.         | Suppresses acts endangering ship safety and WMD transport.                   |
| **Jurisdictional Logic** | Zonal: Authority depends on the vessel's maritime location. | Non-Zonal: Conduct-based; applies if navigation exceeds single state waters. |
| **Key Restrictions**     | Requires "two-ship" scenario and "private ends" motive.     | Eliminates "two-ship" and "private ends" requirements.                       |
| **Boarding Rights**      | Flag state exclusivity on high seas (Art. 92).              | Explicit flag-state consent procedures (Art. 8bis).                          |

### Sector-Specific Regimes

To address specialized enforcement bottlenecks, several supplementary regimes provide critical authorities:

- **SOLAS/ISPS:** Addresses port-state control and security through administrative certifications and risk management.
- **MARPOL:** Tackles environmental harm through a dual enforcement structure between coastal states in the EEZ and flag states.
- **UNTOC (Palermo Convention):** Facilitates inter-state cooperation and mutual legal assistance to combat human trafficking and smuggling.
- **1995 UN Fish Stocks Agreement (UNFSA):** Resolves high-seas enforcement bottlenecks by permitting non-flag state boarding of vessels to verify conservation compliance.

### Jurisdictional Vacuums: The Archipelagic "Friction Point"

The restrictive "two-ship" and "private ends" criteria of UNCLOS leave significant jurisdictional vacuums regarding political violence and single-ship mutinies. For Indonesia, this means the UNCLOS definition of piracy is functionally useless for domestic enforcement within the "Great Indonesian Seas." Because Indonesia’s internal and archipelagic waters are subject to full sovereignty, international piracy law does not apply, placing the total burden of suppression for "armed robbery at sea" on the domestic framework. This gap necessitates a transition from theoretical treaty mechanics to the specific spatial realities of maritime zones.

## 3. Mechanics of Jurisdictional Allocation and Enforcement

Strategic maritime enforcement is governed by spatial zoning, where coastal state authority is inversely proportional to the vessel’s distance from the baseline.

### Zonal Sovereignty Matrix

| Maritime Zone       | Outer Geographical Limit | Scope of Legal Authority                            | Law Enforcement Powers                                                          |
|---------------------|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Internal Waters** | Landward of baseline     | Full territorial sovereignty.                       | Unrestricted police and criminal jurisdiction.                                  |
| **Territorial Sea** | 12 nautical miles        | Sovereignty qualified by Right of Innocent Passage. | Enforcement for non-innocent passage (espionage, smuggling).                    |
| **Contiguous Zone** | 24 nautical miles        | Limited enforcement (Art. 33).                      | Prevent/punish customs, fiscal, immigration, and sanitary violations.           |
| **EEZ**             | 200 nautical miles       | Sovereign rights over resources and environment.    | Boarding, search, and seizure for fisheries/environmental violations (Art. 73). |
| **High Seas**       | Beyond 200 nm            | Exclusive flag-state jurisdiction (Art. 92).        | Universal jurisdiction for piracy (Art. 105); Right of Visit; Hot Pursuit.      |

### Tactical Interdiction Protocols

To maintain the legality of an interdiction and satisfy international standards of proportionality, domestic forces must adhere to the criteria for Hot Pursuit (UNCLOS Art. 111) and the Right of Visit (Art. 110):

- **Commencement:** Pursuit must begin within the state’s internal waters, territorial sea, or EEZ.
- **Signaling:** A visual or auditory stop signal must be given within range of the suspect vessel.
- **Continuity:** The pursuit must be continuous and uninterrupted.
- **Termination:** The right ends once the vessel enters the territorial sea of its own or a third state.

### Strategic Interdiction of Stateless Vessels

Stateless vessels represent the "low-hanging fruit" of maritime security. Because they enjoy no flag-state protection under Art. 110, any sovereign state may board, search, and assert jurisdiction over them on the high seas without the need for diplomatic consent. For Indonesia, these vessels provide a unique opportunity to exercise maritime sovereignty without the risk of diplomatic friction. These mechanics form the basis of the domestic profiles seen in global leaders.

## 4. Comparative Benchmarking of Domestic Jurisdictional Profiles

Comparative benchmarking is essential for identifying models that can be adapted to bridge Indonesia's current operational gaps.

### Global Case Studies

- **Australia:** Operates a consolidated statutory model under the Maritime Powers Act 2013. It provides standardized coercive powers to maritime officers, as affirmed in CPCF v Minister, which upheld domestic statutory detention powers over non-incorporated international obligations.
- **United States:** Asserts global extraterritorial reach through the Maritime Drug Law Enforcement Act (MDLEA), allowing for high-seas prosecutions without a direct U.S. nexus, provided there is flag-state consent or the vessel is stateless.
- **Singapore:** Emphasizes high-efficiency port security via the Maritime Offences Act, focusing on rapid tracking and prosecution to maintain its status as a global transshipment hub.
- **Norway & Netherlands:** Utilize civil law frameworks for maritime crime. The Netherlands requires rigorous proces-verbaal (sworn evidentiary files), while Norway focuses on Arctic environmental protection and UNFSA-aligned fisheries enforcement.

### Indonesian Baseline and Gap Analysis

Indonesia's framework (Law No. 45/2009 and Law No. 32/2014) remains heavily focused on IUU fishing but suffers from notable "friction points":

- **Inter-agency Overlap:** Fragmentation between BAKAMLA, KKP, and TNI-AL leads to coordination delays.
- **Treaty Gaps:** Indonesia’s non-ratification of the 2005 SUA Protocol reflects a strategy of "sovereignty insulation" over "multilateral enforcement efficiency."

The strategic gap is clear: Australia’s "unified statutory powers" contrast sharply with Indonesia’s "inter-agency overlap," creating a significant deficit in operational efficiency. This serves as the foundation for the BTS transferability assessment.

## 5. Transferability Assessment: Applying the BTS Framework

The application of the BTS framework allows for an objective determination of which international practices are viable for the Indonesian Judiciary.

### Scoring the Australian Model

- **Institutional Compatibility (Score: 2/5):** While Australia's model is efficient, its transferability is currently low due to Indonesia's entrenched agency friction. Indonesia lacks a unified "Maritime Border Command," making the adoption of a single statutory power act institutionally difficult without prior structural reform.
- **Legal Compatibility (Score: 4/5):** The Indonesian legal system is capable of accommodating a consolidated maritime power statute, provided it is harmonized with archipelagic sovereignty.

### Scoring Regional Transfer Agreements

- **Financial Feasibility (Score: 3/5):** Regional trial frameworks, such as those used in the Horn of Africa, carry high logistical costs for trials and witness transport but can be offset by international capacity-building grants.
- **Expected Justice Impact (Score: 5/5):** This model has the highest potential to end the "Catch and Release" phenomenon by ensuring that suspects interdicted on the high seas are successfully prosecuted in regional courts.

### Rights and Sovereignty Analysis

Indonesia’s non-ratification of the 2005 SUA Protocol results in a low Rights and Sovereignty Compatibility score for international boarding protocols. This choice to favor "sovereignty insulation" over the SUA framework directly contributes to the domestic "Catch and Release" problem, as Indonesia lacks the treaty-based mechanisms (aut dedere aut judicare) to demand or provide prosecution for certain extraterritorial acts.

## 6. Strategic Recommendations and Systemic Reform

To strengthen its reputation as a maritime authority, the Indonesian Judiciary must harmonize domestic law with international standards to close critical jurisdictional vacuums.

### Overcoming the "Catch and Release" Phenomenon

The lack of an affirmative obligation to prosecute results in the frequent release of maritime criminals. Indonesia should:

- Establish Regional Transfer Agreements to facilitate the trial of suspects in regional hubs.
- Fully criminalize extraterritorial offenses within the Penal Code (KUHP) to provide a firm domestic basis for trials involving acts committed beyond the territorial sea.
- Formalize "prosecute or extradite" protocols within bilateral security agreements.

### Combatting Shadow Fleets

Shadow fleets exploit Flags of Convenience (FOCs) to hide beneficial ownership. Indonesia should strengthen Port State Enforcement via the PSMA. By denying port entry and market access to vessels with fraudulent or dual registries, Indonesia creates a "legal bottleneck" that bypasses the complexities of high-seas interdiction.

### Digital Evidence Standardization and the Civil Law Requirement

To satisfy the rigorous standards of the Indonesian civil law system, particularly the requirement for a proces-verbaal equivalent to those used in the Netherlands, Indonesia must standardize protocols for:

- **AIS and VMS Logs:** Authenticating positional data to prove entry for hot pursuit.
- **SAR and Satellite Imagery:** Documenting illegal ship-to-ship (STS) transfers.
- **EO/IR Video:** Providing continuous visual logs of tactical boardings to prevent claims of excessive force.
- **Forensic Navigational Data:** Extracting data from GPS and ECDIS units to ensure it meets judicial burdens of proof.

Without these standards, even the most sophisticated tactical interdiction by the TNI-AL will fail during the initial judicial hearing in Jakarta. Closing these jurisdictional vacuums is a strategic imperative. By resolving inter-agency friction and adopting standardized evidence protocols, the Indonesian Judiciary will secure the nation’s maritime domain and its position as a global leader toward 2045.
