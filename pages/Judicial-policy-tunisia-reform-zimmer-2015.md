# Judicial-policy-tunisia-reform-zimmer-2015

**The Tunisian Judicial System and Court Administration Reform Policy Framework** is a post-revolutionary judicial reform and court administration evaluation framework reviewed by Markus Zimmer (Executive Editor of the IJCA, 2015) in the *International Journal for Court Administration* (IJCA). It maps the historical evolution of Tunisian law from the pre-colonial era (tribal customary law and Hadith/Sunnah-based Sharia), the 1881 French protectorate era (the 1896 Directorate of Judicial Services, regional courts, and French-based civil/penal procedural coding), the post-independence authoritarian era of 1956 (Bourguiba and Ben Ali), to the 2010/2011 Revolution (triggered by Mohammad Bouazizi). This revolution resulted in the 2014 Constitution (Articles 113 and 114 regarding the independence of the judiciary), alongside a January 2015 field assessment documenting enormous caseloads, severe budget constraints, and Salafist-Jihadist security threats \[1–8\].

## Summary

Published by Markus Zimmer in the *International Journal for Court Administration* (IJCA), this policy framework reviews the historical trajectory and contemporary challenges of court administration in post-2011 Revolution Tunisia. From the era of the Bardo Palace (1899) and the French protectorate (1881–1956), the Tunisian judicial system was subjected to the imposition of French civil law and the dominance of a foreign language \[2–4\]. Following independence in 1956, Tunisia was ruled by the authoritarian regimes of Habib Bourguiba and Zine El Abidine Ben Ali until the outbreak of the 2010/2011 Revolution, sparked by the Mohammad Bouazizi incident in Sīdī Bū Zayd \[5–7\]. The ratification of the 2014 Constitution, specifically through Articles 113 and 114, explicitly guaranteed the independence of the judicial branch. The January 2015 field assessment confirmed that Tunisian judges operate under dire circumstances, facing enormous caseloads, minimal budgets, and severe security threats from Salafist-Jihadist groups and Libyan instability.

## Legal Basis

- **2014 Constitution of Tunisia (Articles 113 & 114):** Constitutional provisions guaranteeing the independence of the judicial branch and establishing the institutional framework for the justice system.
- **Decree Establishing the Directorate of Judicial Services (1896):** The French colonial regulation that created a judicial oversight directorate in Tunisia.
- **Tunisian Declaration of Independence (1956):** The termination of the French protectorate status and the establishment of the Republic of Tunisia under Bourguiba.
- **Not found in the available sources:** Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text \[FACT\].

## Background

Tunisian judicial history is characterized by deep legal pluralism (tribal customary law, Sharia, and French Civil Law). Following the fall of Ben Ali's authoritarian regime during the *Arab Spring*, Tunisia emerged as the only nation to successfully ratify a democratic Constitution in 2014 \[2–3, 7–8\].

## Objectives

1\. Develop an independent judicial branch in accordance with Articles 113 and 114 of the 2014 Constitution. 2. Modernize court administration to efficiently manage enormous post-revolutionary caseloads. 3. Facilitate and coordinate technical assistance programs from the UN, European Union, Council of Europe, and the US Government.

## Scope

This policy encompasses historical and operational evaluations of the judicial system across the entire territory of the Tunisian Republic.

## Key Provisions

- **Guarantees of Judicial Independence (Articles 113 & 114 of the 2014 Constitution):**
  - Legal protection for judges and the determination of an independent institutional framework for court administration.
- **Cleansing the French Colonial Legal Legacy:**
  - Transitioning from French-language courts and the 1896 colonial legal codes toward a national legal system based on democratic principles \[3–4, 8\].
- **Coordination of International Judicial Support Projects:**
  - Execution of court administration improvement projects backed by the UN, EU, Council of Europe, and the US.

## Implementation

- **Field Assessment Mission (January 2015):** Evaluation of court conditions by an expert team led by Markus Zimmer under the coordination of the IJCA.
- **Proposal of Court Management Projects:** The launch of court administration improvement project proposals by the US Government and European Union institutions.

## Policy Problems

- **Enormous Caseloads:** A severe shortage of judges and administrative staff to manage the massive surge in post-revolutionary cases.
- **Terrorism and Security Threats:** Salafist-Jihadist terror attacks in Tunis and Sousse, alongside the military crisis in neighboring Libya, disrupting judicial stability.

## Reform / Change

- Translating the operational details of Articles 113 and 114 of the 2014 Constitution into new, robust organic legislation.
- Increasing security protections and allocating emergency funding for courts.
