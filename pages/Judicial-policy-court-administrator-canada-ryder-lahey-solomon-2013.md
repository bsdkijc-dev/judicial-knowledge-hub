# Judicial-policy-court-administrator-canada-ryder-lahey-solomon-2013

**The Development and Role of the Court Administrator in Canada Policy Framework** is a judicial administration policy framework and comparative study on the evolution of the trial court administrator role in Canada and the United States. Formulated by Pamela Ryder-Lahey (Director of Court Services, Newfoundland) and Prof. Peter H. Solomon, Jr. (University of Toronto), it outlines the transition from traditional court management by judges or local clerks toward an executive partnership model between the Chief Judge and the professional Court Administrator, guided by a public service ethos, caseflow management, and core competencies based on ACCA/NACM standards.

## Summary

Published by Pamela Ryder-Lahey and Prof. Peter H. Solomon, Jr. in the *International Journal for Court Administration* (IJCA), this policy framework examines the history of court administration modernization in Canada and the United States since the 1970s. Overcoming the weaknesses of traditional systems managed by judges or elected clerks, Canada developed the profession of the court administrator, working within an executive partnership model alongside the Chief Judge. This framework emphasizes that judicial independence is not compromised by executive administration (as affirmed by the Supreme Court of Canada in *Valente v. The Queen* 1985), and highlights three core pillars: executive partnership, a user-friendly public service ethos, and integrated caseflow management.

## Legal Basis

- **Supreme Court of Canada Decision: Valente v. The Queen (1985 2 S.C.R. 273):** Establishes the constitutional standard that judicial independence only requires judicial control over direct adjudicative functions (judge assignments, trial scheduling, courtroom allocation), while operational administrative functions can be managed jointly with the executive branch.
- **Report of the Ontario Courts Inquiry (Justice Thomas Zuber, 1987):** Outlines the doctrine of shared responsibility and collaborative meetings between judges and court staff.
- **Provincial Court Act (Province of Newfoundland and Labrador):** Regulates the position of court staff as employees of the Department of Justice while simultaneously serving as Officers of the Court.
- **Not found in the available sources:** Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text.

## Background

Before 1970, courts in Canada were managed in a fragmented manner ("a fractured mosaic of individual fiefdoms") by judges lacking management training. Surging caseloads drove the adoption of professional trial court administrators trained through academic programs (such as Brock University in 1980) and guided by pioneering literature by Millar & Baar (1981).

## Objectives

1\. Build an executive partnership model between the Chief Judge and the Court Administrator. 2. Establish user-friendly courts grounded in a public service ethos. 3. Accelerate dispute resolution through caseflow management systems and compulsory mediation. 4. Elevate the professional standards of court administrators through the ACCA/NACM core competencies framework.

## Scope

This policy applies to all levels of provincial courts and superior courts in Canada, and serves as a comparative reference for the United States judicial system.

## Key Provisions

- **Executive Partnership Model:**
  - Although formally reporting to the Ministry of Justice (dual subordination), court administrators work on a daily basis in functional equality with the Chief Judge to manage case allocation, staff, and budgets.
- **Nine ACCA Core Competencies:**

1.  Purposes and Responsibilities of Courts.
2.  Leadership.
3.  Caseflow Management.
4.  Information Technology Management.
5.  Court and Community Communications.
6.  Human Resource Management.
7.  Resources, Budget and Finance.
8.  Education, Training and Development.
9.  Strategic Planning.

- **User-Friendly Services and Facilities:**
  - *Child-Friendly Facilities:* Dedicated waiting rooms equipped with closed-circuit television (CCTV) and child-sized courtrooms without high podiums.
  - *Self-Service Family Law Centre:* Free self-service assistance for unrepresented litigants at the Ottawa Courthouse.
  - *Tech-Friendly Courtrooms:* Provision of direct computer and internet network access for real-time legal precedent research.
- **Toronto Region Case Management (Superior Court of Justice):**
  - Dispute oversight by a Case Management Master, mandatory mediation within 3 months, and settlement conferences within 6–9 months prior to trial.

## Implementation

- **Newfoundland and Labrador Practice:** The Director of Court Services manages daily operations alongside the Chief Judge, while consultations with the Deputy Minister of Justice focus strictly on budget submissions.
- **Ontario Five-Year Plan (2002–2007):** Implementation of service standards that are evaluated on a periodic basis.

## Policy Problems

- **Dual Subordination:** The structural position of the court administrator caught between the directives of the Chief Judge and executive ministry regulations.
- **Civil Delay and Cost of Access:** The core challenges identified by the CBA Task Force (1995) that prompted the creation of the Canadian Forum on Civil Justice.

## Reform / Change

- Institutionalization of compulsory mediation in family and general civil cases.
- Transition of courts from rigid adjudication institutions into public service bodies focused on citizen satisfaction.

## Relationships with Other Entities

- **Association of Canadian Court Administrators (ACCA):** The professional organization developing competency standards for Canadian court administrators.
- **Department of Justice / Ministry of the Attorney General:** The executive body providing funding and provincial court administrative staff.
