# Judicial-policy-uk-supreme-court-cra-2005

# 'The UK Supreme Court – A Fine New Vintage, or Just a Smart New Label on a Dusty Old Bottle?' (Drewry, 2013)

### Wiki Entity Page

The **United Kingdom Supreme Court** (**UKSC**) is the highest court of appeal in the United Kingdom for civil matters across all UK jurisdictions (England, Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland) and for criminal matters (from England, Wales, and Northern Ireland). Established pursuant to Part 3 of the **Constitutional Reform Act 2005**, it formally assumed the appellate jurisdiction of the House of Lords on October 1, 2009. The Court operates as an independent, non-ministerial executive department housed in the Middlesex Guildhall on Parliament Square, London.

## Summary

Authored by Prof. Gavin Drewry of Royal Holloway, University of London, this case study reviews the background and structural innovation of the UKSC's establishment. The reform was fundamentally triggered by the necessity to meet the standards of objective judicial independence mandated by Article 6(1) of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) and corresponding Strasbourg Court jurisprudence (*Procola v Luxembourg*; *McGonnell v UK*), which demand a strict physical and constitutional separation between the legislature and the judiciary. Administratively, the UKSC applies New Public Management (NPM) principles, managed by a Chief Executive (Jenny Rowe) who acts as the Accounting Officer and answers directly to the Public Accounts Committee of the House of Commons.

## Legal Basis

- **Constitutional Reform Act 2005 (c. 4):** The primary statutory authorization establishing the Supreme Court, defining its jurisdiction, governance structure, independence, and rulemaking powers (Supreme Court Rules 2009).
- **Appellate Jurisdiction Act 1876:** The historical predecessor statute that created the judicial House of Lords and Lords of Appeal in Ordinary (superseded and repealed by the CRA 2005).
- **Human Rights Act 1998 & ECHR (Article 6(1)):** The statutory and treaty-based requirement for objective judicial independence and tribunal impartiality.

## Background & Policy Problems

For over a century following the Appellate Jurisdiction Act 1876, the highest court of appeal in the UK was embedded inside the non-elected second chamber of the national legislature—the House of Lords. Although judicial functions were separated from legislative business in practice (especially after the creation of the Appellate Committee in 1948), the Law Lords remained voting members of Parliament. On June 12, 2003, Prime Minister Tony Blair unexpectedly announced the creation of an independent UK Supreme Court to resolve ongoing constitutional anomalies.

### Main Anomalies Addressed

1\. **Violation of Separation of Powers:** ECHR jurisprudence dictated that housing the highest appellate judges within the legislature created a perceived conflict of interest, requiring structural separation. 2. **Government as a Litigant Party:** The UK Government is a party in over **50% of cases** coming before the top court, making it critical that court administration be free from Ministry of Justice oversight to protect judicial independence. 3. **Erosion of Historical Caseload Focus:** The Court's docket shifted dramatically from a tax-heavy focus in the 1950s (\>30% tax cases) to a public law and human rights docket (37% in 2003/04; 24% ECHR in 2009), demanding modern administrative handling.

## New Public Management (NPM) Governance Structure

- **Chief Executive & Accounting Officer:** Jenny Rowe was appointed in January 2008 to oversee the budget and operations, entirely distinct from the Courts Service agency of the Ministry of Justice.
- **Efficiency Mandate (Section 51 CRA 2005):** Statutorily mandates that court resources be managed to achieve an "efficient, effective, and economic" system.
- **Bifurcated Organizational Structure:**
  - *Corporate Resources Directorate:* Manages HR, finance, communications, and health & safety.
  - *Judicial Services:* Led by a legal Registrar, managing case filing, listing, and 7 temporary judicial assistants.
- **Staffing:** 38.4 Full-Time Equivalent (FTE) staff.

## Common Law Constitutionalism & Constitutional Statutes

Although the UK lacks a codified written constitution, Supreme Court jurisprudence has fostered common law constitutional doctrines:

- **Constitutional Statutes:** Laws LJ in *Thoburn v Sunderland City Council* defined "constitutional statutes" as laws regulating the fundamental relationship between the citizen and the state (e.g., Magna Carta, Bill of Rights 1689, HRA 1998, ECA 1972).
- **Qualification of Parliamentary Sovereignty:** In *Jackson v Attorney General*, Lord Steyn stated *obiter* that if Parliament attempted to abolish judicial review, the court might qualify parliamentary sovereignty to protect fundamental legal principles.

## Relationships with Other Entities

- **House of Lords & JCPC:** Institutional predecessor and co-located appellate court sharing judges and infrastructure.
- **Ministry of Justice (MoJ) / DCA:** Executive funding and oversight bodies, though the UKSC operates distinctly as a non-ministerial department.
