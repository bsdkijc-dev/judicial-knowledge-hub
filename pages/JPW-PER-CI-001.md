## 3. POLICY ANALYSIS

- **Background**: Traditional Public-Private Partnership (PPP) models established in the 1990s primarily governed bilateral, profit-oriented relationships between public authorities and market operators for infrastructure delivery. Modern urban challenges—including climate disruption, social inequality, and rapid technological transition—demonstrate the limitations of purely commercial PPPs, prompting a shift toward inclusive urban co-governance models.
- **Problem**: Conventional market procurement and standard PPPs frequently fail to address localized poverty, urban decay, socio-spatial polarization, and climate adaptation because they prioritize pure financial returns over public value, equity, and civic participation.
- **Legal/Policy Gap**: Public procurement frameworks (such as traditional EU tendering rules) emphasize price competition and market efficiency, creating procedural barriers that exclude non-profit community groups, civil society actors, and scientific institutions from co-designing public services or managing urban assets.
- **Objectives**: Establish legal, financial, institutional, and digital instruments that enable multi-stakeholder governance (Public-Private-Science-Social-Community Partnerships or 5Ps), operationalize the "quintessential quintuple helix" governance model, and secure equitable sharing of economic and social benefits among urban residents.
- **Scope**: Encompasses local municipal administration, urban commons regeneration, innovation procurement (e.g., Pre-Commercial Procurement and Public Procurement of Innovative Solutions), social enterprise incubation, and urban climate transition projects across European cities.
- **Implementation**: Executed through local administrative regulations (e.g., Turin's Regulation for Governing Urban Commons, Reggio Emilia's Climate Democracy Regulation); Pacts of Collaboration (\*patti di collaborazione\*); social partnerships (\*baratto amministrativo\*) under Art. 190 of Legislative Decree n. 50/2016; co-design arrangements under Art. 55 of Legislative Decree n. 117/2017; and EU-funded experimental programs such as Urban Innovative Actions (UIA).
- **Evidence**: Empirical evaluation of 13 lighthouse city case studies funded by the EU Urban Innovative Actions initiative—including Co-City Turin (Italy), B-MINCOME Barcelona (Spain), USE-IT! Birmingham (UK), FED Gothenburg (Sweden), TAST'in FIVES Lille (France), 5Bridges Nantes (France), Vienna CoRE (Austria), Curing the Limbo Athens (Greece), URBAN SOIL 4 FOOD Maribor (Slovenia), CALICO Brussels (Belgium), E-Co-Housing Budapest (Hungary), Home Silk Road Lyon (France), and Yes We Rent Mataró (Spain).
- **Impact**: Legal recognition of urban commoning; creation of district-based social enterprises and community land trusts; tax credits/exemptions for residents maintaining public spaces under horizontal subsidiarity; and establishing active civic roles in delivering public services.
- **Reform**: Enact explicit statutory recognition of USDIPs in procurement codes; establish "Urban Commons Project Finance" / Social Finance mechanisms to share risk; deploy City Science Offices (CSOs) and Urban Co-Labs as innovation procurement brokers; and codify non-competitive collaborative dialogue procedures for social and environmental co-governance.
- **Unresolved Issues**:
  - Financial self-sustainability of community-managed urban projects once initial EU or municipal seed grants expire.
  - Legal risk aversion among municipal civil servants reluctant to depart from traditional competitive tendering procedures.
  - Ensuring genuine representativeness and avoiding the instrumentalization or exploitation of vulnerable or low-income residents in uncompensated social partnership schemes.

## 5. MEDIAWIKI ARTICLE

**Urban Sustainable Development and Innovation Partnerships** (USDIPs) refers to a legal category, administrative policy framework, and multi-stakeholder co-governance instrument designed to enable collaborative delivery of urban technological, ecological, and social innovations.[^1]

### Lead

Conceived as an evolution beyond traditional bilateral [Public-Private Partnerships](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Public-Private_Partnership) (PPPs), USDIPs establish formal "quintuple helix" or Public-Private-Science-Social-Community Partnerships (5Ps).[^2] Legal analysis led by [Christian Iaione](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Christian_Iaione) of [Luiss Guido Carli University](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Luiss_Guido_Carli_University) demonstrates how municipal authorities can leverage horizontal subsidiarity under [Article 118(4) of the Italian Constitution](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Article_118%284%29_of_the_Italian_Constitution), statutory mechanisms in the [Italian Public Contracts Code](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Italian_Public_Contracts_Code) (Legislative Decree n. 50/2016), and the [Italian Third Sector Code](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Italian_Third_Sector_Code) (Legislative Decree n. 117/2017) to engage urban residents, social enterprises, and scientific institutions as equal co-governors of urban commons.[^3]

### Summary

The USDIP model shifts urban governance from a Schumpeterian market-driven framework toward mission-oriented, citizen-centric co-production.[^4] Rather than relying exclusively on competitive commercial concessions, USDIPs operationalize non-competitive, solidarity-based legal vehicles—such as Pacts of Collaboration (\*patti di collaborazione\*), administrative barter (\*baratto amministrativo\*), social partnerships, and Community Land Trusts—to manage public infrastructure and local services.[^5]

### Legal Basis

The administrative legal architecture rests upon national statutory acts and European directives:

- **[Constitution of Italy](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Italy)**: Article 118, paragraph 4, establishing the constitutional principle of horizontal subsidiarity.[^6]
- **[Legislative Decree n. 50/2016](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Legislative_Decree_n._50/2016) (Public Contracts Code)**: Article 189 (horizontal subsidiarity interventions), Article 190 (social partnerships / administrative barter), and Article 36 (direct awards below thresholds).[^7]
- **[Legislative Decree n. 117/2017](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Legislative_Decree_n._117/2017) (Third Sector Code)**: Articles 55 and 71, authorizing public-social co-design (\*co-progettazione\*) and concession of public cultural heritage to non-profit entities.[^8]
- **[Directive 2014/24/EU](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Directive_2014/24/EU)**: Recognizing innovation partnerships, social innovation, and public-public cooperation.[^9]
- **[International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Economic%2C_Social_and_Cultural_Rights)**: Article 15, establishing the human right to science and citizen participation in scientific progress.[^10]

### Background

Over three decades of empirical evidence reveal the limitations of conventional PPPs in resolving urban poverty, climate adaptation, and spatial polarization.[^11] In response to the post-2008 economic crisis and climate challenges, European municipalities (pioneered by cities such as Bologna, Turin, Barcelona, and Reggio Emilia) developed local regulations for urban commons.[^12]

### Objectives

- Transition public administration from a top-down regulator to an "Enabling State" that facilitates civic co-governance.[^13]
- Democratize innovation procurement by incorporating end-users and community groups into service design.[^14]
- Ensure an equitable distribution of social and economic benefits generated by urban assets.[^15]

### Scope

Applies to local municipal governments, public infrastructure development, urban renewal, social enterprise incubation, circular economy initiatives, and district-level energy transitions across EU Member States.[^16]

### Key Provisions & Policy Tools

The framework defines four operational tools necessary to implement USDIPs:

1.  **Regulatory Procurement Tool**: Adapting innovation procurement rules (PPI/PCP) and establishing non-competitive "collaborative dialogue" procedures via Pacts of Collaboration.[^17]
2.  **Financial Tool**: Deploying "Urban Commons Project Finance" and Social Finance instruments (e.g., Social Impact Bonds) to share operational risks.[^18]
3.  **Institutional & Learning Tool**: Establishing Collaboratories (Co-Labs), Living Labs, and City Science Offices (CSOs) to act as urban innovation brokers.[^19]
4.  **Digital Tool**: Implementing cooperatively owned platforms, e-procurement systems, and civic participation forums.[^20]

### Implementation

Operationalized through local municipal enactments, such as Turin's Regulation for Governing Urban Commons (tested in the UIA Co-City Turin project) and Reggio Emilia's Regulation on Climate Democracy and Justice.[^21] Adjudicated by the Italian Court of Auditors (\*Corte dei Conti\*), which qualified social partnerships as non-market expressions of horizontal subsidiarity exempt from competitive EU concession rules.[^22]

### Policy Problems

- **Risk Aversion**: Inertia among public officials accustomed to standard profit-oriented procurement.[^23]
- **Representativeness Deficits**: Risk that unorganized community groups may lack technical expertise or fail to represent marginalized residents.[^24]
- **Financial Sustainability**: Maintaining long-term operations once initial public grants or EU trial subsidies expire.[^25]

### Reform

Scholars advocate explicit legislative codification of USDIPs within national procurement codes, formalizing the role of Innovation Public Procurement Brokers (IPPB) and establishing dedicated Urban Commons Project Finance vehicles.[^26]
