# STRATEGIC-TRANSFERABILITY-INDONESIA-MARITIME

# Strategic Transferability Assessment: International Maritime Jurisdictional Frameworks and the Indonesian Judicial Blueprint (2035–2045)

## 1. Executive Context: The Benchmark Transferability Framework

As the Indonesian judiciary navigates the 2035–2045 reform horizon, the integration of international maritime norms must be governed by the Benchmark Transferability Score. It is a strategic fallacy to assume that international reputation or the mere ratification of treaties equates to local viability. The Indonesia Judiciary Future Blueprint demands a rigorous filter to prevent the adoption of "legal phantoms"—provisions that satisfy global diplomatic expectations but collapse under domestic institutional constraints. This report systematically evaluates global maritime legal frameworks against an 8-dimension scoring system to ensure that reform efforts catalyze actual justice rather than administrative overreach.

### The 8 Dimensions of Transferability

| Dimension                   | Definition                                                                                                   |
|-----------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Legal Compatibility**     | The degree of alignment between international conventions (UNCLOS/SUA) and existing Indonesian statutes.     |
| **Institutional Readiness** | The structural capacity of maritime agencies and courts to exercise newly adopted jurisdictional powers.     |
| **Technology Readiness**    | The requirement for advanced surveillance, AIS, and digital forensics to support maritime evidence.          |
| **Workforce Readiness**     | The depth of specialized training required for judges and prosecutors in complex international maritime law. |
| **Financial Feasibility**   | The long-term cost-benefit ratio of enforcement, adjudication, and correctional obligations.                 |
| **Scalability**             | The viability of applying a jurisdictional model across the expansive 17,000-island archipelago.             |
| **Rights Compatibility**    | Adherence to international human rights standards, specifically detention protocols and non-refoulement.     |
| **Expected Justice Impact** | The projected efficacy of the framework in suppressing crime and maintaining the rule of law.                |

We must appraise the necessity of grounding maritime enforcement in Indonesian-specific institutional readiness. Without a foundation of domestic procedural capacity, the adoption of sophisticated extraterritorial mandates will remain performative rather than transformative.

## 2. Analytical Evaluation: UNCLOS Spatial Architecture and Legal Compatibility

The strategic architecture of maritime governance is dictated by spatial partitioning under the 1982 UNCLOS. These boundaries define the limits of national sovereignty, transitioning from absolute territorial control to limited functional rights as one moves seaward. For the Indonesian judiciary, understanding these transitions is vital to determining where domestic laws have "high compatibility" versus where they encounter "jurisdictional friction."

In critiquing the Legal Compatibility dimension, we observe a sharp divide in coastal authority:

- **Sovereign Zones (Internal Waters & Territorial Sea, 0–12 nm):** Authority here is near-absolute. However, compatibility is tested by the obligation to permit "Innocent Passage." Judges must be trained to differentiate between expeditious transit and acts "prejudicial to the peace, good order, or security" of the State.
- **Functional Zones (EEZ & Contiguous Zone, 12–200 nm):** In the Contiguous Zone (up to 24 nm), Indonesia possesses specific authority to prevent infringements of its customs, fiscal, immigration, and sanitary (CFIS) laws. A critical, yet often overlooked, compatibility area is Article 303(2), which allows Indonesia to treat the unauthorized removal of archaeological and historical objects from the seabed as a breach of customs regulations. This provides a high-impact avenue for protecting national maritime heritage.

The transferability of these obligations follows a tiered evaluation:

- **High Transferability:** CFIS enforcement in the Contiguous Zone and resource-related adjudication in the EEZ (Article 56).
- **Moderate Transferability:** Managing "Innocent Passage" disputes. This requires a workforce capable of interpreting whether a vessel’s conduct "disturbs the peace of the country" under Article 27.
- **Low Transferability:** General criminal prosecution of foreign-flagged vessels in the EEZ for non-resource crimes, which remains the exclusive domain of the Flag State under Article 92.

## 3. Assessment of Enforcement Typologies: Flag, Coastal, and Port State Models

Strategic maritime reform must close "jurisdictional gaps" that allow criminal syndicates to operate in the seams between national authorities. This requires an evaluation of the four primary jurisdictional typologies.

The Institutional Compatibility of Port State Jurisdiction (Article 218) represents a high-reward frontier. It allows Indonesia to prosecute high-seas discharge violations if the vessel voluntarily enters a port. However, the Workforce Readiness burden is immense; it requires judges to master MARPOL standards and evidentiary rules for incidents occurring outside national waters.

Regarding Flag State Jurisdiction, the "Genuine Link" requirement (Article 91) is a primary friction point. Indonesian courts must recognize the precedent set by ITLOS in M/V Saiga (No. 2), which clarifies that the genuine link is intended to ensure effective Flag State duties, not to allow third States to challenge a vessel’s registration. This limits the ability of the Indonesian judiciary to question the nationality of foreign vessels unless they are demonstrably "stateless."

Stateless Vessel Interdiction (Article 110) represents a high-transferability, low-friction strategic advantage. Stateless vessels lack jurisdictional protection. Under the 2035–2045 Blueprint, Indonesia should prioritize the "universal" right to board, search, and prosecute stateless vessels encountered on the high seas or in the EEZ, as this requires no Flag State consent and offers an immediate "Expected Justice Impact."

The complexity of Concurrent Jurisdiction—where the Territorial, Nationality, and Protective principles overlap—demands that the judiciary formalize priority rules. Failing to do so results in diplomatic friction and stalled prosecutions, ultimately diminishing the deterrent effect of maritime law.

## 4. Multilateral Mechanisms: SUA Convention and Treaty-Based Transferability

The shift from UNCLOS piracy (limited by "private ends" and the "two-vessel" requirement) to the SUA Convention (1988/2005) is essential for addressing maritime terrorism and BCN (biological, chemical, nuclear) weapon transport. SUA operates on the principle of Aut Dedere Aut Judicare (extradite or prosecute), ensuring offenders find no safe haven.

However, implementation must be balanced against Rights Compatibility. Precedents such as Hirsi Jamaa v. Italy and Medvedyev v. France are non-negotiable benchmarks. These cases establish that:

- Physical detention at sea must have a clear legal basis.
- Suspects must be brought "promptly" before a judicial authority.
- The principle of non-refoulement prohibits returning suspects to faces of persecution.

The Financial Feasibility of long-term detention for maritime criminals is a major constraint. To address this, Indonesia should evaluate the UNODC Global Maritime Crime Programme (GMCP) model. This model facilitates the transfer of sentenced persons to their home regions for incarceration. For the Indonesian archipelago, scalability depends on concluding bilateral prisoner transfer treaties that alleviate the burden on domestic correctional infrastructure while maintaining international human rights standards.

## 6. Strategic Conclusions and Implementation Logic

The 2035–2045 reform cycle must acknowledge that Technology Readiness is the ultimate gatekeeper for advanced jurisdictional models. The admissibility of digital navigation data, vessel logbooks, and radar records as primary evidence is a prerequisite for successful Port State (Art 218) and SUA prosecutions. Without specialized maritime forensics, the judiciary will remain unable to convert high-seas interdictions into domestic convictions.

Furthermore, "Shiprider" agreements, while high-priority for IUU fishing and drug trafficking, face low-compatibility due to the complexity of embarked-officer protocols and the legal challenges of immediate pursuit procedures across maritime boundaries.

To enhance the Benchmark Transferability Score, the Indonesian Judiciary must execute three Strategic Directives:

- **Codify Digital Maritime Forensics:** Update procedural codes to ensure that AIS data, radar records, and satellite imagery are admissible as objective evidence for environmental and jurisdictional violations.
- **Operationalize Statelessness Protocols:** Develop a standardized judicial workflow for the immediate exercise of domestic jurisdiction over vessels lacking valid registration or using multiple flags of convenience.
- **Establish "Sea-Detention" Judicial Oversight:** Create specialized maritime chambers capable of conducting prompt remote hearings for suspects interdicted at sea, satisfying the Medvedyev promptness requirement.

Adoption of international maritime law must be a Principle-Based Adoption. Indonesia should not seek to mirror global standards for the sake of reputation alone. Instead, it must prioritize frameworks that align with its spatial realities and institutional capacity, ensuring that every legislative change translates into a more secure and just maritime domain.
