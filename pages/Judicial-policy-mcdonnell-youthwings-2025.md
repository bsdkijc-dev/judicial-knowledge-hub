# Judicial-policy-mcdonnell-youthwings-2025

**Young radicals, moderates and aligned: Ideological congruence and incongruence in party youth wings** is a comparative empirical research paper authored by Duncan McDonnell (Griffith University) together with an international research team, published in the *European Journal of Political Research* (EJPR) in 2025 \[1–2\].

## Summary

This study examines the ideological relationship between party youth wing members and senior party leaderships. Analyzing survey data from the YOUMEM project covering 4,679 members across 12 party youth wings in six Western countries (Australia, Austria, Germany, Italy, Spain, and Sweden), the study proposes a tripartite typology: \*radicals\* (members who place themselves more extreme than the party), \*moderates\* (members more moderate than the party), and \*aligned\* (members who match the party). The findings show that radicals make up a majority of members (58.3%), but their proportion is far higher in centre-left youth wings than in centre-right ones.

## Background

Since the classic literature of Ostrogorski, Michels, and Duverger through to May (1973), grassroots members — particularly youth wing members — have been regarded as the most ideologically pure and radical group compared to pragmatic party elites. However, previous research had not distinguished the \*direction\* of incongruence, nor the socialization factors that shape it.

## Objectives

1\. Propose a new typology of ideological congruence (\*radicals\*, \*moderates\*, \*aligned\*). 2. Test the effect of membership length on radicalism (Hypothesis 1a/1b). 3. Test the impact of political career ambitions (office-seeking) on ideological alignment (Hypothesis 2) \[20–21\]. 4. Analyze ideological differences between centre-left and centre-right youth wings (Hypothesis 3) \[21–22\]. 5. Measure the effect of a senior party's government vs. opposition status on member perceptions (Hypothesis 4) \[22–25\].

## Scope

The research covers 12 youth wings of major parties in 6 countries: Australia (AYL, YL), Austria (SJÖ, JVP), Germany (Jusos, JU), Italy (GD, FIG), Spain (JSE, NNGG), and Sweden (SSU, MUF) \[25, 29–33\].

## Key Provisions

- **Congruence Typology:** Calculated from the mathematical difference between respondents' self-placement and their placement of the senior party on a 1–10 left-right scale.
- **Group Proportions:** \*Radicals\* make up 58.3%, \*Aligned\* 25.3%, and \*Moderates\* 16.5%.
- **Centre-Left Radical Dominance (Hypothesis 3 Supported):** Centre-left youth members have a much higher probability of being radical (~75%) compared to centre-right (\<50%) (p \< 0.001) \[50–52, 57\]. Austria's SJÖ has the highest proportion of radicals (90.5%).
- **Role of Political Ambition (Hypothesis 2 Partially Supported):** Members who aspire to stand for office are more likely to be aligned than radical \[50–52, 55\].
- **Effect of Membership Length (Hypothesis 1a Partially Supported):** The longer someone has been a member, the higher their probability of being radical rather than aligned, due to intensive socialization within peer circles \[48, 50–52, 54\].
- **Government Status (Hypothesis 4 Not Supported):** The senior party's government/opposition status did not significantly affect members' ideological alignment \[50–51, 58\].

## Implementation

The methodology was implemented through online surveys via LimeSurvey and Qualtrics (2018–2022), multinomial logistic regression estimation with country-clustered standard errors, and extensive robustness testing \[25–26, 47, 50–52, 65–69\]. Data is publicly deposited on Harvard Dataverse.

## Policy Problems

1\. Internal tension between radical youth members and party leaders pursuing median voters. 2. The risk of member dissatisfaction leading to inactivity or exit from the party. 3. Obstacles to recruiting electoral candidates who align with the official party platform.

## Reform / Change

Rejects the thesis that grassroots members have become passive or homogeneous, demonstrating instead that internal ideological dynamics remain characterized by friction between radical youth and pragmatic elites \[72–73\].
