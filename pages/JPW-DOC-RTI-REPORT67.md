## Policy Analysis

### Background & Objectives

- Effective public safety policy requires an accurate understanding of the actual crime situation, specifically measuring crime unrecorded by police (the dark figure of crime).
- Primary objectives of the National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS):

1.  Estimate types and volume of unreported crime.
2.  Obtain detailed victim profiles and victimization conditions.
3.  Collect crime trend data through fixed-point monitoring.
4.  Clarify public perceptions of safety.
5.  Provide victimization data to relevant institutions and the public.

### Methodology & Changes in the 6th Survey (2024)

- **Sampling**: Selected 6,916 men and women aged 16+ via two-stage stratified random sampling (3,458 each; adjusted from 7,000 due to excluding Ishikawa Prefecture following the 2024 Noto Peninsula Earthquake).
- **Fieldwork**: In-person home interviews conducted from January 19 to February 29, 2024, with online response options.
- **Sensitive Categories**: Self-administered questionnaires used for stalking, domestic violence (DV), child abuse (age \<=17 by guardians), and sexual incidents.
- **Valid Responses**: 4,179 individuals (60.4%) for interviews; 4,103 individuals (59.3%) for self-administered questionnaires.
- **Questionnaire Revisions**: Removed indicators for car theft, attempted burglary, and internet auction fraud. Added indicators for road rage, online defamation/slander/privacy leaks, and the impact of COVID-19 on sensitive cases.

### Key Findings & Risk Factors

- **Overall Rate**: Total crime victimization (household and personal, excluding child abuse) reached 24.9% over the past 5 years and 11.5% during 2023.
- **Trend Analysis**: The 5-year rate slightly increased in the 6th survey (24.9%) compared to the 5th survey (2019: 23.8%), despite a long-term decline since the 2nd survey (2004).
- **Logistic Regression Risk Factors**: Victimization was significantly higher among residents in cities with populations \>=100,000, detached houses, households with 2+ members, individuals aged \<=59, workers, and those with security equipment.
- **Category Specifics**:
  - *Household*: Road rage was highest (16.5%), followed by bicycle theft and car vandalism.
  - *Personal*: Child abuse reached 2.9% up to age 18. Stalking, DV, child abuse, and sexual incidents were significantly higher among females. Personal victimization occurred \>70% at home or in local areas, except stalking and sexual incidents which occurred most at workplaces.
  - *Fraud / Identity Theft*: Unauthorized use of credit/debit/e-payment cards was highest (6.5%). Attempted special fraud was higher among males aged 40–59 and workers, whereas actual special fraud was higher among males aged 65+.

### Dark Figure of Crime & Reporting Patterns

- Motorcycle theft had the highest police reporting rate among household crimes, while road rage had the lowest (2.2%).
- For all personal victimization categories (excluding child abuse), non-reported cases exceeded reported cases. For child abuse, \>60% of respondents "did not tell anyone".
- **Reporting Drivers**: Primary reasons to report were preventing further crime, punishing perpetrators, and recovering property/damages.
- **Non-Reporting Drivers**: Primary reasons not to report were petty/unsuccessful crime or minimal/no loss. For stalking, DV, and robbery, victims often "handled it themselves (knew the offender)". For child abuse, victims "did not know what to do" or "thought no one would act".

### Public Safety Perceptions & Sentencing Views

- Reversing previous trends where safety perceptions steadily improved, the 6th survey showed a decrease in respondents viewing Japan as safe and an increase in those viewing it as unsafe.
- Respondents with higher fear of crime, those viewing Japan as unsafe, and those with personal victimization experiences favored prison sentences over monetary fines.

### Interpretations & Structural Inferences

- Demonstrates a post-pandemic shift in public safety perception, marked by heightened subjective insecurity and new crime vectors (road rage and cyber incidents).
- Extremely low reporting rates for road rage (2.2%) and child abuse (\>60% unmentioned) highlight the need for accessible digital reporting tools and community safety networks that bypass formal police procedures.
