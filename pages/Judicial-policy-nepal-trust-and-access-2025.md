# 'Judicial Trust in Nepal: Demand- and Supply-Side Perspectives' (Chapagain, 2025)

### Wiki Entity Page

**The Judicial Trust and Access to Justice Policy Framework in Nepal** (*Judicial Trust in Nepal: Demand- and Supply-Side Perspectives*) is a judicial policy framework and empirical evaluation regarding the level of public trust and confidence and access to justice in Nepal, analyzed from two perspectives: the *demand-side* (justice seekers and vulnerable groups) and the *supply-side* (judges, advocates, and judicial officials).

## Summary

Published in the *International Journal for Court Administration* (2025) by Dr. Bidhya Chapagain, this policy framework reveals the existence of a significant "trust deficit" towards the Nepalese judiciary. Qualitative research involving 52 stakeholders demonstrates that from the *demand-side*, the public complains about political intervention in judicial appointments, a backlog of 165,000 cases, and the alienation of vulnerable groups (*Dalits, Madhesi, PwD, rural women*) \[2, 6, 21, 25, 27–28\]. From the *supply-side*, judges confirm the destructive impact of politicization and propose public-oriented (*people-centered*) reforms \[30–32, 36–37\].

## Legal Basis

- **Constitution of Nepal 2015**: Guarantees the right to equal justice and an independent judiciary.
- **Five-Year Strategic Plans of the Nepali Judiciary**: Strategic Plan I (2004–2009), Plan II (2009–2014), Plan III (2014–2019), and Plan IV (2019–2023) \[12, 32–33, 62–64\].
- **Reports of the Supreme Court High-Level Probe Commissions (2015, 2021)**: Official reports addressing irregularities, malpractices, and corruption within the judiciary.
- **Sustainable Development Goal 16 (SDG 16)**: Equal access to justice for all.

## Background

Although Nepal has transitioned to a federal structure, the judiciary continues to operate under a centralized unitary model. Political instability and recurring judicial scandals have exacerbated public skepticism. Judicial reforms thus far have tended to be "inward-looking," focusing solely on internal procedural efficiency without directly engaging the community \[9, 11–12\].

## Objectives

1\. Map the root causes of the judicial trust deficit in Nepal from both demand and supply perspectives. 2. Overcome systemic barriers to access to justice for marginalized groups. 3. Encourage transparent, merit-based judicial appointments to eliminate political intervention. 4. Reduce the backlog of 165,000 cases through modernization and judicial transparency.

## Scope

The study encompasses empirical research in Kathmandu and three rural districts in Nepal, involving 30 community members in 3 *Focus Group Discussions* (FGDs) and 22 in-depth interviews with judges, court officials, and advocates \[15–19\].

## Key Provisions

- **Dual-Sided Evaluation of Judicial Trust**:

1.  *Demand-Side (Justice Seekers)*: Views the judiciary as a distant, expensive institution influenced by political affiliation and social status \[2, 21, 23, 27–29\].
2.  *Supply-Side (Judicial Actors)*: Acknowledges the importance of integrity and communication transparency, but complains about the courtroom insulation of judges \[30–32, 34–36\].

- **Case Backlog Findings**: The Supreme Court of Nepal resolves only 17% of total annual cases, leaving a backlog of \>27,000 cases in the Supreme Court and 165,000 cases across all judicial levels.
- **Access Distrust Level**: 72% of female victims of sexual violence in Nepal do not seek formal legal protection due to a lack of trust and social stigma.

## Implementation

- **Community Outreach Strategy**: Encouraging the implementation of community engagement programs and public communication to demystify the judicial process \[31–33, 46, 53\].
- **Judicial Appointment Reform**: Enforcing ethical standards and transparency in judicial recruitment under the supervision of the Judicial Council.

## Policy Problems

- **Politicization of Judicial Appointments**: Public trust collapsed due to the perception that court decisions are influenced by political party loyalty \[2, 27–29\].
- **Barriers for Marginalized Groups**: Dalits, Madhesis, persons with disabilities, and rural women face double discrimination and legal language alienation.

## Reform / Change

- **Digital Courts**: Adopting digital justice system technologies similar to those in Estonia and Singapore.
- **Mobile Courts**: Implementing Kenya's *mobile courts* model to bring justice closer to rural areas.
- **Community Legal Education**: Expanding clinical legal education modeled after practices in Indonesia.
