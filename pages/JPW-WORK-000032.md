## 4. POLICY ANALYSIS

- **Background**: \[FACT\] This study examines the evolution of personal data protection law and digital privacy in the European Union through the lens of recent CJEU case law (2018–2019). Rapid advancements in information technology, social media platforms, global search engines, and automated data collection required clear legal boundaries reconciling effective law enforcement with the protection of citizens' fundamental rights.
- **Policy Problem**: \[FACT\]
  - **Telecommunications Data Access**: Doubt raised by a Spanish national court (\*Audiencia Provincial de Tarragona\*) over whether disclosing SIM card owner/IMEI data by telecommunication providers is strictly limited to serious crimes (\*delitos graves\*) punishable by more than 5 years' imprisonment.
  - **Third-Party Data Collection Liability**: Lack of clarity regarding the liability of commercial website operators embedding third-party modules (such as Facebook's "Like" button) that automatically transmit visitor data.
  - **Balancing Data Rights and Freedom of Information**: Handling search engine de-referencing applications pointing to sensitive personal data (political opinions, religious beliefs, criminal records).
  - **Territorial Scope of the Right to be Forgotten**: Demand by the French data protection authority (CNIL) that Google execute global de-referencing across all worldwide domain extensions.
  - **Passive/Automatic Cookie Consent**: Use of pre-ticked checkboxes (\*casilla por defecto\*) in online promotional games to collect consumer data.
- **Legal/Policy Gap**: \[FACT\] Absence of a harmonized EU norm extending the territorial reach of de-referencing beyond EU borders; lack of clarity on joint responsibility (\*corresponsabilidad\*) before the full application of GDPR 2016/679; and the need to restrict hidden identifiers (\*identificadores ocultos\*) without explicit consumer consent.
- **Objectives**: \[FACT\] Affirm alignment between Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU (CFR) and sectoral regulations; uphold effective criminal investigations without breaching proportionality; guarantee transparency in commercial data harvesting; and limit the extraterritorial reach of EU law on international search engines.
- **Implementation**: \[FACT\]
  - **Case C-207/16 (Ministerio Fiscal)**: CJEU ruled that accessing subscriber SIM/IMEI identity data without access to communication data is not a serious interference (\*injerencia grave\*), permitting authorization for general criminal investigations without being restricted to serious crimes.
  - **Case C-40/17 (Fashion ID)**: Website operators embedding a Facebook "Like" button share joint responsibility (\*corresponsable\*) for initial data collection and transmission, but are not liable for subsequent processing carried out exclusively by Facebook.
  - **Case C-136/17 (GC e.a. v CNIL)**: Search engines must verify whether refusing to de-reference links with sensitive content passes the strict necessity test regarding freedom of information.
  - **Case C-507/17 (Google v CNIL)**: Search engine de-referencing obligations are restricted to EU member state domain versions and do not apply extraterritorially worldwide.
  - **Case C-673/17 (Planet49)**: Pre-ticked cookie consent checkboxes violate EU law; consent must be specific, active, and fully informed.
- **Evidence & Impact**: \[FACT\] Annulment of a €100,000 fine imposed on Google Inc. regarding global extraterritoriality claims; strengthening consumer association standing in data privacy litigation; and enforcement of explicit cookie consent across all European websites.
- **Reform & Unresolved Issues**: \[FACT\] EU member state national authorities remain empowered to enforce higher data protection standards domestically (including global de-referencing if permitted under national law); and full implementation of GDPR 2016/679 principles across all digital data controllers.

5.  **Policy Implementation (C-207/16)**: What did the CJEU rule regarding police access requests for SIM/IMEI identity data in criminal theft investigations under the Ministerio Fiscal case?
    - **Factual Answer**: \[FACT\] The CJEU ruled that disclosing user SIM/IMEI identity data does not constitute a serious interference (\*injerencia grave\*), allowing authorization for general criminal offense investigations without requiring serious crimes (\*delitos graves\*).
6.  **Policy Implementation (C-40/17)**: Why was website operator Fashion ID held jointly responsible (\*corresponsable\*) with Facebook Ireland?
    - **Factual Answer**: \[FACT\] Because Fashion ID embedded Facebook's "Like" button on its website for commercial benefit, making it jointly responsible for the initial collection and transmission of visitor data, though not for subsequent processing by Facebook.
7.  **Policy Problem (C-136/17)**: What condition must search engine operators verify when evaluating de-referencing requests for sensitive personal data?
    - **Factual Answer**: \[FACT\] Search engines must verify whether maintaining the link is strictly necessary (\*strictly necessary\*) for protecting internet users' freedom of information.
8.  **Policy Problem (C-507/17)**: Is Google required to execute de-referencing across all global versions of its search engine worldwide?
    - **Factual Answer**: \[FACT\] No. The CJEU ruled that EU law does not apply extraterritorially, restricting mandatory de-referencing to search engine versions corresponding to EU member states.
9.  **Policy Implementation (C-673/17)**: Why were pre-ticked checkboxes (\*casilla por defecto\*) for cookie consent ruled unlawful in the Planet49 case?
    - **Factual Answer**: \[FACT\] Because EU law (Directives 2002/58, 95/46, and GDPR) requires explicit, specific, and active consent from users, meaning passive browsing or pre-selected choices fail to meet validity standards.
10. **Legal Relations**: Are national authorities of EU member states permitted to grant higher levels of fundamental rights protection than EU standards regarding de-referencing?
    - **Factual Answer**: \[FACT\] Yes, the CJEU confirmed that EU regulations do not prevent member state national authorities from applying broader protections at the national level.

## 6. MEDIAWIKI OUTPUT

## Summary

**Brief Examination of the Importance of Technological Advances in the Most Recent Jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European Union** (*Breve examen de la importancia de los avances tecnológicos en la jurisprudencia más reciente del Tribunal de Justicia de la Unión Europea*) (Stable ID: **JPW-WORK-000032**) is a doctrinal legal analysis authored by Juan José González Rivas, President of the Constitutional Court of Spain. The work examines five landmark decisions rendered by the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) between 2018 and 2019 regarding the adaptation of fundamental rights to digital technology. The study evaluates the limits of telecommunications data access in criminal inquiries (\*Ministerio Fiscal\* C-207/16), joint liability in social plug-in data harvesting (\*Fashion ID\* C-40/17), requirements for de-referencing sensitive content (\*GC e.a.\* C-136/17), territorial limits of the right to be forgotten (\*Google v CNIL\* C-507/17), and prohibitions on automated cookie consent (\*Planet49\* C-673/17).

## Legal Basis

The jurisprudential and policy analyses in this publication rely on European Union and Spanish domestic legal frameworks:

- **Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (CFR)**: Article 7 (respect for private and family life) and Article 8 (protection of personal data).
- **EU Data Protection Frameworks**: General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR / Regulation EU 2016/679), Directive 95/46/EC, Directive 2002/58/EC (ePrivacy), and Directive 2009/136/EC.
- **Spanish Domestic Statutes**: Law 25/2007 on data retention, Criminal Code (Art. 33.2.b), and Criminal Procedure Law (\*LECr\* Art. 579.1.1º).

## Background

Rapid technological evolution across communication networks, social media platforms, and search engines generated friction between individual privacy rights, law enforcement efficiency, and public freedom of information. The CJEU issued a series of key rulings to balance these fundamental rights under the CFR and GDPR frameworks.

## Objectives

1\. Detail CJEU legal criteria regarding police access limits to telecommunications subscriber data. 2. Map legal liability for website operators utilizing third-party social plug-ins. 3. Establish territorial limits for enforcing search engine de-referencing (right to be forgotten). 4. Prevent unauthorized consumer data collection via automated consent mechanisms (\*cookies\*).

## Scope

The study covers five core operational pillars: 1. *Telecommunications Data Access (C-207/16)*: Distinction between sensitive communication data and subscriber SIM/IMEI identity records. 2. *Joint Data Liability (C-40/17)*: Joint responsibility of Fashion ID and Facebook Ireland for initial data transmission. 3. *Sensitive Data & Right to be Forgotten (C-136/17)*: Strict necessity test (\*strictly necessary\*) for de-referencing political, religious, or criminal content. 4. *Territorial Scope of De-referencing (C-507/17)*: Rejection of global extraterritoriality claims against Google LLC. 5. *Informed Cookie Consent (C-673/17)*: Invalidation of pre-ticked checkbox (\*casilla por defecto\*) schemes under Planet49.

## Key Provisions

- **Non-Serious Interference Access (C-207/16)**: Accessing subscriber SIM/IMEI identity data does not constitute serious interference, allowing authorization in general criminal investigations.
- **Joint Responsibility (C-40/17)**: Website operators embedding social "Like" buttons for commercial benefit share responsibility for initial user data collection.
- **EU Territorial Limit (C-507/17)**: Search engine operators are obligated to de-reference search results within EU member state domain extensions, but not globally.
- **Active Cookie Consent (C-673/17)**: Cookie consent must be explicit, active, and accompanied by transparent information regarding duration and third-party access.

## Implementation

Enforcement is driven by national courts of EU member states (\*Audiencia Provincial de Tarragona\*, French \*Conseil d'État\*, German \*Bundesgerichtshof\*), data protection authorities (CNIL), and global technology platforms (Google, Facebook).

## Policy Problems

- Balancing public freedom of information with individual privacy rights on search engine indexes.
- Addressing extraterritorial enforcement challenges regarding non-EU internet users.
- Mitigating dark patterns and pre-selected options manipulating consumer consent.

## Reform

- Full enforcement of General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR 2016/679) standards.
- Recognition of national authority powers to enforce broader fundamental rights protections domestically.
