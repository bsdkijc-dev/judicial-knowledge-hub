# `judicial-policy-territoriality-functionality-mak-2013`

**The Framework for Balancing Territoriality and Functionality in Judicial Specialization** is a policy framework and comparative judicial organization analysis that evaluates the paradigm shift from the classic organizational standard based on "territoriality" (geographical proximity and the guarantee of the *lawful judge*) toward a standard of "functionality" (specialization, expertise, and efficiency) in the Netherlands, France, and Germany. It maps the balancing matrix between the classic principles of the *Rule of Law* and the principles of *New Public Management* (NPM) \[1, 5, 8, 13–16, 23–27, 30–33\].

## Summary

Published by Dr. Elaine Mak (Erasmus University Rotterdam) in the *International Journal for Court Administration* (IJCA), this policy framework analyzes the dilemma of specialization in judicial organization across the Netherlands, France, and Germany. Based on her 2008 doctoral thesis, Mak highlights the shifting discourse from the classic *Rule of Law* paradigm (independence, impartiality, geographical proximity, and the *lawful judge*) toward the integration of *New Public Management* principles (transparency, effectiveness, efficiency) \[13–16\]. The study presents an instrument for balancing territorial limits with functional needs based on the complexity and frequency of cases \[18–21\].

## Legal Basis

- **Dutch Judicial Law:** *Article 116(1) of the Dutch Constitution (Grondwet)*; *Judiciary Organisation Act (Wet op de rechterlijke organisatie)* Articles 40, 43, 58; and the *Decree on better use of session capacity (Besluit nevenvestigings- en nevenzittingsplaatsen)*.
- **French Judicial Law:** *Article 34(3) of the French Constitution (1958)*; *Code de procédure pénale* Article L706-17 (Special authority of the TGI Paris for terrorism cases); *Code du travail* Article L511-1; and the *Code de l'organisation judiciaire* Article L411-4.
- **German Judicial Law:** *German Basic Law (Grundgesetz)* Articles 92 ff, 95, 96 (*Bundespatentgericht*), and Article 101(1) (*gesetzlicher Richter / lawful judge*); as well as the *Patentgesetz* Article 65(1).

## Background

Traditional judicial organization standards heavily venerate geographical location (territoriality) to ensure equal citizen access to justice. However, the tide of modernization and the surge in complex legal cases demand the creation of specialist judges based on legal subject matter (functionality). Dr. Elaine Mak's research dissects the balance between these two standards to prevent management efficiency from undermining judicial independence and impartiality.

## Objectives

1\. Identify the characteristics and instruments of judicial specialization (case concentration, allocation of specialist judges, inter-court cooperation). 2. Analyze the normative tension between the classic *Rule of Law* and *New Public Management* (NPM) \[13–16\]. 3. Formulate a taxonomy for a jurisdiction allocation matrix based on case type and frequency \[19–21\]. 4. Develop discussion scenarios to maintain court legitimacy in the eyes of the public \[30–33\].

## Scope

This policy covers a comparative study of judicial organization in three Continental European legal traditions: the Netherlands (a single integrated court system), France (separation of the *ordre judiciaire* and *ordre administratif*), and Germany (five specialized judicial branches / *Gerichtsbarkeiten*) \[1, 5, 8, 10–12\].

## Key Provisions

- **Definition of Specialization (Hol & Loth):** A principle of professionalism encompassing three aspects: mastery of knowledge, comparison level with other legal professions (surroundings), and organizational division of labor.
- **Jurisdiction Allocation Matrix:**
  - *Simple & Frequent Cases:* Handled by general first-instance territorial courts.
  - *Complex & Frequent Cases:* Handled by specialized territorial courts (e.g., *conseils de prud'hommes* or *Arbeitsgerichte*).
  - *Simple & Infrequent Cases:* Handled by special courts with general jurisdiction (e.g., examining magistrates for *megazaken*).
  - *Complex & Infrequent Cases:* Handled by highly specialized centralized functional courts (e.g., the *Companies and Business Court / Ondernemingskamer*).
- **Risk of Violating the Lawful Judge (Gesetzlicher Richter):** The transfer of case files between courts for efficiency risks deviating from the fundamental right to a judge established by law.

## Implementation

- **Netherlands:** The use of *traveling judges*, the establishment of the *Noordelijke Fraudekamer*, and the authority of the *Ondernemingskamer*.
- **France:** The restructuring of the judicial map (*carte judiciaire*) by Minister Rachida Dati and the concentration of terrorism cases at the TGI Paris.
- **Germany:** The operationalization of five specialized judicial branches and the federal patent court (*Bundespatentgericht*).

## Policy Problems

- **Threats to Impartiality in Special Courts:** The *Clickfonds* case in the Netherlands demonstrated that establishing a special chamber triggers public perceptions of judicial bias (appearance of impartiality).
- **Erosion of Judicial Immovability:** Placing judges in session locations outside their headquarters (*nevenzittingsplaatsen*) erodes their protection against unilateral transfer.

## Reform / Change

- **Three Future Scenarios:**

1.  *Pushing the Limits:* 100% priority on transparency, effectiveness, and NPM efficiency.
2.  *Return to the Basics:* Re-enforcing the separation of powers, independence, and the guarantee of the *lawful judge*.
3.  *Rethinking Legitimacy:* Balancing functional, institutional, and argumentative dimensions for court legitimacy based on stakeholder needs.

### 3 Most Important Improvements
