# Judicial-policy-courts-as-anthrobots-2024

# **'Courts as Anthrobots: Learning from Human Forms of Interaction to Develop a Philosophically Healthy Model for Judicial Automation' (Kelemen & de Miranda, 2024)**

### Wiki Entity Page

**The Anthrobotic Judicial Automation and Philosophical Health Model** (*Courts as Anthrobots*) is a conceptual policy framework and judicial automation ethics model that conceptualizes courts as *anthrobotic* systems—an interactive cohabitation between humans (judges, lawyers, clerks) and machines (algorithms/AI). It proposes an automation design that aligns with the group ethos (*esprit de corps*) and three modes of intelligence to achieve a philosophically healthy justice system (*philosophical health*).

## Summary

Judicial automation must not be designed using a single universal approach. Kelemen & de Miranda (2024) assert that automation must be tailored to the specific legal tradition (*Civil Law* vs. *Common Law*) and the type of group *esprit de corps*. Purely analytic intelligence-based automation risks perpetuating algorithmic bias (*automation of bias*) and eroding public trust. Therefore, automation should be limited to routine analytical tasks to free up judges' time to cultivate dialectic and *crealectic* intelligence \[1, 5, 78–86, 108, 121\].

## Legal Basis

- **European Ethical Charter on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Judicial Systems** (CEPEJ / Council of Europe, 2018).
- **Doctrine of Binding Precedent / Stare Decisis** (Common Law Tradition).
- **Doctrine of La bouche de la loi** (Montesquieu, 1748).
- **Prohibition of Non Liquet** (Article 4, French Civil Code 1804).
- **Principle of the Right to a Fair Trial** (US Constitution & Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms).

## Background

Discussions on judicial automation have been ongoing since the 1970s, covering random panel assignments to file sorting and criminal sentencing drafting. The use of machine learning transforms courts into *anthrobotic* units. However, reducing law to mere analytical mathematics threatens the essence of human justice \[4, 78–81\].

## Objectives

1\. Incorporate group ethos (*esprit de corps*) and *Value Sensitive Design* into the judicial AI architecture. 2. Prevent the *automation of bias* and maintain public trust in the courts. 3. Integrate three modes of intelligence: Analytic, Dialectic, and *Crealectic* \[78–93\]. 4. Realize *philosophical health* and *epistemic justice* within the legal system.

## Scope

This theoretical policy includes a comparative analysis of two main branches of Western legal traditions:

- **Civil Law:** Focuses on pure analytical reasoning, anonymous collective representation (*la bouche de la loi*), and a strong conformative ethos.
- **Common Law:** Balances dialectic and creative ethos through adversarial debate, the judge as a precedent-maker, and the delivery of dissenting opinions.

## Key Provisions

- **Four Types of Esprit de Corps:**

1.  *Conformative:* Strong adherence to rules and legal duty \[18, 30–33\].
2.  *Universalist:* Decision-making based on collegial equality and standardized rules (service) \[18, 44–48\].
3.  *Autonomist:* Intellectual independence and hierarchy of expertise (skill) \[18, 48–55\].
4.  *Creative:* Innovation, dialogical dynamics, and openness (enthusiasm) \[18, 55–60\].

- **Three Modes of Judicial Intelligence:**

1.  *Analytic Intelligence:* Logical and mathematical data operations (dominant in machines/AI) \[78–79\].
2.  *Dialectic Intelligence:* Weighing opposing arguments through adversarial debate \[80–85\].
3.  *Crealectic Intelligence:* Creative synthesis, empathy, and understanding of unquantifiable potential possibilities \[86–93, 118–119\].

## Implementation

- **Limiting Automation:** Assigning AI to routine analytical tasks, while substantive decision-making remains handled by human judges.
- **Self-Criticism Protocol:** Installing an anti-bias system (self-criticism protocol) in judicial AI to avoid groupthink.

## Policy Problems

- **Lack of Trust in "Algorithmic Judges":** A study by Yalcin et al. (2023) proves that the public does not trust AI decisions for emotionally complex cases.
- **Automation of Bias:** AI algorithms are prone to repeating historical prejudices recorded in past case data.
- **Epistemic Injustice:** The risk of marginalized groups not being heard due to distorted automation patterns.

## Reform / Change

Adopting **5 Principles of Philosophical Health** in the judiciary \[97–113\]: 1. *Mental Heroism* (Example: *Brown v. Board of Education* 1954) \[98–101\]. 2. *Deep Orientation* (Example: *R v. Morgentaler* 1988) \[101–105\]. 3. *Critical Creativity* (Example: Justice Harlan's dissent in *Plessy v. Ferguson* 1896) \[105–109\]. 4. *Deep Listening* (Respecting diverse perspectives / theory of mind) \[110–111\]. 5. *The Creal* (Faith in the constantly evolving dynamics of justice potential) \[111–113\].
