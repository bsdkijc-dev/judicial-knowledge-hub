## Policy Analysis

- **Background & Policy Problem**:
  - Provides new knowledge and perspectives on recidivism reduction for policymakers, practitioners, and researchers.
  - Requires deep empirical understanding of inmate-perceived factors driving desistance alongside international recidivism metrics.
- **Methodology & Research Components**:
  - **Self-Report Inmate Survey (N=896)**: Surveyed 452 first-time inmates (Group 1) and 444 repeat inmates (Group 2). Group 2 sub-cohorts included repeat inmates with short desistance (\<2 years; Group 3) and long desistance (\>10 years; Group 4).
  - **Cross-Country Recidivism Survey**: Evaluated published data from 15 countries, focusing deeply on England/Wales, the United States, South Korea, and New Zealand.
  - **International Field Survey**: Consulted with the National Institute of Justice and Washington State Institute for Public Policy (USA), as well as the Department of Corrections (New Zealand).
- **Inmate Survey Findings**:
  - The single most dominant desistance factor across all groups (up to 60% of respondents) was the presence of "persons who need them".
  - First-time inmates (Group 1) significantly prioritized custody duration, financial leeway, physical/mental health, absence of crime triggers, rewarding jobs, and personal hobbies/goals.
  - Repeat inmates with short desistance (\<2 years; Group 3) perceived a significantly higher risk of future re-offending ('yes'/'almost yes') compared to those with long desistance (\>10 years; Group 4).
- **International Metrics**:
  - **England/Wales (2016)**: 1-year reconviction rate stood at 28.5%.
  - **United States (2005 Cohort)**: Re-arrest rates ranged from 43.9% (1 year) to 83.4% (9 years).
  - **South Korea (2012)**: Reimprisonment rates rose from 0.2% (3 months) to 24.8% (3 years).
  - **New Zealand (2016)**: 1-year reconviction rate was 46.8% for released inmates versus 27.5% for community-sanctioned offenders.
- **Policy Recommendations**:
  - Implementation of tailored case management approaches to enhance motivation.
  - Creation of social network coordinators and cross-disciplinary correctional teamwork.
  - Adoption of cost-benefit analysis and quantitative modeling to measure social impact.
  - Institutionalization of Translational Criminology to bridge communication gaps between researchers and practitioners.
- **Interpretations & Structural Inferences**:
  - Recidivism reduction requires building meaningful interpersonal bonds alongside translational criminology interventions, rather than relying solely on physical detention.
  - Elevated risk perception among repeat inmates with short desistance spans indicates that rapid re-offending damages confidence and recovery motivation, requiring intensive early-stage case management post-release.
