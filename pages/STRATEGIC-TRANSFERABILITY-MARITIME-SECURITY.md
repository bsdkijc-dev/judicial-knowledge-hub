# STRATEGIC-TRANSFERABILITY-MARITIME-SECURITY

# Strategic Transferability Assessment: Adapting Global Maritime Security Governance to Institutional Frameworks

## 1. Macro Strategic Trends in Global Maritime Security (2015–2026)

The global maritime domain has undergone a structural transformation, shifting from a period of localized tactical threats to a state of systemic volatility. Between 2015 and 2022, maritime security was largely defined by the containment of piracy through international naval presence. Since 2024, the landscape has evolved into a complex nexus of transnational organized crime, state-sponsored *shadow fleets*, and kinetic asymmetric warfare. This represents a transition from tactical maritime crime toward structural disruptions that threaten the integrity of the UNCLOS-governed order. For modern governance, the mandate has therefore moved beyond tactical vessel protection toward strategic institutional reform capable of addressing hybrid threats.

### Macro Strategic Indicators

| Macro Strategic Indicator     | Historical Baseline (2015–2022)                     | Contemporary Status (2024–Present)                           | Strategic "So What?"                                                       |
|-------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Global Maritime Incidents** | Averaging 115–120 per year; generally declining.    | Rose to 137 in 2025; 88% successful boardings.               | Increased tactical success for criminals despite naval presence.           |
| **Shadow Fleet Activity**     | Niche operations (Iran/Venezuela).                  | 1,300 to 1,481 active vessels.                               | A parallel fleet generating \$170B+ for sanctioned regimes.                |
| **Chokepoint Disruption**     | Occasional physical bottlenecks, e.g. *Ever Given*. | 70% Suez tonnage drop; kinetic strikes in Bab el-Mandeb.     | Rerouting causing a 6% spike in ton-mile demand and systemic inflation.    |
| **Narcotics Trafficking**     | Bulk shipments via traditional direct routes.       | \>1,347 tonnes seized; 97% cocaine.                          | Deep infiltration of containerized logistics via "rip-on/rip-off" schemes. |
| **Migrant Smuggling**         | Central Mediterranean dominance.                    | Surge along Western African Atlantic Route (46,843 in 2024). | Record mortality (1,200 deaths in 2025) linked to conflict-driven asylum.  |

### Shadow Fleet Risk

The rise of the "Shadow Fleet"—nearly 1,500 aging tankers—redefines the risk profile for coastal states. These vessels operate with unrated insurance and opaque ownership, facilitating energy exports that generated \$120.5 billion for Russia and \$50.5 billion for Iran in recent cycles. A single mechanical failure in a chokepoint by these uninsured vessels could shift the multi-billion-dollar liability for environmental cleanup directly onto the coastal state. This escalating risk environment necessitates the **Benchmark Transferability Score (BTS)** to evaluate which international governance models are genuinely compatible with national frameworks.

## 2. Benchmark Transferability Score (BTS) Framework

As proposed in the *Indonesia Judiciary Future Blueprint 2035–2045*, the **Benchmark Transferability Score (BTS)** is a strategic assessment framework designed to ensure that high-reputation international practices are not blindly adopted but are rigorously vetted for local institutional compatibility.

### BTS Assessment Dimensions

The BTS framework evaluates maritime security interventions across eight dimensions:

- **Legal compatibility:** Alignment with national jurisdictional laws and UNCLOS.
- **Institutional compatibility:** The capability of domestic agencies to host the reform.
- **Technology readiness:** Availability of infrastructure, such as satellite tracking.
- **Workforce readiness:** Specialized training and vetting of maritime personnel.
- **Financial feasibility:** Long-term funding and liability coverage.
- **Scalability:** Ability to expand the model across diverse maritime zones.
- **Rights compatibility:** Adherence to human rights and seafarer protections.
- **Expected justice impact:** The projected success in reducing crime and increasing order.

### Core BTS Principle

\> **High international reputation does not automatically imply high transferability.**

For example, sophisticated high-seas interdiction protocols may fail the "Legal compatibility" test in nations with strict constitutional limits on naval law enforcement within territorial waters.

## 3. Transferability Assessment: Counter-Piracy and Sea Robbery Models

Two divergent institutional paths have emerged in the fight against piracy: regional naval integration represented by the Gulf of Guinea (GoG); and shore-based policing represented by the Straits of Malacca and Singapore (SOMS).

### Yaoundé Architecture and Deep Blue Project

The **Yaoundé Architecture** and **Deep Blue Project** in West Africa represent a high-seas naval escort model. While successful at maintaining a baseline of suppression, the "Expected justice impact" remains limited because deep-water abduction capability endures. West African syndicates conducted four major offshore abductions in 2025, kidnapping 23 crew members despite the naval presence.

### Indonesian Marine Police Model

The **Indonesian Marine Police (IMP)** model in the SOMS emphasizes targeted shore-based interventions. The 2025 surge in Singapore Strait incidents—reaching 108 reports according to ReCAAP—supported a high "Institutional compatibility" score for land-based enforcement. By arresting two primary criminal syndicates on land in mid-2025, the IMP achieved a direct and significant drop in boardings during the fourth quarter. For strategic assessment, the data suggests that land-based syndicate dismantling has a higher justice impact on crew safety in narrow channels than high-seas naval patrolling.

## 4. Institutional Feasibility: Governance of Shadow Fleets and Sanctions Evasion

The "Dark Fleet" architecture utilizes AIS spoofing, "flag hopping", and dark ship-to-ship (STS) transfers to circumvent international law. This is no longer merely a regulatory nuisance but a geopolitical engine financing state-backed conflicts through massive revenue streams, including the figures cited in the source of \$120.5B for Russia and \$50.5B for Iran.

### Legal Compatibility

From a "Legal compatibility" perspective, coastal states can utilize UNCLOS Articles 56 and 220 to mandate insurance verification for tankers.

### Financial Feasibility

Financial feasibility is critical because shadow vessels use unrated P&I providers, leaving coastal states exposed to uncompensated oil-spill risks.

### Stateless Vessel Interdiction

Recent precedents cited in the assessment include the January 2026 US-led seizure of the *Marinera* and the June 2026 UK boarding of the *Smyrtos* in the English Channel. These actions are presented as ground-truth proof of concept for **Stateless Vessel Interdiction**. They demonstrate how "Legal compatibility" with UNCLOS Article 110 may be leveraged to secure chokepoints against non-compliant actors.

## 5. Technical and Workforce Readiness: Supply Chain Integrity and Counter-Trafficking

Maritime drug trafficking has moved into legitimate containerized logistics, with 1,347 tonnes seized globally according to the source. This shift necessitates movement from physical inspections toward data-driven interdiction.

### Technology Readiness

Implementation of **AI-Driven Cargo Anomaly Detection** is considered feasible using existing satellite tracking data.

### Workforce Readiness

The primary challenge is the "rip-on/rip-off" scheme. These operations target specific geographic nodes, loading narcotics at Latin American ports including Guayaquil, Santos, and Port-au-Prince. Narcotics are then extracted at European hubs including Antwerp, Rotterdam, and Hamburg. A high "Workforce readiness" score therefore requires not only technical skill but rigorous anti-corruption vetting. Without specialized personnel oversight at these logistics hubs, advanced detection technology remains vulnerable to circumvention by corrupt internal actors.

## 6. Macroeconomic and Human Rights Impact Analysis

Maritime instability has created a dual crisis of economic inflation and humanitarian suffering.

### Economic Indicators (2024–2025)

| Indicator              | Reported Impact                                                         |
|------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Freight Volatility** | SCFI surged 149% in 2024, peaking at 3,600 points.                      |
| **Operational Costs**  | African rerouting added \$400,000 in EU ETS emission costs per voyage.  |
| **Consumer Impact**    | Sustained disruptions increased global consumer prices by 0.6%.         |
| **SIDS Impact**        | Small Island Developing States faced a 1.3% surge in food import costs. |

The source identifies the SIDS food-import increase as a primary "Justice Impact" metric for global governance.

### Humanitarian and Rights Analysis

The "Rights compatibility" of current models is strained. The Western African Atlantic Route (WAAR) reportedly saw a record 1,200 deaths in 2025. The source indicates that this is no longer purely economic migration, noting that Malian nationals represented 40% of arrivals and interpreting this as evidence that the driver had shifted toward conflict-driven asylum. At the same time, the shadow fleet has contributed to a seafarer abandonment crisis, with more than 6,000 sailors reportedly left on unseaworthy vessels by disappearing shell companies. This creates a need for a "Rights compatibility" reform that prioritizes seafarer protection, search and rescue, and humanitarian considerations over simple border enforcement.

## 7. Strategic Synthesis: Roadmap for High-Transferability Governance

The BTS framework indicates that effective governance must shift from reactive naval presence toward proactive institutional oversight.

### Four-Pillar Roadmap

- **1. Regulatory Evasion:** Standardize **Stateless Vessel Interdiction** protocols, using the *Smyrtos* case as a cited reference, and implement mandatory insurance verification to protect coastal-state finances.
- **2. Chokepoint Protection:** Prioritize shore-based enforcement to dismantle syndicates on land, drawing on the IMP model, which the assessment identifies as more effective than naval escorts at reducing total incidents.
- **3. Supply Chain Integrity:** Deploy **AI-driven anomaly detection** at high-risk nodes, including the Guayaquil–Rotterdam corridor, while strengthening port-side anti-corruption vetting.
- **4. Environmental / IUU Security:** Utilize **Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)** to track "dark vessels" and establish regional liability funds to cover the costs of uninsured casualties.

## Strategic Conclusion

By filtering international practices through the BTS framework, states can ensure that maritime strategies are institutionally resilient. In an era of evolving asymmetric threats, institutional agility and precise legal alignment are presented as essential conditions for maintaining global trade stability and the integrity of the UNCLOS maritime order.

## Key Strategic Principle

\> **International maritime governance should be transferred through evidence-based institutional adaptation, not direct institutional imitation.**
