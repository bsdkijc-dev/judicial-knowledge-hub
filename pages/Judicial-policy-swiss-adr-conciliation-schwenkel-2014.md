# Judicial-policy-swiss-adr-conciliation-schwenkel-2014

**The Mandatory Conciliation Authorities and Public Confidence Policy Framework in Switzerland** is a judicial administration policy and public confidence evaluation framework regarding mandatory conciliation authorities (\*Friedensrichter / Vermittler / juges de paix / giudici di pace\*) in Switzerland. Formulated by Christof Schwenkel (University of Lucerne / Interface Policy Studies, 2014) based on a national survey of 3,484 respondents across 26 Swiss cantons under the \*Basic Research into Court Management in Switzerland\* project supported by the Swiss National Science Foundation (SNSF). This framework maps three institutional models of cantonal conciliation (Type 1: integrated into courts of first instance; Type 2: independent authorities appointed by parliament/government/courts; Type 3: decentralized authorities directly elected by the people), tests the impact of institutional design on public confidence, and evaluates the implementation of Article 197 of the Swiss Code of Civil Procedure (CCP 2011) \[2, 6, 11–13, 28, 54–56\].

## Summary

Published by Christof Schwenkel in the \*International Journal for Court Administration\* (IJCA), this empirical policy framework evaluates the legitimacy and public confidence of mandatory conciliation authorities (\*Schlichtungsbehörden\*) across 26 Swiss cantons. Under Article 197 of the Swiss CCP 2011, every civil dispute must be preceded by an attempted conciliation proceeding. Although procedural law is uniform nationally, each canton retains autonomy under Article 3 of the CCP to determine its conciliation institutional structure. This study categorizes cantons into 3 types of institutional models and demonstrates that neither institutional design nor direct popular vote significantly affects public confidence \[11–13, 52, 56\]. Public trust (averaging 6.81/10) is predominantly determined by positive personal experiences and the quality of service during the conciliation proceedings.

## Legal Basis

- **Swiss Code of Civil Procedure (CCP / Schweizerische Zivilprozessordnung / ZPO 2011):**
  - \*Article 197 CCP\*: Establishes the general obligation that every civil proceeding must be preceded by a conciliation attempt.
  - \*Article 200 CCP\*: Exceptions to mandatory general conciliation for employment, tenancy, and gender equality disputes.
  - \*Article 201(1) CCP\*: The responsibility of conciliation authorities to reconcile parties informally.
  - \*Article 212 CCP\*: The authority to render a decision for financial disputes not exceeding CHF 2,000 upon the plaintiff's request.
  - \*Article 213 CCP\*: The right of the parties to replace conciliation with voluntary mediation.
  - \*Article 3 CCP\*: The foundation of cantonal autonomy to organize the structure and qualifications of conciliators.
- **Cantonal Court Organisation Laws**: Cantonal regulations governing the election procedures and jurisdictions of conciliators (e.g., GOG AG §14, KV BL §25, LOJV VD §24, GPR ZH §40) \[14–19\].
- **Not found in the available sources**: Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text.

## Background

Conciliation has a long tradition in Switzerland dating back to the 19th century, designed to reduce court workloads and provide friendly, low-cost legal channels (\*low-threshold access to justice\*). The unification of the CCP in 2011 reinforced this obligation across all cantons.

## Objectives

1\. Map the 3 types of institutional models for conciliation authorities across the 26 Swiss cantons \[11–13, 20\]. 2. Measure the level of public confidence in conciliation authorities \[28–30, 55\]. 3. Empirically test the influence of institutional design, elections, and personal experience on public trust \[35–45, 50–52\].

## Scope

This policy encompasses the analysis of general civil conciliation authorities in the 26 Swiss cantons based on national survey data from 3,484 respondents conducted between March and April 2013.

## Key Provisions

- **Typology of Three Cantonal Institutional Conciliation Models**:

1.  \*Type 1 (Integrated Court-based - 5 Cantons)\*: Conciliation is conducted directly by professional judges/clerks at courts of first instance (BS, FR, GE, JU, NE).
2.  \*Type 2 (Independent Appointed - 10 Cantons)\*: Conciliation authorities are independent from courts; members are appointed by cantonal parliaments, governments, or courts (AR, BE, GR, LU, NW, OW, SG, SH, UR, VD).
3.  \*Type 3 (Decentralized Popularly Elected - 11 Cantons)\*: Decentralized bodies at the municipal level where justices of the peace (\*Friedensrichter / Vermittler\*) are directly elected by popular vote, mostly laypersons (AG, AI, BL, GL, SO, SZ, TG, TI, VS, ZG, ZH).

- **Empirical Findings on Public Confidence (Scale 0–10)**:
  - \*Average Confidence in Swiss Conciliation\*: \*\*6.81\*\* (higher than Cantonal Governments at 6.34 and Cantonal Parliaments at 6.16, but lower than Cantonal Courts at 7.04 and the Federal Supreme Court at 7.28).
  - \*Testing Institutional Models (H1)\*: Rejected. There is no significant difference between Type 1 (6.87), Type 2 (6.82), and Type 3 (6.79).
  - \*Positive Experience (H4)\*: Strongly accepted. A positive experience during conciliation is the primary determinant for increasing trust (Beta = .567 to .613, R² = 43.4%) \[43, 51–52, 57\].

## Implementation

- \*\*Citizen Interaction Data\*\*: 21% of Swiss citizens have interacted directly with a conciliation authority, and 24.3% have participated in the election of a justice of the peace.
- \*\*Experience Evaluation\*\*: 59% of citizens with direct contact reported a positive experience, 24% were neutral, and 17% had a negative experience.

## Policy Problems

- **Personal Contact Erosion Paradox**: Having personal contact with a conciliation authority is generally negatively correlated with trust (r = -.103), proving that poor or unfair interactions risk severely damaging institutional legitimacy \[42, 47, 51–52, 56–57\].
- **The Myth of Direct Election Legitimacy**: The popular vote of justices of the peace is proven not to automatically enhance public confidence.

## Reform / Change

- Shifting the focus of judicial reform from institutional/legal restructuring toward improving the quality of trial proceedings and training the personality and mediation skills of conciliators.
- Elevating procedural fairness standards in conciliation hearings \[42, 57–59\].

### 3 Most Important Improvements

1\. **Shift the Focus of Judicial Reform Toward Improving Trial Process Quality:** Guide cantons not to waste energy on restructuring institutional design laws (such as changing the election methods of justices of the peace), but to focus on personality training, mediation/conciliation techniques, and enhancing the quality of proceedings. 2. **Standardize Procedural Fairness:** Ensure that all conciliation hearings are conducted with empathy and respect for the parties to prevent negative experiences, which have been proven to significantly damage public trust \[42–43, 57–59\]. 3. **Development of Micro-Interaction Qualitative ADR Research:** Deepen follow-up studies to strictly differentiate between the influence of procedural justice and outcome satisfaction in shaping public confidence \[43, 58–59\].
