# JPW-PER-CC-001

**The Italian Legal Imaginary and Journal Policy Framework** refers to the editorial, epistemological, and comparative policy structure developed by *The Italian Law Journal* (ItALJ) to critically analyze, unpack, and disseminate the Italian legal tradition within global legal discourse.[^1]

## Lead

Founded in 2014 by [Camilla Crea](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Camilla_Crea) and a group of Italian civil and comparative law scholars, *The Italian Law Journal* is an open-access, peer-reviewed e-journal using English as a lingua franca.[^2] Grounded in the post-WWII constitutionalization of private law under the [1947 Italian Constitution](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Italy) and the rise of comparative legal studies, the framework seeks to move Italian legal thought beyond classic formalism and national insularity.[^3]

## Summary

The framework addresses two major epistemological turns in Italian legal history:

1.  **National constitutionalization of private law**: infusing the [1942 Civil Code](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Italian_Civil_Code) with constitutional values — human dignity, personalism, substantive equality (Art. 3(2)), and solidarity — shifting private law's focus from purely economic concerns toward the person.[^4]
2.  **Rise of comparative legal studies**: breaking the isolation of domestic law through methodologies including [Gino Gorla](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Gino_Gorla)'s case method, [Mauro Cappelletti](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Mauro_Cappelletti)'s functionalism, and [Rodolfo Sacco](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Rodolfo_Sacco)'s theory of legal formants and cryptotypes.[^5]

## Legal basis

The policy framework operates at the intersection of:

- **[Italian Constitution (1947)](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Italy)**: Articles 2 (inviolable rights), 3 (formal and substantive equality), and 42 (social function of property).[^6]
- **Civil Code of 1942**: reinterpreted through constitutionally oriented reading and general clauses (good faith, social utility, unjust harm).[^7]
- **Ministerial decrees of 1999 and 2005**: establishing comparative law as an autonomous, mandatory course in Italian law school curricula.[^8]

## Background

Italian legal scholarship was historically constrained by a perception of rigid positivism and dogmatism, described by [John Henry Merryman](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/John_Henry_Merryman) as the "Italian Style."[^9] Earlier domestic benchmarks — Alessandro Pizzorusso's *Italian Studies in Law* (1992) and Giacinto della Cananea's *Italian Journal of Public Law* (2009) — preceded ItALJ's focus on private law as a tool of social engineering.[^10]

## Objectives

- Make the Italian legal tradition visible and accessible to the international community in English.[^11]
- Foster bidirectional, critical dialogue rather than uncritical repetition of settled narratives.[^12]
- Promote interdisciplinary openness linking private law with history, economics, sociology, and philosophy.[^13]

## Scope

Applies to international legal scholarship, private law reform, comparative jurisprudence, legal education, and judicial interpretation across continental Europe, the Americas, and other global legal systems.[^14]

## Key concepts

- **Theory of legal formants**: dissecting legal systems into statutory, judicial, and doctrinal "formants," alongside unexpressed "cryptotypes."[^15]
- **Indirect *Drittwirkung***: applying constitutional fundamental rights to horizontal relationships between private parties.[^16]
- **Alternative use of law**: applying constitutional interpretation to advance social justice and protect disadvantaged parties.[^17]

## Implementation

Published biannually under the auspices of Università degli Studi del Sannio and sponsored by the [Harvard Italian Law Association](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Harvard_Italian_Law_Association) (HILA), the Società Italiana degli Studiosi del Diritto Civile (SISDIC), the Società Italiana per la Ricerca nel Diritto Comparato (SIRD), and the National Italian American Bar Association (NIABA).[^18]

## Policy problems

- **Linguistic periphery**: esoteric Italian legal terminology and outdated, hard-to-access English translations of the Italian Civil Code.[^19]
- **Disciplinary silos**: persistent academic fragmentation between public, private, and comparative law departments.[^20]

## Reform proposals

Scholars advocate translating primary Italian judicial decisions into English and expanding trans-systemic legal education programs.[^21]
