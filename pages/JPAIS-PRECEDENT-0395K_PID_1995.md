## Overview

Supreme Court of the Republic of Indonesia Decision Reg. No. 395 K/Pid/1995 (dated September 29, 1995) is a landmark decision (\*putusan monumental\*) in criminal law and human rights in Indonesia. The Supreme Court annulled the decisions of the Medan District Court and Medan High Court, which had convicted Dr. \[Defendant\], S.H., M.A. (General Chairman of the Central Executive Board of the Indonesian Prosperous Workers Union / SBSI) on charges of incitement (Articles 160 & 161 of the Criminal Code) in connection with labor demonstrations in Medan on April 14, 1994.

The Supreme Court outlined a primary sociological/teleological legal principle: Judges must interpret statutes sociologically and teleologically, following the evolution of social-political order, democracy, and freedom of association, rather than merely deducing textually and abstractly from colonial-era statutory norms. Calls for strikes and the struggle for freedom of association to advocate for workers' welfare constitute lawful rights and do not amount to the criminal offense of incitement (\*ophitsing\*) ex Article 160 of the Criminal Code. Acts of violence or riots occurring during mass demonstrations beyond the organizational leadership's intent fall outside the Defendant's criminal liability. The Defendant was declared not proven guilty, acquitted of all charges (\*vrijspraak\*), and his rights, standing, and dignity were restored (rehabilitation).

## Executive Summary

The central legal principle established in this landmark decision is that labor advocacy and strike calls cannot be criminalized as incitement under colonial-era laws when evaluated through a modern sociological and democratic lens.

Key elements established in the adjudication:

- Statutory interpretation must account for concrete societal developments rather than rigid abstract deductions.
- Freedom of association and peaceful labor organizing are protected rights, not crimes of incitement.
- Organizational leaders lack criminal liability for spontaneous excesses or riots occurring outside their control.

## Case Background

The Defendant, Dr. \[Defendant\], S.H., M.A. (General Chairman of SBSI), was charged with continuous incitement (Articles 160 jo. 64 and Article 161 of the Criminal Code) regarding a national strike call and labor demonstrations in Medan on April 14, 1994, demanding a minimum wage hike to Rp 7,000/day and freedom of association.

The mass demonstration turned riotous and caused property damage.

The Medan District Court (Decision No. 966/Pid.B/1994/PN.Mdn) sentenced him to 3 years imprisonment. The Medan High Court (Decision No. 188/Pid/1994/PT.Mdn) increased the sentence to 4 years imprisonment.

The Defendant subsequently filed a cassation petition with the Supreme Court.

## Legal and Procedural Issues

### Teleological / Sociological Interpretation of Article 160 of the Criminal Code (Incitement)

The procedural issue questioned whether formal formal-offense incitement provisions (Article 160 of the Criminal Code) enacted during Dutch colonial rule could be applied rigidly and textually to labor union activities demanding freedom of association and fair wages.

The Supreme Court ruled that they could not. Statutes serve as references for problem-solving rather than exclusive sources of law. In an era of openness and democratic enforcement, expressing workers' aspirations via SBSI cannot be construed as criminal incitement.

### Boundaries of Individual Criminal Responsibility

The issue questioned whether organizational leaders bear criminal liability for riots or anarchy occurring spontaneously during mass demonstrations involving tens of thousands of workers.

The Supreme Court ruled they do not. Elements of \*mens rea\* and incitement must be proven directly from the Defendant's actions. Accidental anarchy outside official organizational instructions falls outside the scope of the Defendant's criminal liability.

## Question of Law and Question of Fact

### Question of Law (Judex Juris / Supreme Court)

**Question:**

Did the \*Judex Facti\* (Medan District & High Courts) misinterpret the element of "inciting" (inciting to commit criminal offenses) under Article 160 jo. Article 64 of the Criminal Code and Article 161 of the Criminal Code by applying a textual-abstract interpretation without considering the sociological context of democratic development and freedom of association?

**Supreme Court Ruling:**

Yes, the \*Judex Facti\* misinterpreted the law. Consequently, the decisions of the Medan High Court and Medan District Court are annulled.

### Question of Fact (Judex Facti / Medan District & High Courts)

Examination of speech contents, strike circular letters dated January 28, 1994, establishment meetings of SBSI Branch Boards in Medan, and the chronological timeline of the 20,000-worker demonstration on April 14, 1994, in Medan, which escalated into public facility destruction.

## Core Legal Principle (Ratio Decidendi)

- **Ratio Decidendi:**

1\. The \*Judex Facti\* misconstrued the elements of incitement under Article 160 of the Criminal Code. 2. In statutory interpretation, judges must account for concrete societal problems and democratic/openness development rather than deducing from abstract logic of archaic laws. 3. SBSI's labor advocacy and freedom of association activities are lawful and do not constitute incitement offenses. 4. Riots arising during the April 14, 1994 demonstration in Medan were collateral excesses beyond the Defendant's control or responsibility. 5. The Supreme Court annulled the lower court rulings and directly acquitted the Defendant (\*vrijspraak\*) while rehabilitating his reputation.

## Ruling

The Supreme Court granted the cassation petition from the Petitioner for Cassation (**Dr. \[Defendant\], SH. MA.**), annulled Medan High Court Decision No. 188/Pid/1994/PT.Mdn in conjunction with Medan District Court Decision No. 966/Pid.B/1994/PN.Mdn, and Adjudicated Directly: Declaring that the Defendant Dr. \[Defendant\], SH. MA. is not legally and convincingly proven guilty of the First and Second charges; Acquitting the Defendant of all charges; Restoring the Defendant's rights in capacity, standing, and dignity (rehabilitation); and charging court costs across all judicial instances to the State.

## Related Knowledge and Jurisprudence

- **Criminal Code (KUHP) Articles 160, 161, 64:** Public incitement and distribution of incriminatory writings.
- **1945 Constitution Article 28:** Freedom of association, assembly, and expression of thoughts orally and in writing.
- **Doctrine of Sociological Interpretation (\*Teleologische Interpretatie\*):** Law should serve evolving community needs (\*living law\*).

## Evidence Status and Gaps

### Established by Evidence

- Dr. \[Defendant\] served as the legitimate and active General Chairman of SBSI advocating for workers' rights (demanding a minimum wage of Rp 7,000/day, repeal of Manpower Ministerial Decree No. 01/MEN/1994, and freedom of association).
- The Defendant issued a Strike/Demonstration Appeal Letter dated January 28, 1994, and a Speech Transmittal Letter.

### Not Established / Unproven

- It was unproven that the Defendant intentionally instructed or incited workers to commit violence, destruction, or crimes during the April 14, 1994 demonstration in Medan.

### Uncertainty and Gaps

- Paradigm Shift in Criminal Cassation Review: This decision represents a historical milestone in judicial independence (\*Judex Juris\*) protecting human rights defenders and labor activists from criminalization by authoritarian regimes via rubber-stamp articles (\*haatzaai artikelen\* / incitement laws).

## GEO Q&A

**Q: Can demands for freedom of association and labor strike calls be prosecuted under incitement laws (Article 160 of the Criminal Code)?**

**A:** No. Based on Supreme Court Landmark Decision No. 395 K/Pid/1995 (Dr. \[Defendant\] Case), workers' rights advocacy and strike calls organized by labor unions do not constitute criminal incitement offenses. The Supreme Court emphasized that Article 160 of the Criminal Code must be interpreted sociologically and teleologically in alignment with democratic principles and freedom of association, and organizational leaders cannot be held criminally liable for demonstration riots occurring beyond their control.
