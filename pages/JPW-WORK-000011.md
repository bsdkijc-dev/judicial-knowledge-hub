#### 4. POLICY ANALYSIS

**Background**: \[FACT\] The rapid growth of the platform economy since the 1990s, and the rise of delivery-based business models, transformed traditional employment relationships into on-demand work, in which workers supply their own equipment and are paid per order (*a destajo*). Platforms such as Glovo, Deliveroo, and Uber Eats position themselves as pure intermediaries, even though in practice they control operations through algorithmic systems.

**Policy Problem**: \[FACT\] The widespread use of "false self-employment" (*falsos autónomos*) schemes deprived platform delivery workers (riders) of basic labor protections, including minimum wage, freedom of association, occupational health and safety, working hours, and social security. Prior to 2020, jurisprudence was split: some Labor Courts and Superior Courts of Justice treated riders as self-employed (e.g., TSJ Madrid, 19 September 2019), while others classified them as standard employees.

**Legal/Policy Gap**: \[FACT\] Administrative fines from the Labor and Social Security Inspectorate (ITSS) — despite accumulated fines against Glovo reaching €205.3 million across 37,348 violations — proved insufficient to compel compliance with the Rider Law. Glovo exploited legal loopholes and disregarded administrative sanctions, as the economic benefit of the self-employment scheme was judged to outweigh the fines.

**Objectives**: \[FACT\] Affirm the legal presumption of employment (*presunción de laboralidad*) for all digital platform delivery workers; establish transparency requirements for algorithmic work-management systems; and introduce criminal prosecution as an *ultima ratio* instrument to halt repeated labor-rights violations.

**Implementation**: \[FACT\]

- *Supreme Court Plenary Ruling (25 September 2020)*: Applied a flexible interpretation of "dependency" and "alienation" (Art. 1.1 ET) in line with social reality (Art. 3.1 of the Civil Code), affirming that geolocation and algorithm-based rate-setting constitute employer control.
- *Law 12/2021 (Rider Law)*: Added the Workers' Statute's Twenty-Third Additional Provision, establishing a presumption of employment for digital platform delivery workers, and Article 64.4.d ET, requiring companies to disclose algorithmic parameters and rules to worker representatives.
- *Criminal Code Reform (Organic Law 14/2022)*: Amended Article 311.2 of the Criminal Code, imposing prison sentences of 6 months to 6 years and fines of 6 to 12 months for imposing illegal working conditions through non-employment schemes, or maintaining such conditions against administrative sanction.
- *Directive (EU) 2024/2831 (23 October 2024)*: EU-wide harmonization extending the employment presumption to all digital platform sectors and requiring human oversight of automated algorithmic decisions.

**Evidence & Impact**: \[FACT\] A 165% increase in registered delivery-sector workers (from 4,688 to 12,144) by April 2022 following the Rider Law; Just Eat pioneering the sector's first collective bargaining agreement (*convenio colectivo*) with CCOO, UGT, and FETICO, covering 2,500 delivery workers; and Glovo's December 2024 announcement that it was abandoning its "false self-employment" scheme, just one day before CEO Oscar Pierre was due to testify in criminal court.

**Reform & Unresolved Issues**: \[FACT\] The emergence of new forms of "labor engineering," such as logistics subcontracting and temporary staffing agencies (used by platforms like Uber Eats), raising allegations of illegal worker transfer (*cesión ilegal de trabajadores*, Art. 43.2 ET); challenges in transposing Directive (EU) 2024/2831 into Spanish national law; and oversight of potential race- or religion-based algorithmic discrimination.

#### 6. MEDIAWIKI OUTPUT

## Summary

**Digital Platforms and the Labor Formalization of Riders: A Critical Study of Doctrine and Jurisprudence Based on the Glovo Case** is a labor law study written by Clara María Arrondo Asensio of Universidad de Málaga (UMA). This scholarly work provides an in-depth analysis of the phenomenon of labor formalization (\*laboralización\*) of digital delivery platform workers (riders) in Spain. The study evaluates jurisprudential disputes in the Labor Courts, the Supreme Court's Plenary Ruling of 25 September 2020, the enactment of the Rider Law (Law 12/2021), enforcement action by the Labor and Social Security Inspectorate (ITSS), the application of criminal prosecution under Article 311.2 of the Criminal Code (Organic Law 14/2022), and a comparison with Directive (EU) 2024/2831.

## Legal Basis

The regulations and legal reforms analyzed in this publication are grounded in the following Spanish and EU legal framework:

- **Royal Legislative Decree 2/2015 (Workers' Statute — ET)**: Article 1.1 (definition of the standard employment relationship), Article 43.2 (illegal worker transfer), Article 64.4.d (right to algorithmic transparency), and the Twenty-Third Additional Provision.
- **Royal Decree-Law 9/2021 & Law 12/2021 (Rider Law)**: Establishment of the legal employment presumption for digital platform delivery workers.
- **Organic Law 14/2022, of 22 December**: Reform of Article 311.2 of the Criminal Code to criminalize the imposition of illegal working conditions through non-employment schemes.
- **Directive (EU) 2024/2831 of the European Parliament and of the Council**: EU-wide harmonization on improving working conditions on digital platforms.

## Background

The emergence of digital delivery platforms such as Glovo, Deliveroo, and Uber Eats gave rise to legal controversy over the use of "false self-employment" schemes. Companies claimed to act as pure intermediaries, even though in practice they controlled work timing, rates, and allocation through geolocation algorithms.

## Objectives

1\. Guarantee a legal presumption of employment for digital delivery platform workers. 2. Prevent violations of individual and collective labor rights. 3. Ensure transparency of the algorithmic parameters determining working conditions. 4. Combat repeated labor fraud through criminal prosecution under Article 311.2 of the Criminal Code. 5. Begin harmonizing digital platform work standards across the European Union.

## Scope

The study covers analysis across four main pillars: 1. *Jurisprudential Evolution*: Contradictory TSJ rulings leading to the Supreme Court Plenary's resolution (STS 4746/2019). 2. *Rider Law & Algorithmic Governance*: The addition of the 23rd Additional Provision and Article 64.4.d to the Workers' Statute. 3. *Criminal Prosecution & the Glovo Case*: €205.3 million in ITSS sanctions, the reform of Article 311.2 of the Criminal Code, and criminal charges against CEO Oscar Pierre. 4. *European Perspective*: Comparative analysis of the Rider Law against Directive (EU) 2024/2831.

## Key Provisions

- **Employment Presumption (23rd Additional Provision, ET)**: Delivery activity via digital platforms is classified as a paid standard employment relationship.
- **Algorithmic Transparency (Art. 64.4.d ET)**: Companies must disclose algorithmic rules to worker representatives.
- **Criminal Sanctions (Art. 311.2 of the Criminal Code)**: Prison sentences of 6 months to 6 years for imposing illegal working conditions through non-employment schemes.
- **Human Oversight (EU Directive 2024/2831)**: Mandatory human evaluation and oversight of automated platform algorithmic decisions.

## Implementation

Implementation occurs through ITSS oversight, collective bargaining agreements (Just Eat), criminal prosecution by national courts, and adjustments to Glovo's operating model.

## Policy Problems

- The emergence of new labor-engineering schemes, such as logistics subcontracting and temporary staffing agencies, raising allegations of illegal worker transfer.
- The inability of administrative fines alone to halt labor fraud absent the threat of criminal prosecution.
- Challenges in monitoring potential algorithmic discrimination based on automated profiling.

## Reform

- Enactment of Law 12/2021 (Rider Law).
- Reform of Article 311.2 of the Criminal Code via Organic Law 14/2022.
- Adoption of Directive (EU) 2024/2831.

## Relationships with Other Entities

- **Implements**: Law 12/2021 & Organic Law 14/2022 (Art. 311.2 of the Criminal Code).
- **Applies To**: Digital delivery platforms (Glovo, Uber Eats, Just Eat, Deliveroo).
- **Conflicts With**: The use of "false self-employment" schemes on digital platforms.
