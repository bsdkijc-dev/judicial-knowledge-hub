## 3. POLICY ANALYSIS

- **Background**: Proclaimed at the Nice European Council on 7 December 2000, the Charter gained binding primary legal status equal to the Treaties upon the entry into force of the Lisbon Treaty in December 2009 via Article 6(1) TEU. Prior to the Charter becoming binding, fundamental rights in the EU were protected mainly as unwritten general principles derived from CJEU case law and constitutional traditions common to Member States.
- **Problem**: The Charter lacks explicit statutory canons of interpretation within its text, sharing this silence with EU primary Treaties and secondary legislation. The CJEU generally refrains from explicitly codifying its interpretative methods to avoid being restricted by rigid schemes.
- **Legal/Policy Gap**: Ambiguity surrounding how traditional interpretative criteria—textual, systemic/comparative, teleological (effet utile), and subjective—apply to a sui generis legal instrument incorporated into primary law by reference rather than negotiated line-by-line in an intergovernmental conference.
- **Objectives**: Maintain coherence across the EU legal order, ensure the practical effectiveness (effet utile) of fundamental rights, provide dynamic adaptability for living legal instruments, and prevent restrictions on individual protection.
- **Scope**: Applies to the CJEU and national courts whenever EU institutions adopt secondary acts or when Member States act within the scope of EU law pursuant to Article 51(1) CFREU.
- **Implementation**: Enforced through preliminary references under Article 267 TFEU, applying Article 52 CFREU guidelines (para 2 for Treaty conditions, para 3 for ECHR alignment, para 4 for common constitutional traditions) and official Charter Explanations.
- **Evidence**: Case-law analysis of key rulings including AMS (Case C-176/12), Bauer (Joined Cases C-569/16 & C-570/16), Garlsson Real Estate (Case C-537/16), Di Puma e Zecca (Joined Cases C-596/16 & C-597/16), Max-Planck (Case C-684/16), CCOO (Case C-55/18), Vernaza Ayovi (Case C-96/17), and Kossowski (Case C-486/14).
- **Impact**: Established direct horizontal effect for self-executing provisions like Article 31(2) paid annual leave in Bauer, while denying direct horizontal effect to broad conditional principles like Article 27 worker information in AMS.
- **Reform**: Shifted CJEU practice from ad hoc reliance on general principles toward a text-grounded yet flexible interpretative model that uses international instruments (such as ILO conventions and ECHR case law) to expand worker safety and non-discrimination protections.
- **Unresolved Issues**:
  - Managing friction between dynamic fundamental rights expansion and the principle of attributed competences under EU Treaties.
  - The difficulty of subjective interpretation due to the Charter being drafted by an inter-institutional Convention rather than negotiated in an intergovernmental conference.
  - Ensuring consistent application of the distinction between directly effective "rights" and non-self-executing "principles" across horizontal private litigation.

## 5. MEDIAWIKI ARTICLE

**Judicial Interpretation Framework for EU Fundamental Rights** refers to the hermeneutic rules, principles, and judicial methods applied by the [Court of Justice of the European Union](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Court_of_Justice_of_the_European_Union) (CJEU) when interpreting the [Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Charter_of_Fundamental_Rights_of_the_European_Union) (CFREU).[^1]

### Lead

Since the entry into force of the Lisbon Treaty in 2009, the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union holds primary legal value equal to the EU Treaties under [Article 6(1) TEU](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Article_6%281%29_TEU).[^2] Research by legal scholars, including [Giulia D'Agnone](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Giulia_D%27Agnone) of the [University of Campania "Luigi Vanvitelli"](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/University_of_Campania_%22Luigi_Vanvitelli%22), analyzes how the CJEU navigates the absence of explicit statutory interpretive canons by applying textual, systemic/comparative, and teleological (*effet utile*) methods.[^3] This framework balances legal certainty with dynamic protection for fundamental rights as living legal instruments.[^4]

### Summary

The CJEU generally avoids binding itself to a rigid, predetermined scheme of interpretation.[^5] Because the Charter is a *sui generis* instrument incorporated by reference rather than negotiated line-by-line in an intergovernmental conference, the Court uses dynamic techniques to ensure rights remain effective in practice.[^6]

### Legal Basis

The interpretative framework operates under:

- **[Article 6(1) TEU](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Article_6%281%29_TEU)**: Attributing binding Treaty-level legal value to the Charter.[^7]
- **[Article 52 CFREU](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Article_52_CFREU)**: Defining scope and interpretation, including alignment with the [European Convention on Human Rights](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights) (para 3) and constitutional traditions common to Member States (para 4).[^8]
- **[Article 53 CFREU](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Article_53_CFREU)**: Establishing minimum levels of protection.[^9]
- **[Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Vienna_Convention_on_the_Law_of_Treaties) (1969)**: Codifying standard international rules of treaty interpretation.[^10]

### Background

Prior to 2009, fundamental rights were protected in the EU primarily as unwritten general principles derived from CJEU case-law and common constitutional traditions.[^11] Proclaimed at Nice in 2000, the Charter provided a written catalogue, leading to an increasing reliance on codified textual provisions alongside established systemic principles.[^12]

### Objectives

- Ensure the practical effectiveness (*effet utile*) of individual rights guaranteed by EU law.[^13]
- Maintain coherence across the EU legal order and national legal systems.[^14]
- Provide dynamic adaptability to evolving social and economic conditions.[^15]

### Scope

Applies to all EU institutions adopting secondary legislation and to EU Member States whenever they implement or act within the scope of EU law pursuant to [Article 51(1) CFREU](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/Article_51%281%29_CFREU).[^16]

### Key Provisions & Canons

1.  **Textual Interpretation**: Evaluates specific wording (e.g., distinguishing self-executing rights under Article 31(2) in *Bauer* from non-self-executing principles under Article 27 in *AMS*).[^17]
2.  **Systemic & Comparative Interpretation**: Contextualizes Charter articles using international instruments, such as [ILO](https://judicialknowledge.miraheze.org/wiki/ILO) conventions and ECHR case-law.[^18]
3.  **Teleological Interpretation (*Effet Utile*)**: Focuses on achieving the core objective of the provision (e.g., requiring mandatory working time recording systems in *CCOO*).[^19]
4.  **Subjective Interpretation**: References historical intentions of drafters, though largely set aside in post-Lisbon CJEU jurisprudence due to the Charter's unique drafting process.[^20]

### Implementation

Enforced through preliminary reference rulings under Article 267 TFEU, establishing binding precedent for national courts across all Member States.[^21]

### Policy Problems

- **Textual Vagueness**: Abstract formulations in Title IV (Solidarity) require secondary legislation to clarify justiciability.[^22]
- **Horizontal Direct Effect Uncertainty**: Borderline distinctions between "rights" that create private obligations and "principles" that require legislative implementation.[^23]

### Reform

Scholars emphasize maintaining interpretive flexibility to preserve the Charter as a living legal instrument without overstepping the principle of attributed competences under EU Treaties.[^24]
