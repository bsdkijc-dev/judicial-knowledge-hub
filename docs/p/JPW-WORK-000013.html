4. POLICY ANALYSIS

Background: [FACT] Telework in Spain transitioned massively from a minority option to a strategic mode of work following the COVID-19 pandemic. This shift brought major legal and doctrinal challenges requiring resolution by the courts to establish the boundaries of workers' and employers' rights and obligations.

Policy Problem: [FACT]

  1. Clarity on Costs and Equipment: Widespread use of adhesion contract clauses by employers attempting to avoid telework expense-compensation obligations, or claiming that costs are offset by workers' personal savings.
  2. Technical Incidents and Power Cuts: Uncertainty over whether time lost to power cuts or internet connectivity issues qualifies as effective working time.
  3. Incompatibility of Disciplinary Dismissal: The absence of a specific classification for digital infractions (such as repeated/unilateral disconnection), forcing employers to rely on an analogy to Article 54.2.d ET ("breach of contractual good faith"), which risks creating an impossible burden of proof (probatio diabólica).
  4. Unilateral Modification of On-Site Presence: The practice of employers unilaterally changing telework percentages and on-site schedules without written consent.
  5. Monitoring and Privacy: The potential for privacy rights violations (Art. 20 bis ET, Art. 18.1 LTD) through the use of digital monitoring tools without transparency.
  6. Territorial Jurisdiction Disputes: Difficulty determining court jurisdiction when work is performed 100% from home, with no physical on-site location.

Legal/Policy Gap: [FACT] The absence of a specific "digital dismissal" regime under Article 54 ET adapted to the Fourth Industrial Revolution era; and the lack of rules integrating psychosocial risk and digital fatigue into the Occupational Risk Prevention Law (Law 31/1995).

Objectives: [FACT] Guarantee protection of digital workers' fundamental rights (privacy, guarantee of indemnity, digital disconnection); ensure compensation for operational costs; limit employer arbitrariness in adhesion clauses; and uphold the principle of equal treatment between teleworkers and on-site workers.

Implementation: [FACT]

  • Positive Legislation: Enactment of Law 10/2021; amendment of Art. 34.8 ET via RDL 5/2023 to strengthen the right to working-hours adaptation for family reconciliation; adoption of the 4th Additional Provision of Organic Law 1/2025 for telework in the Judicial Public Service; and Organic Law 5/2024 (Art. 12.3 & 3rd Additional Provision) on indemnity protection.
  • Jurisprudential Enforcement:
    • STSJ Madrid, 10 May 2023: Confirmed compatibility between receiving total permanent incapacity benefits and performing telework, based on residual work-capacity criteria and reasonable adjustments.
    • SAN 10 May 2021 & STS 19 September 2023: Power cuts outside a worker's control count fully as effective working time, and cannot be deducted from salary or require makeup hours.
    • SAN 22 March 2022 & SAN 10 November 2022: Annulled adhesion clauses claiming costs were offset by personal savings, and confirmed the nullity of unilateral changes to on-site presence percentages (Art. 8 LTD).
    • STS 11 January 2024: Rejected the obligation to pay transport allowances to teleworkers, as no physical commuting costs are incurred.
    • SAN 6 May 2024: Employers may unilaterally establish internal digital disconnection policy after prior consultation with worker representatives, while implementation details are left to collective agreement.
    • STSJ Madrid 30 November 2023 & STS 1995/2025: For 100% telework, territorial jurisdiction is determined by the worker's reference domicile as stated in the employment contract.

Evidence & Impact: [FACT] Approximately 1.64 million Spanish workers telework routinely, and 1.52 million partially (roughly 3.16 million total). Jurisprudential enforcement has successfully struck down various abusive employer clauses and affirmed equal treatment.

Reform & Unresolved Issues: [FACT] The de lege ferenda need to establish a specific "digital dismissal" regulation outside the rigid framework of Article 54 ET; alignment with the Proposed Directive (EU) 2023/2378; and the need for collective agreements to address psychosocial risk protection arising from "digital presenteeism."

  5. Scope: What are the main legal issues resolved by Spanish courts regarding telework in this study?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Compatibility with permanent incapacity, working-hours adaptation (Art. 34.8 ET), dismissal due to disconnection, expense compensation, power cuts, transport allowances, working/overtime hour registration, territorial court jurisdiction, and internal digital disconnection policy.
  6. Policy Implementation: How did the Supreme Court (STS 19 September 2023) and the Audiencia Nacional (SAN 10 May 2021) treat power-cut incidents during telework?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Power cuts outside the worker's control count fully as effective working time, meaning workers need not make up lost hours and cannot have their pay deducted.
  7. Policy Problem: Why was a clause claiming that telework costs were offset by workers' personal savings declared null and void by the Audiencia Nacional (SAN 10 November 2022)?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Because it contravened Article 7.b of Law 10/2021, which requires employers to pay/compensate all operational costs arising from telework, without the possibility of waiving this via an adhesion clause.
  8. Legal Relationship: What is the relationship between Organic Law 3/2018 (LOPD) and employer authority to set digital disconnection policy, per SAN 6 May 2024?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Articles 87–88 of the LOPD and Article 18.1 of Law 10/2021 grant employers unilateral authority to establish internal digital disconnection policy after prior consultation with worker representatives, while implementation details are left to collective agreement.
  9. Reform: What reform did Royal Decree-Law 5/2023 introduce to Article 34.8 of the Workers' Statute (ET)?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] It expanded workers' right to request adaptation of working hours and work mode (including telework) for family reconciliation until a child reaches age 12, and required employers to provide an objective justification for any refusal.
  10. Evidence/Case Study: How many teleworkers are there in Spain, according to EPA/INE and Infojobs data cited in the study?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Approximately 1,641,000 people telework exclusively/routinely, and 1,520,600 people (7.6%) telework occasionally (roughly 3.16 million total).

6. MEDIAWIKI OUTPUT

Summary

Critical Analysis of the Issues Resolved by the Courts on Telework is a labor law study written by José Iván Pérez López of Universidad de Málaga (UMA). This scholarly work presents a comprehensive evaluation of Spanish labor courts' diagnosis of telework following the enactment of Law 10/2021 on Remote Work. The study evaluates key issues such as telework's compatibility with total permanent incapacity, working-hours adaptation for family reconciliation (Art. 34.8 ET), criteria for disciplinary dismissal due to repeated disconnection, the legal status of power cuts as effective working time (Art. 30 ET), elimination of transport allowances, registration of working and overtime hours, territorial court jurisdiction, and the nullity of adhesion clauses shifting operational costs onto workers.

Legal Basis

  • Law 10/2021, of 9 July, on Remote Work (LTD): The main regulatory framework for remote work (Arts. 2, 7, 8, 11, 12, 13, 18.1).
  • Royal Legislative Decree 2/2015 (Workers' Statute — ET): Articles 1.1, 4.1, 4.2, 5.a, 20.2, 20 bis (privacy), 30 (work impediments), 34.8 (working-hours adaptation), 34.9 (hours registration), 35, 53.4.b, and 54.2.d (grounds for disciplinary dismissal).
  • Royal Decree-Law 5/2023, of 28 June: Reform of Art. 34.8 ET to align with Directive (EU) 2019/1158 on family-life reconciliation.
  • Organic Law 3/2018, of 5 December (LOPDGDD): Articles 87 and 88 on the right to digital disconnection in the workplace.
  • Organic Law 1/2025, of 2 January: 4th Additional Provision on telework regulation in the Judicial Public Service.
  • Organic Law 5/2024, of 11 November: Article 12.3 and 3rd Additional Provision on worker indemnity guarantees.
  • EU Directives: Directive 2003/88/EC (working time regulation) and Proposed Directive (EU) 2023/2378.

Background

Telework rapidly transitioned from an emergency pandemic measure to a permanent work modality for over 3.16 million workers in Spain. This transition triggered a wave of litigation in the Labor Courts, as the traditional norms of the Workers' Statute had not fully accommodated the dynamics of digital work.

Objectives

1. Provide a precise judicial diagnosis of telework disputes following Law 10/2021. 2. Ensure employer compensation for operational costs and work equipment. 3. Affirm the protection of effective working time during technical disruptions beyond the worker's control. 4. Facilitate the right to working-hours adaptation for family-life reconciliation. 5. Promote the formulation of a specific digital dismissal regime.

Scope

The study covers eleven main judicial pillars: 1. Permanent Incapacity: Compatibility of benefits with telework via reasonable adjustments. 2. Working-Hours Adaptation: Application of Art. 34.8 ET and the employer's duty of objective justification. 3. Disconnection and Dismissal: Culpability criteria for repeated disconnection (Art. 54.2.d ET). 4. Costs and Adhesion: Nullity of clauses shifting cost burdens to workers. 5. Power Cuts: Protection under Art. 30 ET for technical incidents at home. 6. Transport Allowance: Rejection of claims for commuting costs that did not occur. 7. Overtime Hours: Credibility and evidence in hours registration. 8. Territorial Jurisdiction: Reference domicile for 100% telework. 9. Internal Policy: Employer authority to establish digital disconnection policy. 10. Unilateral Modification: Annulment of unilateral changes to on-site presence. 11. Rest and Breaks: Distinction between physiological breaks and rest time.

Key Provisions

  • Effective Working Time (Art. 30 ET): Technical disruptions such as power cuts count fully as effective working time and cannot be deducted from pay.
  • Absolute Cost Compensation (Art. 7.b LTD): Employers must bear telework costs; adhesion clauses claiming offset via personal savings are null and void.
  • Working-Hours Adaptation (Art. 34.8 ET): Employers may not reject telework adaptation requests on formalistic or generic grounds.
  • Digital Disconnection Policy: Employers may unilaterally establish internal policy after prior consultation with worker representatives.
  • Territorial Jurisdiction: For 100% telework disputes, jurisdiction lies with the Labor Court of the worker's reference domicile.

Implementation

Implementation occurs through enforcement of Supreme Court, Audiencia Nacional, and Superior Court jurisprudence, alongside adjustment of internal institutional and company regulations.

Policy Problems

  • The absence of a specific classification for "digital dismissal" under Article 54 ET, creating an impossible burden of proof.
  • A gap in oversight of psychosocial risk and digital fatigue within occupational risk prevention law.
  • The risk of "digital presenteeism" for workers who are ill while working from home.

Reform

  • Implementation of Law 10/2021 on Remote Work.
  • Reform of Art. 34.8 ET via Royal Decree-Law 5/2023.
  • Implementation of Organic Law 1/2025 and Organic Law 5/2024.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • Implements: Law 10/2021, the Workers' Statute, and Organic Law 3/2018 (LOPD).
  • Applies To: All workers and employers engaged in telework in Spain.
  • Conflicts With: Abusive adhesion contract clauses shifting operational costs to workers.
