Definition

An official judicial policy research publication issued by the Judicial Policy Research Institute (JPRI) of the Supreme Court of Korea that comprehensively examines the concept, technological architecture, and legal nature of blockchain-based smart contracts; evaluates civil law doctrines, consumer protection, personal data privacy, and cross-border jurisdiction; analyzes comparative international legislative models (US, EU, Belarus, Portugal, Italy) and standardization frameworks (ISO/TC 307); and formulates concrete statutory amendments alongside a standalone draft Smart Contract Framework Act (스마트계약 기본법률안).

4. Policy Analysis

Background
Interest in blockchain-based smart contracts has grown significantly across finance (DeFi), real estate, logistics, insurance, voting, healthcare, and IoT. While proponents claim smart contracts represent a game-changer signaling "the end of classic contract law," critics point to software bugs, immutability risks, hacking, and code rigidity.

Policy Problem
Linguistic & Semantic Limits: Smart contracts operate via rigid IF-THEN conditional logic in binary code. They cannot encode qualitative, discretionary legal standards such as "good faith," "duty of care of a good manager," "reasonable period," or "force majeure."

Immutability vs. Legal Remedies: Blockchain immutability prevents retroactive cancellation, rescission (해제/해지), or modification of executed contracts when legal defects (mistake, fraud, duress, or incapacity) occur.

Privacy Conflicts: Blockchain transparency and permanent distributed recording directly conflict with Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) mandates regarding data destruction, correction/erasure rights (잊혀질 권리), and cross-border transfer restrictions.

Lack of Statutory Recognition: Korea's positive law lacks explicit definitions or legal efficacy provisions for smart contracts, creating jurisdictional and enforcement uncertainty.

Legal/Policy Gap
Code vs. Law: The report refutes Lawrence Lessig's extreme "Code is Law" proposition, establishing that code must operate within positive legal frameworks ("Code has to obey the Law").

Legal Nature: Smart contracts are legally recognized as special real-execution contracts (의사실현계약) formed when an off-chain offer (code publication) meets a matching acceptance (condition fulfillment).

Objectives
Establish statutory recognition for smart contracts, harmonize automated execution with civil remedies, protect consumers from automated fraud/mistakes, resolve privacy conflicts, and promote technical standardization.

Implementation & Proposed Reforms
Amendment to Framework Act on Electronic Documents and Transactions: Insert Paragraph 4 into Article 6 (제6조 제4항), declaring that for smart contracts, the time of sending and receiving an electronic document is presumed to be the time it is recorded on the blockchain.

Enactment of Smart Contract Framework Act (스마트계약 기본법률안): Introduce a 5-article standalone statute establishing:

  1. Article 1 (Purpose): Regulate basic smart contract matters and promote related industries.
  2. Article 2 (Definitions): Define "Smart Contract" as a program operating on a distributed, decentralized, shared, and replicated ledger that enables asset acquisition/transfer upon condition fulfillment; define "Blockchain Technology."
  3. Article 3 (Relationship to Other Laws): Mutatis mutandis application of Civil Act Part III Chapter 2 (Contracts).
  4. Article 4 (Legal Efficacy): Declare that a smart contract shall not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because it is in smart contract form or contains smart contract elements.
  5. Article 5 (Standardization): Authorize the Minister of Science and ICT to promote standardization research and project funding.

Technical & Contractual Safeguards: Integrate internal cancellation functions (selfdestruct in Solidity, satellite contracts, or reverse transactions), multi-signature oracles, and emergency "kill switches" (as seen in EU Data Act Art. 30).

Privacy Guidelines: Issue administrative data protection guidelines (modeled on Spain's AEPD 2022 framework) requiring privacy impact assessments (GDPR Art. 35) and off-chain personal data storage.

Evidence & Case Studies
Singapore SICC Case (B2C2 v Quoine [2020] SGHC(I) 3): Analyzed algorithmic currency trades where a platform error triggered a 250x price anomaly; court ruled that unilateral mistake in automated execution must be evaluated based on the programmer's mental state at the time of coding.

Global Legislation: Reviewed US state statutes (Wyoming Wyo. Stat. § 34-29-103, Arizona Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 44-7061, Illinois 205 ILCS 730), Belarus Decree No. 8 (2017), Portugal E-Commerce Law No. 7/2004, and Italy Law No. 135/2018.

7 Policy Problem Why can't traditional legal doctrines like "good faith" or "reasonable period" be directly coded into smart contracts? It established that when evaluating unilateral mistakes in automated smart contract execution, the court must assess the state of mind and intent of the programmer at the time the code was written.

6. MediaWiki Output

A Study on the Legal Issues of Smart Contracts is an official judicial policy research report published by the Judicial Policy Research Institute (JPRI) of South Korea's Supreme Court.

Summary

This document is an official judicial policy research report published by the Judicial Policy Research Institute (JPRI) of South Korea's Supreme Court. It analyzes the concept, technical structure, and legal nature of blockchain-based smart contracts. It evaluates civil law, consumer protection, and personal data protection, and formulates draft amendments to the Electronic Documents Act along with a proposed Smart Contract Framework Act (스마트계약 기본법률안).

Legal Basis

The legal frameworks analyzed include:

  • Civil Act of the Republic of Korea (민법): Article 3 (Capacity of Rights), Articles 103, 104, 107–110 (Defects in Intention & Mistake), Article 111 (Effectiveness of Declaration of Intention), Articles 140 & 141 (Cancellation), Article 527 (Binding Force of Offer), and Article 563 (Sales Contract).
  • Framework Act on Electronic Documents and Transactions (전자문서 및 전자거래 기본법): Article 2, Article 4(1), Article 5, Article 6 (Time of Sending/Receiving), and Article 17.
  • Electronic Signature Act (전자서명법): Article 2 and Article 3.
  • Act on Consumer Protection in Electronic Commerce (전자상거래법): Articles 2, 10, 13, 17, 20.
  • Personal Information Protection Act (개인정보보호법): Articles 2, 15–18, 21, 31, 36, 37, 39-12.

Background

The use of smart contracts is expanding across finance (DeFi), real estate, logistics, insurance, and IoT. However, characteristics such as transparency, code rigidity, immutability, and the risk of bugs/hacking create legal uncertainty requiring positive regulation.

Objectives

  1. Clarify the legal nature of smart contracts as contracts formed by real execution (의사실현계약).
  2. Reconcile code automation with civil rescission/termination rights and consumer protection.
  3. Draft a Smart Contract Framework Act to provide legal certainty and alignment with international standards (ISO/TC 307).

Scope

  • Comparative Legal Analysis: US law (Wyoming, Arizona, Illinois Blockchain Technology Act), Belarus (Decree No. 8), Portugal, Italy (Law 135/2018), the EU Data Act, and Spain's AEPD guidelines.
  • Case Law Analysis: The Singapore International Commercial Court (SICC) ruling in B2C2 Ltd v Quoine Pte Ltd [2020] SGHC(I) 3.

Key Provisions

  • Legal Nature: Smart contracts are recognized as a special form of contract by real execution (의사실현계약), where the offer is made through code publication and acceptance occurs upon condition fulfillment.
  • Rejection of the "Code is Law" Doctrine: Code must be subject to positive law ("Code has to obey the Law").
  • Amendment to the Electronic Documents Act: Addition of Article 6 Paragraph 4, providing that the time of sending/receiving an electronic smart contract document is the time it is recorded on the blockchain.

Implementation

The government and judiciary recommend using technical functions such as selfdestruct in Solidity, satellite contracts, verified oracles, and emergency kill switches to lawfully execute contract cancellation or termination.

Policy Issues

  • Conflict between blockchain immutability and the obligation to erase personal data (잊혀질 권리) under the Personal Information Protection Act.
  • The inability of programming languages (such as Solidity) to encode qualitative legal norms such as "good faith" or "valid reason."

Reform / Amendments

  • Amendment of Article 6 of the Framework Act on Electronic Documents and Transactions.
  • Enactment of a Smart Contract Framework Act (스마트계약 기본법률안 — 5 articles).
  • Development of Personal Information Protection Guidelines for smart contracts by the government.

Relationship to Other Entities

  • Publisher: Judicial Policy Research Institute & Supreme Court of Korea.
  • Lead Researcher: Kim Jung-hwan.
  • Related Ministries: Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT) & Personal Information Protection Commission.
