The consequences of repeatedly losing on legitimacy beliefs is an empirical comparative political science and public policy research paper authored by Anna Kern (Ghent University & KU Leuven) and Ann-Kristin Kölln (University of Gothenburg), published in the European Journal of Political Research (EJPR) in 2022.

Summary

This study examines the causal effect of repeated electoral defeat on citizens' beliefs in political legitimacy. Using six-wave panel data from the Dutch LISS panel (2007–2012), covering three consecutive national elections (2006, 2010, and 2012), the study tests whether repeated losses reduce satisfaction with democracy and political trust. The findings show that repeated defeat never reduces satisfaction with democracy, but does causally reduce political trust by 2.1% to 7.1% in representative institutions [7, 53–55, 73–74].

  • Dutch Proportional Electoral System: The study is set within an open-list proportional representation system with no electoral threshold and multi-party coalition governance.

Background

Democratic stability depends on "losers' consent" — the willingness of voters whose parties lost to accept the election results and comply with laws made by the new government [2, 9–11]. Classic theory (Lijphart 1999) assumes that long-term political exclusion from repeated defeat erodes system legitimacy. However, earlier studies suffered from two key weaknesses: they tested single legitimacy indicators in isolation and relied on cross-sectional data that could not establish causal relationships [4, 18–19].

Objectives

1. Test the hypothesis that, compared to one-time losers, repeated losers hold lower levels of legitimacy beliefs. 2. Compare the effect of repeated defeat on two legitimacy indicators: Satisfaction with Democracy (retrospective performance) versus Political Trust (prospective institutional faith) [34, 76–77]. 3. Identify the causal effect of repeated defeat using Difference-in-Differences regression with fixed effects.

Scope

The research analyzes longitudinal panel data from 5,394 to 6,811 Dutch respondents (LISS Panel 2007–2012), evaluated across 16 separate test specifications combining different election periods, defeat operationalizations, and legitimacy indicators.

Key Provisions / Findings

  • Resilience of Satisfaction with Democracy (SWD): Across all 16 model specifications, repeated defeat never had a statistically significant negative effect on satisfaction with democracy [7, 45–46, 53, 55, 74]. Repeat losers were no less satisfied than one-time losers.
  • Causal Decline in Political Trust: An additional electoral loss causally and significantly reduced political trust in representative institutions by 2.1% to 7.1% (p < 0.05) [7, 53–55, 74].
  • Trust Index Decomposition: The decline in trust occurred consistently across all representative institutions: government, parliament, politicians, and political parties.
  • Two Theoretical Dimensions of Legitimacy:
    • Satisfaction with Democracy functions as a retrospective performance evaluation ("how things went in the past"), which remains relatively stable.
    • Political Trust functions as a prospective belief ("certainty about future institutional behavior"). Repeated exclusion breeds skepticism about the fairness and impartiality of political institutions [17, 77–78].

Implementation

The methodology was implemented through First Difference and Difference-in-Differences regressions with respondent-level and period-level fixed effects, along with placebo parallel-trend testing on pre-election periods (2008–2009, 2010–2011) [39, 54, 68–71]. Data were provided by CentERdata (Tilburg University), with funding support from FWO (Grant No. 40370), AUFF (Grant No. AUFF-E2017-7-13), and Riksbankens Jubileumsfond (Grant No. RIK18-1267:2).

Policy Issues

1. The risk of eroding public trust in representative institutions due to repeated political exclusion. 2. The mistaken practice of using "satisfaction with democracy" and "political trust" interchangeably in public policy evaluation.

Reform / Implications

1. Designing electoral institutions and consensus governance arrangements that expand power-sharing to minimize the full exclusion of minority voters [8, 22–23]. 2. Separating the measurement of retrospective performance from prospective trust in public opinion surveys and legitimacy research [7, 76–77].
