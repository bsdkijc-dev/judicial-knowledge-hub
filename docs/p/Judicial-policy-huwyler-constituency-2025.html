Constituency references in social media: MPs' usage and voters' reaction is an empirical comparative political science research paper authored by Oliver Huwyler (University of Vienna), Nathalie Giger (University of Geneva), Stefanie Bailer (University of Basel), Tomas Turner-Zwinkels (Tilburg University), and Silvan Heller (University of Basel), published in the European Journal of Political Research (EJPR) in 2025.

Summary

This study examines Members of Parliament's (MPs) use of constituency references ("local cues") on social media (Twitter/X) and their effect on voters' decisions across seven European countries. Through three stages of analysis — an analysis of 1.3 million Tweets from German and Swiss MPs (2009–2019), a survey experiment with 16,597 respondents, and an analysis of preference votes in Swiss National Council elections (2011–2019) — the study finds a disconnect between politicians' expectations and voters' responses: although MPs significantly increase local references during campaign season, voters do not provide any electoral reward for these references.

Background

Constituency representation is a core duty of parliamentarians alongside legislative work. Social media provides MPs with a direct, unfiltered channel to demonstrate local engagement ("behavioral localism"). Politicians assume that conveying local references will increase voter trust and yield electoral benefits [2, 7, 16–17].

Objectives

Test whether parliamentarians strategically increase local references on social media during campaign season (Hypothesis 1).

Test whether voters provide an electoral reward for local references on social media (Hypothesis 2).

Analyze the effect of local references on intra-party and inter-party (panachage) preference votes in open-list election systems [28–29, 69–71].

Scope

This comparative research covers seven European countries (Germany, Switzerland, United Kingdom, France, Netherlands, Belgium, and Poland), representing variation in electoral systems (single-member plurality, closed-list proportional representation, mixed-member systems, and open-list proportional representation).

Key Provisions / Findings

Campaign Season Effect (Hypothesis 1 Supported): MPs significantly increase local references in the three months before an election. The probability that a Tweet contains a local cue rises by a factor of 1.75 (from ~7% outside campaign season to ~12% during campaign season, p < 0.001) across all electoral contexts.

Rejection of Hypothesis 2 (True Null Electoral Finding): ** Survey Experiment: Local cues successfully increased perceived candidate "localness" (p < 0.001), but had no positive effect on self-reported vote likelihood [2, 7, 65–66]. ** Swiss Elections: The number of local Tweets sent during campaign season had no statistically significant relationship with intra-party preference votes (b = -21.99, p > 0.05) or inter-party panachage votes (b = 30.05, p > 0.05) [71–72, 76].

Automated Geospatial Dictionaries: The study used German GN250 data (120,000 locations) and Swiss swissNAMES3D data (380,000 locations) to automatically detect place names, landmarks, and facilities within Tweets [31–32].

Implementation

Methodology was implemented through multi-level binomial regression models, MP fixed-effects models, a weighted-quota online survey experiment, and candidate-level panel regression analysis for Swiss elections. Replication code and data are published openly on GitHub (https://github.com/TomasZwinkels/ProjectR036_localcues).

Policy Issues

A mismatch between politicians' campaign-strategy assumptions and voters' actual perceptions.

Voters' tendency to disregard local social media signals, treating them as rhetoric unsupported by tangible work, or prioritizing party ideology instead.

Reform / Implications

The study demonstrates that localism signals on social media are insufficient to change voter decisions, suggesting that politicians should reconsider how they allocate digital campaign resources.
