Budgeting in the Era of Judicial Independence is an academic policy study and empirical economic analysis authored by Jesper Wittrup. The study tests the relationship between perceived judicial independence and national economic growth across 95 countries (1980–2003), and evaluates the two main models of judicial budgeting in Europe: the traditional Ministry of Justice model and the independent Judicial Council model.

Summary

This study empirically demonstrates that public and business perception of judicial independence has a strong, significant positive effect on national economic growth (R² = 0.411, p < 0.01). However, formal (*de jure*) indicators show no correlation with economic growth. Wittrup analyzes the budget governance challenges of the Ministry of Justice model (risk of accusations of political manipulation or budget freezing) and the Judicial Council model (risk of accusations of internal favoritism and lack of public accountability). As a solution, Wittrup recommends the use of objective, transparent statistical workload indicators.

Background & the Fundamental Political Dilemma

The economic perspective of North and Weingast identifies a "fundamental political dilemma": a government strong enough to protect citizens' property rights is also strong enough to confiscate their wealth. Judicial independence functions as a neutral third-party enforcer that guarantees government commitment, thereby encouraging investment and economic growth.

Empirical Research Findings & Budgeting Models

1. Economic Growth Regression (1980–2003)

  • Uses data from the Penn World Table v6.2 and the World Economic Forum's 2006 survey of 11,000+ business executives across 95 countries.
  • Adding the perceived-judicial-independence variable increases the model's explanatory power from R² = 0.267 to R² = 0.411.
  • The Feld & Voigt *de jure* index shows that merely copying formal rules on paper, without real independence, does not generate economic growth.

2. Comparison of Judicial Budgeting Models in Europe

Budgeting Model Key Characteristics Governance Risk Countries Applying It (2006)
Ministry of Justice Model (MoJ Model) The Ministry of Justice manages and allocates court budgets. Rumors of political punishment/reward (e.g., the Tvind case in Denmark) or rigid budget allocation. England, Germany, Finland, France, Spain, Italy, Croatia, etc.
Judicial Council Model An independent council / courts administration manages the judicial budget. Risk of accusations of favoritism among judges and challenges to public accountability. Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Netherlands, Ireland, Hungary, etc.

Oversight & Accountability (Danish Model)

In Denmark, the transfer of budget authority to the *Domstolsstyrelsen* (1999) is balanced by oversight from the General Public Auditor. If serious financial criticism arises, the Minister of Justice and the Auditor agree on corrective instructions; failure to comply empowers the Minister of Justice to dismiss the entire independent Board of Governors.

Policy Issues

1. Loss of budget efficiency due to the Ministry of Justice's reluctance to reallocate funds between courts. 2. The risk of internal conflict among judges over budget priorities under the Judicial Council model. 3. The need to balance judicial independence with public financial accountability.

Reform / Implications

  • The development and implementation of transparent, objective statistical workload indicators in Denmark, Germany, Finland, Norway, and the Netherlands to provide a fair basis for budget allocation.
