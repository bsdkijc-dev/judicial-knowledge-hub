The Reiling ODR & Court Digitalization Policy Framework is a judicial technology policy framework and evaluation model for the integration of Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) into the justice system. Formulated by Dr. Dory Reiling (Senior Judge at the Amsterdam First Instance Court and former Product Owner of the Dutch Digital Civil Procedure) in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), Vol. 8 No. 2 (May 2017), the framework analyzes the eKantonrechter experiment (a Dutch digital small claims civil procedure based on Article 96 of the Code of Civil Procedure). It critically evaluates the transition from mere "court digitalization" toward an integrated, user-centric ODR model based on the real-world needs of justice seekers [1–21].

Summary

Published by Dr. Dory Reiling in the IJCA (May 2017), this policy framework evaluates the lessons learned from the development of the eKantonrechter digital civil procedure in the Netherlands, launched in May 2014 [1–4, 10–11]. Designed to adjudicate small claims disputes (<€25,000), as well as employment and consumer matters based on Article 96 of the Dutch Code of Civil Procedure (Rv), the procedure utilized DigiD and eHerkenning authentication alongside a structured form interface [4, 7–10]. However, evaluations revealed that the eKantonrechter experienced total user rejection (zero usage in 2016). Reiling exposes that this failure was rooted in the consensual requirement of Article 96 Rv, which necessitated both parties to submit the dispute jointly—a romantic academic assumption that contradicts the reality of litigation, where a claimant typically wants the court to assert their rights over an uncooperative defendant. Reiling argues that judicial ODR must shift from a technology-centric approach to a user-centric design that maps out citizens' actual "justice moments" and integrates ODR directly into the standard contentious court proceedings [13, 15–17].

Legal Basis

  • Wetboek van Burgerlijke Rechtsvordering (Rv) Article 96: A provision in the Dutch Code of Civil Procedure that serves as the basis for consensual small claims procedures without mandatory legal representation.
  • Technology for Justice Guidelines (Reiling, 2010): Guiding principles for judicial web accessibility based on an understanding of citizens' information needs.

Background

Court digitalization innovations frequently fail because they are designed around the internal assumptions of judges and technocrats rather than the real-world behaviors and needs of the public seeking to resolve legal problems.

Objectives

1. Transform judicial digitalization from the mere computerization of physical files into user-centric ODR integration. 2. Provide digital access to justice that is fair, fast, accessible, and sustainable. 3. Actively transfer the "ownership of the problem" back to the litigating parties.

Scope

This policy encompasses an evaluation of the eKantonrechter procedure in the Netherlands and provides a comparative analysis of international ODR systems (CyberSettle, DemanderJustice, Rechtwijzer, CRT British Columbia).

Key Provisions

  • Evaluation of eKantonrechter and Consensuality Failure:
    • The consensual digital civil procedure failed to attract users (zero usage in 2016) because claimants refused to register jointly with defendants, combined with high court fees and the lack of an appeal mechanism.
  • Paradigm Shift to User-Centric Design:
    • Judicial IT design must be rooted in mapping the users' "justice moments" rather than institutional technocratic assumptions.
  • Network/Cloud Problem-Solving Model:
    • Recognizing that citizens' dispute resolution pathways are non-linear and require various ODR instruments seamlessly integrated with formal court processes.

Implementation

  • Development of eKantonrechter (2014): The creation of a Digital Justice Kiosk utilizing DigiD/eHerkenning authentication with a mix of structured and unstructured questionnaire forms.
  • Integration of ODR and Formal Courts: Adopting resolutions from the ODR2016 conference in The Hague to fuse pre-trial ODR instruments into the official judicial litigation chain.

Policy Problems

  • Reluctance of Defendants to Participate: The inherent difficulty of inducing a defendant to voluntarily join an ODR process without legal pressure or incentives.
  • High Costs of Digital Litigation: Maintaining high official court fees serves as a disincentive for citizens considering formal digital ODR platforms.

Reform / Change

  • Abandoning the purely consensual ODR model in favor of integrating ODR into contentious (non-voluntary) court proceedings.
  • Expanding socio-legal research (e.g., Paths to Justice) to thoroughly understand the actual routes society takes to resolve disputes.
