The European and Italian Unjust Enrichment Comparative Restitution Framework refers to the judicial policy structure and comparative private law framework mapping the historical evolution, circulation of legal models, and structural divergence between French-derived civil law systems and Anglo-Germanic legal systems in the realm of restitution and unjustified enrichment (arricchimento senza causa) 1.

Overview

The rules governing restitution in Europe are the product of the historical circulation of three primary legal models: Roman-French, Roman-Germanic, and English Common Law 2. Formulated through comparative scholarship by Antonio Albanese, Associate Professor of Private Law at the University of Bologna, this framework divides European systems into two main operational models:

  1. Franco-Italian Model: Requires five cumulative elements: defendant enrichment, plaintiff damage/impoverishment, correlation between enrichment and loss, lack of legal cause (lack of cause), and strict subsidiarity 3.
  2. Anglo-Germanic Model: Focuses on three primary elements: defendant enrichment, gain obtained at the expense of/from the rights of the plaintiff (auf dessen Kosten), and lack of legal justification, without requiring direct out-of-pocket financial loss or strict subsidiarity 4.

Statutory and Legal Basis

The primary statutory and transnational instruments governing this framework include:

  • Italian Civil Code (1942): Article 2041 (general unjust enrichment clause) and Article 2042 (mandatory subsidiary character) 5.
  • Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (German BGB): Section 812 (general clause and Eingriffskondiktion) and Section 852 (restitution following the expiration of tort claims) 6.
  • Principles of European Unjustified Enrichment Law (PEUEL): Article 2:101 (justification requirement) and Article 7:102 (concurrent obligations) 7.

Historical Background

The historical circulation of European legal models demonstrates that the 1927 Italian-French Draft Code of Obligations (Art. 73) influenced codifications in Albania (1927), Romania (1934), and Greece (1940) 8. Conversely, the 1900 German BGB model (§ 812) influenced codes in Sweden, Switzerland, Portugal, Brazil, the Soviet Union, Hungary, Japan, and China 9. In France, the unjust enrichment action was first established through landmark jurisprudence in the arrêt Boudier case (1892) by the French Court of Cassation 10.

Objectives

  • Map the functional differentiation between performance-based enrichment actions (Leistungskondiktion) and interference-based enrichment actions (Eingriffskondiktion) 11.
  • Facilitate mechanisms for the disgorgement of illicit profits resulting from intellectual property infringement or unauthorized asset usage 12.
  • Advance the harmonization of European restitution law under a unified principle of legal justification 13.

Scope

The scope of this framework covers private contract and obligations law, tort liability, intellectual property rights, unauthorized asset exploitation, and comparative private law across European Union member states 14.

Key Provisions & Principles

  • Abstract Subsidiarity Rule (Art. 2042 Italian Civil Code): Precludes an unjust enrichment claim if the plaintiff abstractly had another action available to obtain compensation, even if that primary action has expired or failed on procedural grounds 15.
  • Profit Disgorgement via Eingriffskondiktion: Under BGB § 812 and German Federal Court of Justice (BGH) jurisprudence (such as the 1971 Flugreiseentscheidung case), gains derived from invading another party's protected legal sphere must be surrendered, even if the owner suffered zero out-of-pocket financial loss 16.
  • Compensation vs. Restitution Distinction: Tort compensation focuses on indemnifying the plaintiff's loss (neminem laedere), whereas unjust enrichment focuses on stripping the defendant's unmeritorious gain 17.

Implementation

The framework is implemented through statutory codification and judicial interpretation across key bodies: the Italian Supreme Court of Cassation (Corte di Cassazione), the German Federal Court of Justice (BGH), the French Court of Cassation (Cour de Cassation), and the UK House of Lords 18.

Policy Challenges

  • Rigidity of Italian Subsidiarity: The doctrine of "abstract subsidiarity" in Italy hinders the practical utility of Article 2041 and marginalizes unjustified enrichment claims compared to other European jurisdictions 19.
  • Failure to Disgorge Illicit Profits: Requiring proof of "patrimonial decrease" (out-of-pocket loss) allows bad-faith actors to retain profits earned through bad-faith rights exploitation whenever direct loss cannot be demonstrated 20.

Proposed Reforms

Legal scholars advocate reinterpreting the "patrimonial decrease" requirement to align with "at the expense of" (auf dessen Kosten), transitioning from abstract subsidiarity to "concrete subsidiarity," and permitting concurrent claims in tort and restitution 21.
