The European Judicial Information Technology Policy Framework: CCJE Opinion No. 14 (2011) on Justice and Information Technologies is a policy framework and IT governance guideline for the administration of justice in Europe. Formulated by the Consultative Council of European Judges (CCJE) of the Council of Europe, it aims to leverage information technology (IT) to enhance access to justice, case management, transparency, and efficiency. Concurrently, it ensures the absolute protection of the right to a fair trial, judicial independence, and procedural safeguards in accordance with Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) [1–3, 18–19, 26, 29].

Summary

Prepared with the support of Dr. Dory Reiling (Judge at the First Instance Court of Amsterdam) as a supporting expert and published in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework evaluates the status of IT adoption across 47 member states of the Council of Europe, drawing on longitudinal data from the European Commission for the Efficiency of Justice (CEPEJ) [1, 4, 5, 8–9]. The framework asserts that IT must facilitate access to justice and case management without diminishing procedural safeguards, replacing the decision-making role of the judge, or eroding judicial independence [3, 18–19, 29].

Legal Basis

  • Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR): Guarantees the right to a fair trial, access to a court, impartiality, judicial independence, and the resolution of cases within a reasonable time.
  • Magna Charta of Judges (CCJE 2010): Codifies the principles of judicial transparency and the obligation to publish information.
  • CCJE Opinions No. 6, 7, and 11: Provides guidelines on the dissemination of judicial information, transparency, and the quality of judicial decisions.
  • Council of the EU Decision on the European Case Law Identifier (ECLI): Establishes cross-border standards for numbering and accessing European jurisprudence.
  • ECtHR Videoconferencing Jurisprudence: Foundational judgments regarding remote proceedings, such as Marcello Viola v. Italy (2006) and Asciutto v. Italy (2007).

Background

The implementation of IT in European judiciaries has grown rapidly, evolving from basic office administration tools into strategic informational mechanisms. However, this rapid adoption has triggered concerns regarding the potential dehumanization of the judicial process, threats to the independence of individual judges, and the systemic marginalization of judges in national IT decision-making and governance.

Objectives

1. Promote the use of IT to expand access to justice, accountability, and case file workflow management. 2. Ensure that all judicial IT applications strictly comply with fair hearing safeguards under Article 6 of the ECHR [3, 9, 18–19]. 3. Guarantee that judicial IT governance decisions fall under the authority of the Council for the Judiciary or an equivalent independent body, with active judicial participation. 4. Develop open jurisprudence publications to strengthen the shadow function of courts.

Scope

This policy encompasses the 47 member states of the Council of Europe, which are categorized by CEPEJ into three tiers of IT adoption:

  • High Level (17 countries): Implementing IT for direct support, court management, and external communication (e.g., Austria, Finland, Estonia, France, Switzerland).
  • Medium Level (20 countries): Implementing IT for direct support and court management, but with limited external interaction (e.g., Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, Spain, Sweden, UK-England & Wales).
  • Low Level (8 countries): IT adoption remains in its nascent stages (e.g., Moldova, Greece, Serbia).

Key Provisions

  • Protection of the Judge's Role: IT must be viewed strictly as an ancillary aid. It must not replace the judge's fundamental role in evaluating evidence, interpreting the law, or adjudicating cases.
  • Authority over Physical Presence: Judges retain full authority to order the physical presence of parties, demand original documents, and hear witnesses in person whenever necessary.
  • Videoconferencing Safeguards: The deployment of videoconferencing facilities must not diminish the guarantees of the defense or compromise the qualitative perception of the trial.
  • 4-Stage EU E-Government Benchmark Model: Encourages the evolution of court electronic communication from Stage 1 (Online Information) and Stage 2 (Downloadable Forms) toward Stage 3 (Two-Way E-filing) and Stage 4 (Full Electronic Transactions, e.g., MCOL-PCOL).
  • Judges' Participation in IT Governance: IT procurement and system architecture decisions must actively involve judges and remain under the supervision of an independent judicial body.

Implementation

  • New Legal Information Market Structure: The direct publication of jurisprudence by courts via the internet (standardized by ECLI) alters the legal information market, transforming courts from mere adjudicators into primary, direct information producers for the public.
  • CEPEJ Longitudinal Data (2004–2008): Demonstrated a significant increase in the presence of jurisprudence databases (from 33 to 41 countries) and word processing at the judge's desk (40 to 45 countries). However, comprehensive electronic file systems (e-files) remained experimental in most nations, with Austria being a notable exception implementing it extensively.

Policy Problems

  • Exclusion of Judges in IT Decisions: In more than half of the member states, critical IT decisions are dictated by the Ministry of Justice or the executive bureaucracy without inclusive judicial participation.
  • Risk of Dehumanization & Rigid Standardization: The widespread use of automated, system-based forms risks creating a rigid judiciary that is less sensitive to the unique, individual circumstances of citizens.

Reform / Change

  • Institutionalizing mandatory initial and ongoing IT training for all judges and court administrative staff.
  • Transferring the authority for IT decision-making from executive branches to the Council for the Judiciary or a similar independent body.
  • Documenting and facilitating the structured exchange of experiences and institutional experimentation regarding IT applications across CoE member states.
