JPW-POLICY-000001

By Cecep Mustafa, Indonesian Judicial College (IJC) · revision 1320, 2026-09-17 · wiki source · Markdown

The Judicial Reform and Dispute Resolution Policy Framework in Asia (Stable ID: JPW-POLICY-000001) is a multi-jurisdictional legal reform policy framework established across Asian nations following the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis. Coordinated under the auspices of the Institute of Developing Economies (IDE-JETRO) and partner academic institutions (including the University of the Philippines College of Law), the framework outlines structural measures to modernize judicial institutions, expand Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR), separate executive power from judicial administration, and integrate social justice, gender equality, and disability rights into national legal systems.

Summary

The framework synthesizes country-level reform reports from eight Asian nations. It addresses chronic judicial delays, docket backlogs, local protectionism, and restrictive administrative standing doctrines. Core strategies include:

  • Court-annexed conciliation and mediation.
  • Creation of specialized courts (e.g., commercial, family, intellectual property, bankruptcy).
  • Professional legal education expansion and mandatory continuing legal education (MCLE).
  • Relaxation of locus standi for Public Interest Litigation.
  • Statutory rights protection for vulnerable populations (women and persons with disabilities).

Legal Basis

The policy framework derives authority from constitutional and statutory benchmarks across participating jurisdictions:

  • Thailand: 1997 Constitution of Thailand (establishing the Constitutional Court and Administrative Courts); Act Establishing Intellectual Property and International Trade Court B.E. 2539 (1996); Bankruptcy Act B.E. 2483 Amendments No. 4 (1998) & No. 5 (1999).
  • Vietnam: 1992 Constitution of the Socialist Republic of Vietnam (Arts. 22, 127); Law on Foreign Investment (amended June 2000).
  • Philippines: 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Arts. II, VIII, XIII); Batas Pambansa Blg. 129; Family Courts Act of 1997 (R.A. 8369); Women in Development and Nation-Building Act (R.A. 7192).
  • Japan: Constitution of Japan; Court Organization Law; Judicial Reform Council Establishment Law (1999).
  • Malaysia: Consumer Protection Act 1999 (Act 599); Copyright Act 1987/1991; Arbitration Act 1952.
  • India: Constitution of India (Arts. 32, 214, 226); Code of Civil Procedure 1908/1999; Code of Criminal Procedure 1973; Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996.
  • China: Constitution of the People's Republic of China; Law of Judges and Law of Prosecutors (1995); Law on the Protection of Disabled Persons (1990).
  • Indonesia: 1945 Constitution of Indonesia (Transitional Regulation Art. II); Indonesian Civil Code (Burgerlijk Wetboek) Book III; Act No. 1 of 1995 (Limited Liability Company).

Background & Policy Problems

Background

Following the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis and accelerating economic globalization, Asian economies exposed severe structural and institutional vulnerabilities. International financial organizations (IMF, World Bank, ADB) and national governments recognized that sustainable economic recovery required moving beyond technical macroeconomic policy to rebuild legal infrastructures, enhance transparency, and enforce the rule of law.

Policy Problems

Courts across Asia suffered from chronic docket congestion and extreme case backlogs (e.g., over 646,000 pending cases in Malaysian Magistrate courts as of February 2000; over 25 million pending cases in India in 1995). Additional structural issues included:

  • Lack of specialized commercial or technical expertise among traditional judges.
  • Local protectionism and administrative interference (e.g., Chinese local courts reliant on local government budgets and personnel control).
  • Rigid standing (locus standi) barriers restricting public access in administrative litigation (e.g., Japan's Syobunsei doctrine).
  • Structural neglect of vulnerable groups, including women and persons with disabilities.

Scope & Implementation

Scope

Covers commercial, administrative, criminal, and social law sectors across eight primary jurisdictions: Thailand, China, Vietnam, Japan, the Philippines, Malaysia, India, and Indonesia.

Implementation Matrix

Jurisdiction Key Implementation Measures
Thailand Supreme Court Practice Guidance on Court-Annexed Conciliation; establishment of the Central Intellectual Property and International Trade Court (1996) and Bankruptcy Court (1999).
Vietnam Establishment of Economic, Labor, and Administrative Courts within the People's Court system; creation of the Training Judicial Offices School (1998).
Philippines Implementation of the "Davide Watch" reform agenda; expansion of Philippine Judicial Academy (PHILJA) training; pilot court mediation projects; enactment of Family Courts Act (1997) and Anti-Rape Law (1997).
Malaysia Creation of specialized mediation bureaus (Insurance Mediation Bureau 1992, Banking Mediation Bureau 1997, Malaysian Mediation Centre) and Consumer Complaints Tribunal (1999).
India Enactment of Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996; establishment of Lok Adalats, CAT, CEGAT, and Consumer Commissions; judicial relaxation of locus standi for Public Interest Litigation (PIL).
Japan Enactment of Judicial Reform Council Establishment Law (1999); introduction of Small Claim Procedures (1996); establishment of industrial Product Liability (PL) Centers; graduate law school system creation.
China Implementation of the Law of Judges and Law of Prosecutors (1995); enactment of national disability protection legislation (1990).
