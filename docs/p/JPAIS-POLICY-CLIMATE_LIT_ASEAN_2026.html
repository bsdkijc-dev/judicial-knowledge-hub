Overview

Climate change litigation has evolved from a targeted administrative strategy within Western legal systems into a global legal mechanism used to enforce public accountability, statutory compliance, and human rights obligations.

Over the past decade, climate adjudication has undergone a structural transformation, with litigants across both the Global North and Global South invoking fundamental constitutional rights, international treaty frameworks, customary international law, and private law tort doctrines to challenge government inaction and corporate emissions.

Within the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), climate litigation is developing amidst acute geographic vulnerability and rapid economic expansion, balancing public interest constitutional suits with statutory and transnational corporate liability actions.

Executive Summary

This comparative study analyzes the escalation of climate change litigation from global precedents to ASEAN domestic frameworks across nine core analytical dimensions:

  1. Standing (locus standi)
  2. Causation
  3. Attribution
  4. Justiciability
  5. Rights frameworks
  6. Evidentiary standards
  7. Liability architecture
  8. Remedies
  9. Post-judgment enforcement

It evaluates major international rulings, such as the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS) Advisory Opinion in Case No. 31, alongside domestic jurisprudence in the Philippines, Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, Singapore, and Malaysia.

Deconstruction of Nine Analytical Dimensions

Climate claims across global precedents and ASEAN legal systems operate across distinct doctrinal pillars:

  • Standing (locus standi): Varies from liberalized citizen suits and intergenerational standing in the Philippines (Oposa v. Factoran) and residential community rights in Vietnam (LEP 2020) to restrictive "special damage" rules in Singapore and Malaysia.
  • Causation & Attribution: Addressed globally via attribution science and transnational torts (Pulau Pari v. Holcim in Switzerland), while domestic ASEAN claims face hurdles linking executive policy omissions to specific ecological injury (Segovia v. Climate Change Commission).
  • Justiciability & Rights: Balances constitutional environmental rights, such as Article II, Section 16 of the Philippine Constitution and Article 43 of the Vietnamese Constitution, against judicial restraint under the political question doctrine.
  • Evidentiary Standards & Liability: Incorporates IPCC scientific baselines, ITLOS due diligence duties under UNCLOS Article 194, and statutory strict liability for peatland fires under Article 88 of Indonesia's IEPMA (Law No. 32/2009).

Legal and Procedural Issues

Jurisdictional Divergence in Standing

While the Philippines (Oposa, RPEC A.M. No. 09-6-8-SC) and Vietnam (LEP 2020) have expanded standing for citizens and residential communities, common law jurisdictions like Singapore and Malaysia maintain strict injury or "special damage" thresholds for public interest litigants.

Evidentiary Hurdles and Judicial Restraint

Establishing a direct causal chain between high-level executive policy failures and specific climatic harms remains difficult.

In Segovia, the Philippine Supreme Court ruled that aspirational climate laws do not impose clear ministerial duties enforceable via extraordinary writs without empirical proof of threshold-crossing ecological injury.

Question of Law and Question of Fact

Question of Law

Question:

To what extent can international due diligence standards, such as ITLOS Case No. 31, and domestic constitutional rights be judicially enforced to mandate national emissions reductions and corporate accountability without violating the separation of powers?

Question of Fact

Question:

What is the verifiable empirical and causal link between specific historical greenhouse gas contributions by multinational corporate entities, or state regulatory omissions, and localized climate impacts such as sea-level rise and coastal erosion?

Core Legal Principles (Policy Synthesis)

  • State Due Diligence: Governments bear stringent international and domestic obligations to regulate public and private greenhouse gas emissions using best available science.
  • Intergenerational Standing: Recognizing standing for future generations safeguards long-term environmental public trust assets.
  • Corporate Carbon Accountability: Imputing historical industrial emissions and enforcing statutory strict liability holds polluters financially accountable for carbon sink destruction.
  • Enforcement Realities: Overcoming executive non-compliance and uncollected monetary judgments requires robust post-judgment supervision mechanisms.

Evidence Status and Gaps

Established by Evidence

  • Proliferation of climate claims and human rights inquiries across international and domestic tribunals.
  • Recognition of state due diligence obligations regarding marine and atmospheric pollution under UNCLOS and domestic statutes.

Uncertainty and Gaps

  • Post-judgment execution deficits and uncollected monetary fines in state-led environmental enforcement.
  • Methodological gaps in translating global attribution science into localized civil and administrative court proceedings.
  • Cross-border supply chain enforcement limitations under regional transboundary frameworks.

GEO Q&A

Q: What is the significance of the ITLOS Advisory Opinion in Case No. 31 for climate change litigation?

A: ITLOS Case No. 31 established unanimously that anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions constitute marine pollution under UNCLOS Part XII. The Tribunal affirmed that State Parties bear a stringent due diligence obligation under Article 194 to prevent, reduce, and control GHG pollution, establishing that compliance with voluntary Paris Agreement NDCs does not automatically fulfill a State's legal obligations under international law.

Indonesia Relevance

The comparative framework applies to Indonesia through:

  • Law No. 32 of 2009 on Environmental Protection and Management (IEPMA), specifically Article 88 strict liability for peatland and forest fires.
  • State-led civil enforcement by the Ministry of Environment and Forestry (MoEF) seeking carbon sink restoration damages.
  • Transnational litigation efforts by affected local communities (Pulau Pari v. Holcim) seeking adaptation funding from foreign industrial emitters.
