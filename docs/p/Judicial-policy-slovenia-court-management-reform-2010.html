Slovenian Court Management and Justice Administration Reform (Act Amending the Courts Act of 2009 / ZS-I Framework) is a judicial governance policy and legal reform framework in the Republic of Slovenia that regulates the division of authority between Justice Administration (Pravosodna uprava) under the Ministry of Justice and Court Management (Sodna uprava) under court presidents and the Judicial Council (Sodni svet).

Summary

Issued through the Act Amending the Courts Act of 2009 (ZS-I) effective January 1, 2010, this reform addressed the court backlog crisis and the diffusion of responsibility triggered by the 1995 judicial reorganization (highlighted in the Lukenda v. Slovenia case). This policy centralized the management of 44 Local Courts into 11 District Courts, transferred the authority to appoint Court Presidents to the Judicial Council (Sodni svet), and institutionalized the professional position of Director of the Court to manage judicial business operations.

Legal Basis

  • Constitution of the Republic of Slovenia:
    • Article 3: Principle of separation of powers and checks and balances.
    • Article 23: Right to judicial protection by an independent and impartial court.
    • Article 125: Independence of judges, bound only by the Constitution and laws.
    • Articles 130, 131, and 132: Status, composition, and authority of the Judicial Council (Sodni svet) [18–20].
    • Articles 156 and 157: Authority of exceptio illegalis and judicial review of administrative acts.
  • Act Amending the Courts Act of 2009 (ZS-I): Amended the Courts Act 1994 and became effective January 1, 2010.
  • Judicial Service Act: Regulates the Service Supervision (Službeni nadzor) mechanism in Articles 79a–79c.
  • ECtHR Jurisprudence: Lukenda v. Slovenia ruling (Application no. 23032/02, 2005).

Background

Following independence, Slovenia implemented the Courts Act 1994, dividing first-instance courts into 11 District Courts and 44 Local Courts. This structure caused 55 courts to operate without unified coordination. Senior judges were concentrated in District Courts, while Local Courts were staffed by junior judges handling massive caseloads. Consequently, case backlogs peaked, leading to the ECtHR ruling against Slovenia in the 2005 Lukenda case.

Objectives

1. Eliminate case backlogs and guarantee the right to a trial within a reasonable time. 2. Centralize first-instance court management at the District Court level without relocating or closing Local Court buildings. 3. Strengthen judicial independence by transferring the appointment of Court Presidents from the Executive to the Judicial Council. 4. Separate business/financial managerial tasks from judicial tasks through the position of Director of the Court.

Scope

This policy applies to the entire general judicial system in Slovenia, which includes:

  • 11 District Courts (Okrožna sodišča) and 44 Local Courts (Okrajna sodišča).
  • 4 Higher Courts (Višja sodišča) and the Supreme Court of the Republic of Slovenia (Vrhovno sodišče).
  • Specialized Courts (3 Labor Courts, 1 Labor & Social Court, and the Administrative Court).

Key Provisions

  • Centralization of District Court Management: All Local Courts within a district became organizational units of their respective District Court. The President of the District Court holds sole management authority, including the drafting of the annual schedule of judges and judicial redistribution. An exception applies to the Ljubljana Local Court due to its massive size (>100 judges).
  • New Role of the Judicial Council (Sodni svet): The authority to appoint and dismiss Court Presidents (6-year term) was transferred from the Minister of Justice to the Judicial Council based on the evaluation of 6-year strategic work programs and interviews.
  • Appointment of the Director of the Court (Direktor sodišča): A civil servant position (5-year term) appointed by the Minister of Justice upon the proposal of the Court President to manage finance, human resources, material resources, public procurement, and security.
  • Service Supervision (Službeni nadzor): Judicial performance supervision proposed by the Minister of Justice must undergo final approval by the Judicial Council if the Court President deems the proposal unjustified.

Implementation

  • Annual Plan of Work: The Court President is obligated to draft an Annual Plan of Work alongside case resolution projections. Failure to achieve targets for 2 consecutive years without valid reasons serves as grounds for dismissal by the Judicial Council.
  • Position Simplification: Abolished the position of Vice-President of the Court in all Local Courts (except the Ljubljana Local Court) to save budgets and return judges to full adjudicative functions.

Policy Problems

  • **Demarcation of Authority**: The operational boundaries between the Court President (judicial management) and the Director of the Court (business management) still require continuous practical adjustments.
  • **Judicial Council Capacity**: The Judicial Council requires strengthened internal staff capacity to execute its comprehensive judicial performance evaluation authority.

Reform / Change

  • Institutionalized regular roundtable forums among the Minister of Justice, President of the Judicial Council, and President of the Supreme Court to align Justice Administration and Court Management policies.
  • Organized biannual coordination meetings with all Court Presidents to review the achievements of the Annual Plan of Work.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • Ministry of Justice of Slovenia: Administrator of Justice Administration (Pravosodna uprava) and appointing body for the Director of the Court.
  • Judicial Council (Sodni svet): A sui generis body appointing Court Presidents and guaranteeing judicial independence.
  • Constitutional Court of Slovenia: Reviewer of the constitutionality of judicial regulations and inter-branch relations.
