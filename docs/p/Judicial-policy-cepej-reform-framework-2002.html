The European Commission for the Efficiency of Justice (CEPEJ) Reform & Judicial Evaluation Framework is a policy framework, statistical evaluation mechanism, and judicial reform instrument established by the Council of Europe (CoE) via Resolution Res(2002)12. It was created to formulate standards, indicators, and time management tools to optimize the quality, effectiveness, and speed of judicial systems across the 47 CoE member states, based on the standards of Articles 5, 6, and 13 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) [1–3, 7–8, 11–12].

Summary

Published by Prof. dr. juris Jon T. Johnsen (University of Oslo) in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework evaluates the first decade of CEPEJ's operations since its establishment in 2002. Formed in response to a backlog crisis at the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) related to trial delays, CEPEJ performs functions such as collecting comparative judicial statistical data (GT-EVAL) and drafting trial time management guidelines (SATURN Centre) [1, 2, 5, 17–19, 43]. This framework triggered the application of reasonable time trial standards across 47 CoE member states without acting as a rigid supervisory body (non-supervisory body).

Legal Basis

  • Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe Resolution Res(2002)12: The founding statute of CEPEJ, adopted on September 18, 2002.
  • European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR):
    • Article 6: The right to a fair trial and the resolution of cases within a reasonable time.
    • Article 5: The right to liberty and security of person.
    • Article 13: The right to an effective remedy at the national level.
  • ECtHR Jurisprudence: Landmark judgments such as Hadjidjanis v. Greece (2005), Scordino v. Italy (2006), and Airy v. Ireland (1979) [11, 66, 78–79].
  • EU Accession Criteria (Copenhagen Criteria): CEPEJ judicial standards are used as a benchmark for the eligibility of EU candidate states.

Background

In the early 2000s, the ECtHR experienced a functional crisis due to a backlog of over 72,000 cases. The vast majority of these complaints originated from citizens protesting the excessive length of judicial proceedings in Southern and Eastern Europe [5–6]. The paradox that the very court designed to protect ECHR rights could not fulfill the principle of a speedy trial prompted the Council of Europe to establish CEPEJ to reform national judicial systems from their roots.

Objectives

1. Examine the results achieved by different judicial systems through reliable statistical evaluation methods. 2. Identify problems and facilitate the exchange of experiences in court governance. 3. Formulate time management instruments to combat trial delays. 4. Provide technical assistance to member states requiring adjustments to meet ECHR standards.

Scope

This policy encompasses the 47 member states of the Council of Europe (covering >800 million citizens). The biennial statistical evaluation (European Judicial Survey) collects over 2.5 million data entries from 46 participating states [18–19].

Key Provisions

  • Four Main Organs of CEPEJ: The Plenary (gathering representatives from all member states twice a year), the Bureau (4 elected members), Expert Working Groups (such as GT-EVAL and SATURN), and the CoE Secretariat.
  • Reasonable Time Benchmarks: ECtHR standards set tolerance limits: Ordinary criminal cases (<5 years); Priority civil cases (<2 years); Complex civil cases (<8 years).
  • EUGMONT & Time Management Checklist: Standardized statistical guidelines for monitoring case workflows and providing early warnings of potential delays [48–49].
  • Legal Aid Principles: Based on Airy v. Ireland, legal aid must be provided to impoverished citizens in complex civil and criminal cases.

Implementation

  • SATURN Centre (Judicial Time Management): Drafted the Framework Programme, optimum duration guidelines, and a Best Practice Compendium based on pilot court research.
  • Application of Gentle Persuasion: Encouraging voluntary national judicial reforms without the direct application of legal sanctions.

Policy Problems

  • Legal Aid Shortcoming: CEPEJ is criticized for not prioritizing legal aid schemes, leading to extreme disparities (e.g., Albania allocated €0.01 per capita while Northern Ireland allocated €54 per capita).
  • Unavailability of Real Time Data: The majority of member states do not yet record the real handling time of each case, forcing reliance on disposition time estimates [56–57, 61–62].

Reform / Change

  • Shifting CEPEJ's strategic focus from drafting new guidelines toward prioritizing the implementation of existing instruments in the field [13, 64–65].
  • Strengthening direct dialogue between CEPEJ and the ECtHR so that SATURN instruments are consistently referenced in judgments concerning Article 6 ECHR violations.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR): The judicial body enforcing the ECHR, which references CEPEJ instruments [5, 78–79].
