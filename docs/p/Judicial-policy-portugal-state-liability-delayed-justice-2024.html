The Portuguese Legal Framework of State Liability for Delayed Justice is a legal and jurisprudential framework in the Portuguese Republic that regulates the civil and administrative liability of the State for damages caused by the violation of the right to a judicial decision within a reasonable time.

Summary

Issued through a combination of statutory law (Article 12 of the RRCEE) and the progressive jurisprudence of the Portuguese Supreme Administrative Court (Supremo Tribunal Administrativo - STA), this framework extensively integrates the case law of the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR). The policy allows citizens to swiftly file claims for damages against the State through the application of a judicial presumption of ordinary non-material damage and the adoption of standardized ECtHR compensation benchmarks, thereby satisfying the requirement for an effective domestic remedy in accordance with Article 13 of the ECHR [5, 57, 58, 68–74, 79, 80].

Legal Basis

  • European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), Article 6(1): The right to a fair and public hearing within a reasonable time.
  • European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), Article 13: The right to an effective remedy before national authorities.
  • Constitution of the Portuguese Republic (CRP), Article 20(4): The constitutional right to a judicial decision within a reasonable time.
  • Regime of Extra-Contractual Civil Liability of the State (RRCEE / Law No. 67/2007), Article 12: Governs the right to compensation for the violation of the reasonable time requirement [12–14].
  • RRCEE Articles 7, 8, and 10: Outlines the four general cumulative conditions for administrative civil liability [14–18].
  • Portuguese Civil Code (Código Civil), Articles 487(2), 496(1), and 563: Establishes the bonus pater familias standard, non-material damage compensation, and the doctrine of adequate causality (teoria da causalidade adequada).

Background

For more than three decades, Portuguese courts have faced lawsuits claiming damages for delayed trials, particularly within administrative and tax jurisdictions. Because Portugal has been condemned approximately 50 times by the ECtHR for violating Article 6(1) of the ECHR, national courts were driven to adopt Strasbourg doctrine to prevent recurring international condemnations and to preserve national legal sovereignty under the principle of subsidiarity.

Objectives

1. Fulfill Portugal's international obligations under Articles 6(1) and 13 of the ECHR. 2. Provide a rapid, effective, and predictable domestic remedy mechanism for affected citizens. 3. Achieve the principle of restitutio in integrum or an equitable financial compensation for the anguish and frustration caused by judicial delays.

Scope

This policy applies across all Portuguese judicial jurisdictions (civil, criminal, administrative, and tax), with a primary focus on damage claims filed in the Supreme Administrative Court (STA) and the Central Administrative Court (TCA) concerning delays in State judicial services.

Key Provisions

  • Four Cumulative Conditions for State Liability:
  1. Illicit Fact: The violation of the duty to administer justice within a reasonable time [14–16].
  2. Fault: Assessed based on an individual negligence standard (bonus pater familias) or anonymous service failure (abnormal functioning of service / faute de service) [16–18].
  3. Damage: Encompasses both material (patrimonial) and non-material (non-patrimonial) damages.
  4. Causal Link: Causality assessment based on Article 563 of the Civil Code.
  • Four ECtHR Reasonable Time Criteria: The duration of a case is assessed based on: (i) the complexity of the case, (ii) the conduct of the authorities/judges, (iii) the conduct of the parties, and (iv) what is at stake for the applicant.
  • Calculation of Dead Time (Tempo Morto): Assessing abnormal halts that obstruct the progress of the trial (punctual perspective).

Implementation

  • Presumption of Ordinary Non-Material Damage: Judges apply a judicial presumption (presunção judicial) that delayed justice automatically inflicts ordinary moral damage (anxiety, frustration, postponed life plans) on the applicant without requiring individualized proof [58–60, 63, 65].
  • Standardization of Compensation (Quantum): Judges adopt standardized compensation scales developed by the ECtHR in similar cases (e.g., Scordino v. Italy) to ensure equity in damages awarded [68–74].

Policy Problems

  • Lack of Specific Statutory Rules: Article 12 of the RRCEE does not contain a detailed definition of reasonable time criteria or a compensation formula, making the framework heavily reliant on judicial creation and interpretation.
  • Burden of Proof for Specific Damages: Non-material damage exceeding ordinary levels (such as severe clinical depression) is not protected by legal presumption and must be strictly and independently proven by the plaintiff.

Reform / Change

  • Dr. Ricardo Pedro recommends the explicit statutory codification of the ECtHR's reasonable time criteria and compensation mechanisms by the Assembly of the Republic to enhance legal certainty.
  • Continuous alignment between the jurisprudence of the Portuguese STA and the ECtHR to maintain the status of RRCEE claims as an effective domestic remedy under Article 13 of the ECHR.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • ECtHR (Strasbourg): The primary reference for ECHR rights interpretation and the provider of compensation quantum standards.
  • Supreme Administrative Court (STA): The principal national court implementing the presumption of damage and ECtHR criteria.
