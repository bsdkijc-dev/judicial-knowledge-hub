American Service-Members' Protection Act (ASPA) — "The Hague Invasion Act"

Wiki Entity Page

American Service-Members' Protection Act (ASPA) (informally nicknamed "The Hague Invasion Act") is a United States federal law enacted by the US Congress in August 2002 as an amendment introduced by Senator Jesse Helms to the National Defense Authorization Act. The law was designed to protect US military personnel and government officials from criminal prosecution by the International Criminal Court (ICC), headquartered in The Hague, Netherlands.

Summary

ASPA prohibits all federal, state, and local government bodies (including domestic courts and law enforcement) from cooperating with the ICC. This prohibition covers the extradition of US citizens to the ICC, the transfer of classified intelligence/law enforcement information, and the conduct of investigations by ICC agents on US soil. In addition, ASPA grants the US President special authority to use "all means necessary and appropriate" to free US or allied personnel detained by or at the request of the ICC.

Legal Basis

  • US Congressional Enactment (August 2002): Passed as an amendment introduced by Senator Jesse Helms to the National Defense Authorization Act.
  • Section 2008 of ASPA: Grants executive authority to the US President to authorize action to free US/allied personnel detained at Scheveningen prison, The Hague.
  • Rome Statute: The international treaty establishing the ICC, which the United States has not ratified.

Background

The US government feared that the establishment of the ICC under the Rome Statute could be exploited for politically motivated prosecutions of US soldiers and officials serving abroad. Congress therefore enacted ASPA to shield US personnel from international judicial jurisdiction.

Objectives

1. Protect US military personnel, elected officials, and government officers from ICC criminal jurisdiction. 2. Prevent US domestic law enforcement and courts from cooperating with ICC investigations. 3. Restrict US military aid to ICC member states that have not signed an "Article 98 agreement".

Scope

  • Cooperation Restrictions: Prohibits extradition, transfer of intelligence files, and field investigations by ICC agents within the United States.
  • Military Aid Restrictions: Freezes US military assistance to ICC member states, except for NATO members, major non-NATO allies, Taiwan, or countries that have signed an Article 98 agreement.
  • Military Authorization ("The Hague Invasion Clause"): Grants the US President authority to free US detainees held at Scheveningen prison near The Hague.

Key Provisions

  • Presidential Waiver Option: The US President may waive the military aid ban if deemed essential to US national interests.
  • Article 98 Agreements: Exempts countries that pledge not to surrender US citizens to the ICC from the military aid sanction.

Policy Problems

1. Diplomatic Tension with the Netherlands: The nickname "The Hague Invasion Act" reflects the potential use of US military force on Dutch sovereign territory (The Hague), conflicting with the Netherlands' international law commitments. 2. Obstacle to International Law Enforcement: ASPA hampers the ICC, which is supported by 109 nations, in enforcing universal justice. 3. Perception of Jurisdictional Imbalance: US non-participation risks reinforcing the impression that the ICC targets only developing nations (particularly in Africa).

Reform / Change

  • Obama Administration Review: Under President Barack Obama, foreign policy related to ASPA was placed under review. The Managing Editor of the *International Journal for Court Administration* (Philip M. Langbroek) recommended repealing the law to enhance the ICC's authority and effectiveness without financial burden to the US.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • US Congress & Senator Jesse Helms: Sponsor and drafter of the ASPA legislation.
  • International Criminal Court (ICC) & Government of the Netherlands: Subject of the restrictions and host of the Scheveningen detention facility.
