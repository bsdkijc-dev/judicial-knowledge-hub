'Training and Recruitment of Judges in Germany' (Riedel, 2013)

Wiki Entity Page

Training and Recruitment of Judges in Germany is a judicial policy study and legal education system monograph authored by Johannes Riedel (Chief Justice / President of the Court of Appeal in Cologne, Germany) and published in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), Vol. 5, No. 2, October 2013 (ISSN 2156-7964) [1–3].

Summary

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of the recruitment procedures, state examinations, competence profiles (Anforderungsprofile), and initial training for career judges in the Federal Republic of Germany [1–5]. The German judicial system utilizes a uniform legal qualification concept (Einheitsjurist), meaning all registered legal professions (judges, prosecutors, lawyers, and legal civil servants) undergo the exact same university education and practical training (Vorbereitungsdienst). The selection of career judges is conducted independently by the 16 states (Länder) through the Ministries of Justice, Higher Regional Courts, and Judicial Electoral Committees (Richterwahlausschüsse).

Legal Basis

  • German Constitution (Grundgesetz - GG):
    • Article 33(2): Ensures every citizen's right to equal access to public office based solely on aptitude, qualifications, and professional ability (Eignung, Befähigung und fachliche Leistung).
    • Articles 92 & 95: Regulate the separation of judicial powers between the Federation and the 16 Länder.
  • German Judiciary Act (Deutsches Richtergesetz - DRiG):
    • Sections 5 & 7: Regulate academic qualifications and state examinations (Befähigung zum Richteramt).
    • Section 9: Requires German citizenship, loyalty to the democratic legal order, judicial qualifications, and social competence (added during the 2002 reform).
  • State Legal Training Acts: Such as the Juristenausbildungsgesetz Nordrhein-Westfalen (JAG NRW).

Legal Education System & State Examinations

1. University Studies & First State Examination (Erstes Staatsexamen): Theoretical legal studies at a university averaging 4 years, culminating in the First Examination divided between a state board exam and a university faculty exam. 2. Preparatory Service (Vorbereitungsdienst): A 2-year paid practical training period that includes rotations in civil courts (5 months), criminal courts/prosecution (3 months), administrative agencies (3 months), and law firms (10 months). 3. Second State Examination (Zweites Staatsexamen): A practical graduation exam focusing on drafting judgments, indictments, and lawyer pleadings. 4. Passing Threshold: Only about 15% of graduates successfully achieve an "above average" score ($\ge 9.00$ points / vollbefriedigend out of an 18-point scale), serving as the primary threshold to be invited for a judicial recruitment interview.

Competence Profiles & Selection Procedures

The Länder Ministries of Justice utilize competence profiles (Anforderungsprofile) encompassing four main categories:

  • Professional Competence: Mastery of the law, ability to apply it in practice, mature judgment, and understanding of judicial duties.
  • Personal Competence: Natural authority, physical/mental resilience under pressure, ability to make swift decisions, and awareness of personal weaknesses.
  • Social Competence: Teamwork ability, communication, conflict resolution/mediation, and public service awareness.
  • Leadership Competence: Clarity of instructions and trust in staff.

Assessment Center Model (Cologne Court of Appeal District Example)

The 1-day selection process includes [32–36]: 1. Group Discussion: Debating a legal policy issue to test structured discussion skills and openness to other opinions. 2. Working Test (10 Cases): Completing 10 concise civil/criminal procedure tasks under time pressure to test prioritization and decision-making speed. 3. Self-Reference (Visitenkarte): A written reflection on personal achievements and motivations. 4. Multi-Stakeholder Panel Interview: Interviewed by the President of the Court of Appeal, President of the Regional Court, Head of Human Resources, Equal Opportunity/Gender Representative, and a Staff Council Representative.

Initial Training of New Judges

Newly appointed probationary judges (averaging 28–30 years old) undergo a probationary period of 3–5 years before receiving a lifetime appointment. Initial training includes [43–46]:

  • Assignment to a 3-judge panel (Landgericht) to share responsibilities with senior judges.
  • An initial workload reduction (50%–70%) in certain administrations.
  • A compulsory 3-day seminar covering trial techniques, witness examination, time management, and communication psychology.

Policy Problems

1. Interview Bias: The risk of selection panels favoring highly eloquent candidates over thoughtful, quieter candidates, requiring a balanced evaluation against their multi-year academic track record. 2. Lack of Transparency in Federal Judicial Selection: The election of federal supreme court judges by the Richterwahlausschuss at the federal level is conducted without open public job advertisements (Ausschreibung). 3. Impact of Single-Judge Reforms: Changes to procedural codes mandating first-instance trials be decided by single judges reduce opportunities for young judges to learn within panels.
