By Cecep Mustafa, Indonesian Judicial College (IJC) · revision 1228, 2026-09-17 · wiki source · Markdown

Performance-Based Budgeting of French Judicial Courts (LOLF 2001) (*Loi organique du 1er août 2001 relative aux lois de finances*) is a fundamental reform of the French Republic's "financial constitution" that shifted the principle of state budget allocation from a "logic of spending" to a "logic of performance." Applied to the judiciary through "Mission Justice" and Program 166 (*Justice judiciaire*), LOLF introduced quantitative performance indicators, the principle of asymmetric budget fungibility (*fongibilité asymétrique*), Operational Program Budgets (*Budget Opérationnel de Programme* – BOP), and the transfer of expenditure authorization to Chiefs of Appeal Courts.

Summary

Enacted on 1 August 2001 and fully implemented starting with the 2006 Budget Act, LOLF aims to improve the transparency and efficiency of public financial management. In judicial administration, LOLF divides the budget into five main programs and delegates management of Operational Program Budgets (BOP) to Chiefs of Appeal Courts (*Chefs de cour*). However, an academic assessment by Prof. Thierry Kirat reveals a gap between the theory of managerial authority and the reality of continued budget centralization by the Ministry of Justice (Chancery), administrative burdens imposed without additional staff, and quantitative indicators that fail to reflect the quality of justice.

Legal Basis

  • Loi organique n° 2001-692 du 1er août 2001 (LOLF): Restructured France's state budget framework and mandated Annual Performance Plans (*Plan annuel de performance*).
  • Decree No. 2004-435 of 24 May 2004 (Article R. 213-30 of the Code of Judicial Organization): Transferred expenditure authorization to Chiefs of Appeal Courts.
  • Legal Aid Act of 10 July 1991: Framework for legal aid cost recovery.
  • Law No. 2007-210 of 19 February 2007: Reformed legal protection insurance (*assurance de protection juridique*), establishing the principle of subsidiarity for state legal aid.

Background & Historical Precursor

Efforts at performance-based budgeting in France date back to 1968 through the *Rationalisation des Choix Budgétaires* (RCB) method, which adopted the American PPBS system. RCB failed due to an unprepared administrative structure. LOLF 2001 was redesigned by financial technocrats without the involvement of legal-economics experts, producing rigid, raw quantitative indicators.

Structure of "Mission Justice"

The judicial public policy architecture is divided into five main programs:

  1. Program 166 (Justice judiciaire): General civil and criminal courts.
  2. Program 107 (Administration pénitentiaire): Prison administration.
  3. Program 182 (Protection judiciaire de la jeunesse): Judicial protection of youth.
  4. Program 101 (Accès au droit et à la justice): Access to law and legal aid.
  5. Program 213 (Conduite et pilotage de la politique de la justice): General administration and Chancery management.

(Note: Administrative Courts (*Juridictions administratives*) are excluded from Mission Justice and instead fall under the "State control and counsel" mission.)

Key Provisions & Managerial Mechanisms

  • Asymmetric Fungibility (*Fongibilité Asymétrique*): Court managers may shift funds from wage allocations toward operating costs or capital investment, but are strictly prohibited from converting operating/capital funds into additional wage appropriations.
  • Budget Opérationnel de Programme (BOP): Managed at the Court of Appeal level jointly by the First President of the Court of Appeal (*Premier Président*) and the Attorney General (*Procureur Général*).
  • Program 166 Performance Indicators: Include average duration for resolving civil/criminal cases, the percentage of courts exceeding time-limit ceilings, and the rate of reversal by higher courts.

Policy Issues

1. Conflict with Separation of Powers: The 2004 decree requires joint authorization (co-scheduling) between the First President of the Court of Appeal (an independent judge) and the Attorney General (hierarchically subordinate to the Minister of Justice), blurring the line between the prosecutorial function and judicial independence. 2. Hidden Centralization (Chancery Control): BOP flexibility at the local level remains tightly controlled by the Director of Judicial Services at the Ministry of Justice, rendering court autonomy largely illusory. 3. Administrative Burden Without Additional Staff: The shift of accounting duties from local *préfectures* to court leadership burdens judges and court clerks (*greffiers*) who are not trained in financial management. 4. Absence of Savings Incentives: Budget efficiencies achieved by lower courts (such as a €1.4 million saving at the TGI of Nanterre) were not returned to those courts for other operating expenses. 5. Shifting the Legal Aid Cost Burden: The government shifted litigation cost burdens onto the private insurance industry and imposed strict limitative caps on costs such as wiretapping and DNA testing.

Reform / Implications

  • A critical assessment by the Court of Audit (*Cour des Comptes*), delivered by First President Philippe Séguin in 2009, concluded that LOLF budgeting in the courts "remains largely theoretical" while old centralized practices continue in effect.
