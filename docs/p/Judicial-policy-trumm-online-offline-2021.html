Online versus offline: Exploring the link between how candidates campaign and how voters cast their ballot is an empirical comparative political science and public policy research paper authored by Siim Trumm (University of Nottingham), published in the European Journal of Political Research (EJPR) in 2021 (print edition Vol. 61, pages 485–502, May 2022).

Summary

This study analyzes the relationship between candidates' use of online and offline campaign tools and their resulting vote share, across both traditional paper ballots and internet voting (i-voting), in the 2019 Estonian parliamentary election (*Riigikogu*). Using data from the 2019 Estonian Candidate Study (N = 344) and official results from the Estonian National Electoral Committee, the study tests whether online campaigning primarily attracts i-voters while offline campaigning attracts traditional voters. The findings show that online and offline campaigning both produce significant, roughly balanced positive effects on traditional vote share and i-vote share alike.

  • Estonian Parliamentary Electoral Framework (*Riigikogu*): The study is set within Estonia's open-list proportional representation system across 12 constituencies, which has offered electronic voting via ID-card / Mobile-ID authentication since 2005/2007.

Background

Adoption of internet voting (i-voting) in Estonia grew rapidly, from 5.5% in the 2007 election to 43.8% in the 2019 parliamentary election. However, earlier research on campaign effects did not distinguish between traditional and internet votes, while earlier i-voting research focused only on voter demographics without examining candidates' campaign strategy choices.

Objectives

1. Test Hypothesis 1: candidates using a broader range of online and offline campaign tools gain higher overall, traditional, and i-vote shares. 2. Test Hypothesis 2: offline campaigning has a stronger effect on traditional vote share, while online campaigning has a stronger effect on i-vote share.

Scope

The research analyzes 344 candidates who ran in the 2019 Estonian parliamentary election across 12 constituencies, evaluating 10 campaign tools (5 offline and 5 online).

Key Provisions / Findings

  • Hypothesis 1 Supported (Positive Campaign Effects): Both online and offline campaign tools significantly increase overall vote share, traditional vote share, and i-vote share (p < 0.01).
  • Hypothesis 2 Rejected (Symmetrical Cross-Channel Effects): Online and offline campaign tools produce remarkably similar effect sizes across both voting channels.
    • Overall Vote Share: Going from 0 to 5 online tools increases predicted vote share by +0.86%; going from 0 to 5 offline tools increases it by +0.73% (combining both yields +1.78%).
    • Traditional Paper Votes: Online tools add +0.69% and offline tools add +0.69%.
    • Internet Votes (i-Votes): Online tools add +1.19% and offline tools add +0.83%.
  • Media-Transcending Political Activity: Voters are not confined to a single medium. Voters who receive offline flyers or canvassing often end up casting an i-vote, and voters reached through online ads often vote on paper.
  • Incumbency and Personal Background Bonus: Prior experience as a national MP provides a +1.14% advantage in vote share (p < 0.01). Focusing on personal background increases candidate vote share (+0.44%), while over-emphasizing party policy decreases it (-0.54%).

Implementation

The methodology was implemented through OLS regression models with a log-transformed dependent variable, controlling for constituency fixed effects, political experience, and party affiliation.

Policy Issues

1. The flawed strategy of candidates who prioritize only online campaigning for internet voters or only offline campaigning for traditional voters. 2. Reduced candidate effectiveness when party policy is emphasized over personal profile within an open-list system.

Reform / Implications

1. Treating online and offline campaigning with equal importance in campaign budgeting and strategy. 2. Designing integrated campaign materials for the entire electorate rather than rigidly segmenting messaging by media channel.
