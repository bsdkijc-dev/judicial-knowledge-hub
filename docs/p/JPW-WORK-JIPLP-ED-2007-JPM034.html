'''"People in greenhouses"''' is a legal editorial written by [[Jeremy Phillips]], editor of
the ''[[Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice]]'', published by
[[Oxford University Press]] in Vol. 2, No. 5, p. 269 (2007), DOI: 10.1093/jiplp/jpm034.

== Summary ==
The editorial argues that the intellectual property system, designed around commercial market
incentives, is not naturally equipped to address problems that sit outside the market —
chief among them environmental harm and climate change. Taking the phrase "we all live in
greenhouses now" as its starting point, it proposes four specific reforms intended to redirect
IP incentives toward faster development and diffusion of environmentally beneficial
technology.

== Legal and Regulatory Background ==
* '''TRIPS Agreement''' — the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property
  Rights, administered by the [[World Trade Organization]] as an annex to the 1994 Marrakesh
  Agreement establishing the WTO. The source's institution list named only Oxford University
  Press and the journal itself, without identifying the WTO as the body administering the
  compulsory-licensing framework the editorial proposes to extend.
  ''ID:'' `JPW-DOCTRINE-TRIPS` — '''CLARIFIED'''
* '''Supplementary Protection Certificates (SPCs)''' — a mechanism that extends patent-like
  protection beyond a patent's normal term to compensate for regulatory delay in bringing a
  product to market. This is not a TRIPS instrument: SPCs exist under specific European Union
  regulations — Regulation (EC) No 469/2009 for medicinal products and Regulation (EC)
  No 1610/96 for plant protection products. The source discussed SPCs alongside TRIPS-based
  proposals without noting that SPCs are an EU-specific mechanism rather than part of the
  multilateral TRIPS framework; extending them to green automotive or fuel technology would
  be a matter for EU legislation, not a TRIPS amendment.
  ''ID:'' `JPW-SCHEME-EU-SPC` — '''CLARIFIED'''

== The Four Proposed Reforms ==
# '''Stronger compulsory licensing of green patents and designs''' — a compulsory-licensing
  regime for environmentally beneficial patents and designs going further than what TRIPS
  currently permits, coupled with adequate remuneration to the rights holder.
# '''Compulsory licensing of environmentally useful copyright works and databases''' — for
  example, vehicle satellite-navigation databases, to reduce unnecessary mileage and fuel
  consumption.
# '''Encouraging patent and technology pooling in eco-sensitive markets''' — without the
  threat of competition-law sanction that would normally attach to competitors coordinating on
  technology.
# '''Extending supplementary protection certificates to green, automotive and new-fuel
  technologies''' — mirroring the extended protection already available for pharmaceutical
  and agrochemical patents, to compensate for delayed commercialisation of green technology
  facing adoption or infrastructure barriers.

== Policy Issues ==
* '''Commercial-market design mismatch''' — the existing IP system is built to reward market
  investment, not to solve problems, like climate change, that arise outside ordinary market
  transactions.
* '''Jurisdictional fragmentation of the four proposals''' — reforms (1) and (2) point toward
  changes at the TRIPS/multilateral level, while reform (4) points toward EU-specific
  legislation; a workable reform agenda would need to treat these as separate legislative
  tracks rather than a single unified change.
* '''Remuneration and objection risk''' — any compulsory-licensing expansion raises open
  questions about setting fair remuneration and managing objections from conventional
  commercial rights holders.

== Interpretation and Inference ==
* '''Interpretation''' — "we all live in greenhouses now" is used to signal that the
  environmental crisis has become a background condition affecting every market and every
  area of law, IP included, and that the IP community should widen its focus from narrow
  rights-holder interests to the broader public interest.
* '''Inference''' — absent reform along these lines, patented green technology risks being
  under-used or reaching the public too slowly. This is the editorial's own inference from the
  proposals it advances, not an empirical finding.

4. Fact / Interpretation / Inference Separation

Facts (attested or on the public record)

  1. "People in greenhouses" was written by Jeremy Phillips and published in JIPLP Vol. 2, No. 5, p. 269 (2007), DOI 10.1093/jiplp/jpm034, by Oxford University Press.
  2. The editorial argues the IP system is designed for commercial markets and not naturally suited to addressing environmental externalities.
  3. It proposes four reforms: stronger compulsory licensing of green patents and designs; compulsory licensing of environmentally useful copyright works and databases; encouraging patent and technology pooling in eco-sensitive markets without competition-law sanction; and extending supplementary protection certificates to green, automotive and new-fuel technologies.
  4. TRIPS is administered by the World Trade Organization; supplementary protection certificates for pharmaceutical and agrochemical products exist under specific EU regulations, not under TRIPS.

Interpretation — the "greenhouses" framing is meant to shift the IP community's focus from narrow rights-holder interests toward the wider public interest in environmental protection.

Inference — without licensing and pooling reform, patented green technology risks slow or incomplete diffusion.

7. Improvements Applied

1. Identified the World Trade Organization as the body administering TRIPS. The source's institution list named only Oxford University Press and the journal, leaving the reader to infer which body actually runs the compulsory-licensing framework the editorial proposes to extend. TRIPS is a WTO agreement, annexed to the 1994 Marrakesh Agreement — added here as CLARIFIED since the source's account was accurate as far as it went but materially incomplete.

2. Corrected the implied jurisdictional unity between TRIPS reform and SPC reform. The source presented the four proposals as a single package without noting that reform (4) — extending supplementary protection certificates to green technology — is not a TRIPS matter at all. SPCs exist under specific EU regulations (Regulation (EC) No 469/2009 for medicinal products, Regulation (EC) No 1610/96 for plant protection products). Treating this alongside multilateral TRIPS reform as though both belonged to the same legal track would mislead a reader about which legislature would actually need to act on which proposal.

3. Verification status per entity, with named entities kept as addressable nodes and no fabricated relationships. The blanket [1–7] citation marker was dropped, including from the statement that two people were absent from those sources, which a citation cannot evidence. Cecep Mustafa and Rosianna Maretha Hasibuan are retained by name with the same permanent IDs used across all seven editorial packages, with UNVERIFIED status and no asserted role.
