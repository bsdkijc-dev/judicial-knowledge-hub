'From the Managing Editor - The Judicial Gown Does Matter' (Langbroek, 2011/2013)

Wiki Entity Page

The Judicial Gown Does Matter Policy Framework is an editorial policy analysis and legal sociology framework authored by Philip Langbroek (Managing Editor of the International Journal for Court Administration - IJCA). This framework examines the symbolic, depersonalizing, and authority-enhancing functions of judicial gowns and courtroom rituals in transforming individual judges into disinterested speakers of the law. The framework integrates the anthropological theories of civil religion and totemic authority (Lior Barshack) alongside empirical political science experiments (Benjamin Woodson, James L. Gibson, and Milton Lodge), which demonstrate that symbols of justice significantly increase public acquiescence to unpopular court decisions [2–5].

Summary

Although judicial gowns may seem distant from everyday legal rationality, Langbroek asserts that gowns and courtroom symbols play a vital role in establishing judicial authority and institutional legitimacy. According to Antoine Garapon, the gown is derived from the royal mantle, where the color black symbolizes chastity, purity, and distraction from worldly affairs. The gown makes the individual persona of the judge "disappear" and transforms them into a neutral speaker of the law. The empirical experiment by Woodson, Gibson, and Lodge (2011) proved that exposure to judicial symbols (such as the judge's gown, gavel, and Lady Justice) significantly increased the acceptance rate of the public—especially those unfamiliar with legal procedures—toward court decisions that contradicted their personal preferences.

Anthropological Functions & Judicial Rituals

1. Depersonalization & Role Equivalence: The gown makes all actors in the trial equivalent in their procedural roles. The gown conceals the subjective identity of the judge and affirms the office that strives for truth. 2. Adjudication as a "Civil Religion": Lior Barshack explains that adjudication is part of a civil religion. The judge does not merely represent the law, but "speaks the law". 3. The Judge as an Animated Totem: In the court ritual, the judge acts as a totem—sacred, unarmed, sexless, and separated from worldly affairs like monastic inhabitants. Touching the judge is considered a desecration. 4. Redefining Legal Relations: Through the magical act of the totem, relations between the disputing parties are detached from their previous state and redefined based on the law, which is considered a higher truth standing independently of the judge and the parties.

Empirical Findings of Woodson, Gibson, & Lodge (APSA 2011)

  • Experimental Methodology: Researchers tested groups of respondents classified by their level of exposure to the Supreme Court. Respondents were asked to read arguments on a controversial case (such as the presence of a cross at a national monument or the treatment of an asylum seeker who committed a crime).
  • Symbol Intervention: Respondents were exposed to pictures of justice symbols (gavel, Lady Justice, or Supreme Court justices in gowns) or neutral pictures.
  • Delivery of Adverse Decision: The decision provided consistently contradicted the preference previously indicated by the respondent.
  • Main Results: The group of respondents with no exposure to the Supreme Court who were exposed to justice symbols showed a significantly higher acceptance rate of adverse decisions compared to the group with no exposure who viewed neutral pictures.
  • Policy Implication: The use of gowns and judicial symbols directly strengthens judicial authority, especially for citizens unfamiliar with legal procedures.

Risks of Automated Decisions & Dehumanization of Power

Langbroek warns that judicial symbols cannot protect society from abuse if other branches of state power fail to respect the judicial position. Legislative efforts to turn the law into context-ignoring, automated decision-making—requiring only formal judicial approval—will turn judges into dehumanizing instruments of power execution. Nevertheless, without these symbols, justice would be less effective and less trusted by the public.
