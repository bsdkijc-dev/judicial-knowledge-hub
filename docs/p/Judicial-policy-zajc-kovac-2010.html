'What Do the European Judges Strive for - An Empirical Assessment' (Zajc & Kovač, 2010)

Wiki Entity Page

European Judicial Motivation and Quality Benchmarks Framework is a law and economics policy analysis and evaluation authored by Dr. Katarina Zajc and Dr. Mitja Kovač (University of Ljubljana). Prepared for the 2010 EGPA Conference and published in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this framework analyzes judicial motivation, incentive structures, and caseload backlogs in European civil law countries using principal-agent theory, Posner's utility maximization model (Homo Economicus), the carrots and sticks mechanism, and an economic evaluation of the Finnish Quality Benchmarks [1–3, 7, 24].

Summary

This study addresses the puzzle of why case backlogs persist in Europe despite continuous efforts to improve decision quality. In continental European judicial bureaucracies, society acts as the Principal while the court/judge acts as the Agent. Because monitoring judges' performance is extremely costly and information is asymmetric ($C_d > B_p$), judges tend to engage in shirking. Zajc & Kovač prove that increasing the salaries of already employed judges is ineffective at increasing productivity. Instead, judicial motivation can be enhanced through non-financial incentives (carrots) such as promotion prospects (rank-order tournaments), peer reputation/awards, and legal legacy [11, 13–16]. The exclusive use of punishments (sticks) is rejected because the multiplication effect can threaten judicial independence [20–22, 37].

Core Theory & Economic Analysis

1. Principal-Agent Theory & Information Asymmetry

In civil law judiciaries, society (Principal) grants authority to the court/judge (Agent). Because human capital-based work is difficult to measure, detection costs ($C_d$) exceed prevention benefits ($B_p$), triggering moral hazard and shirking.

2. Judicial Utility Model (Posner - Homo Economicus)

Judges are utility maximizers who prefer high salaries, reasonable workloads, reputation, and the freedom to make decisions without interference.

3. Carrots vs. Sticks & The Multiplication Effect

  • Carrots (Positive Incentives):
    • Promotion Prospects & Rank-Order Tournaments: Judges compete to achieve high scores (number of decisions, citation rates, clearance speed, reversal ratios) to gain promotions [11–14].
    • Reputation & Awards: "Judge of the Year" awards, top 10% lists, or public appreciation.
    • Legal Legacy: The desire to influence legal development and create jurisprudence.
  • The Danger of Sticks (Sanctions/Punishments) & The Multiplication Effect (Dari-Mattiacci & De Geest):

Although sanctions (threats of dismissal, salary cuts, criminal prosecution) appear strong, applying sticks to non-verifiable performance carries a high risk of abuse by authoritarian rulers and undermines judicial independence [20–22, 37].

Six Aspects of the Finnish Quality Benchmarks

The research evaluates the Finnish Rovaniemi Quality Project model, which consists of 6 aspects and 40 criteria: 1. The Process: Transparency, independence, expediency, settlement encouragement, flexibility, and public access. 2. The Decision: Fair, lawful, convincing reasoning, systematic, easily understood, and correct language. 3. Treatment of Parties: Respect for human dignity, fair advice, prompt service, and communication/PR. 4. Promptness: Optimum processing time, case importance context, and adherence to deadlines. 5. Competence & Skills: Continuous training, annual personal development interviews, and thorough preparation. 6. Organization & Management: Professional management, methodical case assignment, monitoring delayed cases, and preventing workload overload.

Comparative Empirical Findings from European Countries

  • Finland: Annual court budget negotiations with the Ministry of Justice encourage efficiency; individual sanctions are very rare (only 2 reprimands in 2006 out of 901 judges).
  • Germany: Court presidents evaluate case volumes for promotions; there were 20 professional inadequacy proceedings and 22 criminal cases in 2006 out of 20,138 judges.
  • Sweden: Processing time targets are agreed upon annually with the National Courts Administration; only 2 reprimands in 2006 out of 1,039 judges.
  • Netherlands: Targets are set at the court level; the Supreme Court only handles 1–2 judicial dismissals per year.
  • Slovenia: Promotion is governed by 9 statutory criteria under the Judicial Service Act. A 2009/10 financial incentive scheme faced opposition from some judges citing interference with independence.
