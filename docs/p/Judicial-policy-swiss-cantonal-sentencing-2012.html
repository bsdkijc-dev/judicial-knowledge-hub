The Swiss Cantonal Sentencing Disparities & Equality Before the Law Policy Framework is a policy framework and empirical-comparative analysis of criminal justice administration in Switzerland. It evaluates the variation in the application of criminal law, the use of pre-trial detention, the frequency of unsuspended prison sanctions, and their impact on recidivism rates across 26 cantons based on the principle of equal treatment before the law (Article 5 of the Swiss Constitution) [1–3, 15, 21, 32–37].

Summary

Published by Dr. Daniel Fink and Dr. Christophe Koller in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework dissects the reality of criminal justice across 26 Swiss cantons. Utilizing data from the Swiss Federal Statistical Office (SFSO) and the BADAC database, the study proves the existence of extreme disparities in sentencing and the use of pre-trial detention among cantons [1–4, 13–15, 21, 24]. The main finding of this study reveals "The Efficiency Paradox": despite the high inequality of sanctions between repressive and lenient cantons, recidivism rates across all cantons are identical, proving that short-term prison sentences are no more effective than monetary penalties or community service [8, 32, 35–37].

Legal Basis

  • Article 5 of the Swiss Federal Constitution: Guarantees the principle of equal treatment before the law for all citizens.
  • Swiss Criminal Code (Code Pénal Suisse - RS 311): Unification of Swiss criminal law since 1942; amended by the 2007 Penal Sanction System Revision (Article 41 CC) which restricts the use of prison sentences under 6 months [16, 26–28].
  • Swiss Criminal Procedure Code (CPP - RS 312.0): Specifically Article 212 CPP, which establishes the principle of proportionality and strict limits on the use of pre-trial detention.

Background

Within the Swiss federal system, the unification of the Criminal Code enacted in 1942 is implemented by 26 cantons holding full autonomy over the execution of criminal justice. This condition spawned sharply differing criminal policies among the cantons. For decades, the government had never conducted an empirical evaluation of whether this autonomy violated Article 5 of the Swiss Constitution regarding equality before the law.

Objectives

1. Evaluate the empirical variation in criminal sentencing and pre-trial detention across the 26 Swiss cantons. 2. Test the effectiveness of criminal sanctions on the reconviction rate (recidivism). 3. Encourage the formulation of empirically informed crime policies at the cantonal level. 4. Ensure respect for the principle of equality before the law for every defendant.

Scope

This policy evaluates the entire criminal justice system across the 26 Swiss Cantons (including Geneva, Vaud, Zurich, Basel-Stadt, Basel-Landschaft, Bern, Neuchâtel, Fribourg, Schaffhausen, Zug, Obwalden, etc.) based on adult conviction data from the SFSO between 1985 and 2010.

Key Provisions

  • Four Cantonal Evaluation Indicators: (1) Total number of criminal convictions; (2) Frequency of pre-trial detention; (3) Frequency of unsuspended prison sentences; (4) Recidivism rates.
  • Geographical-Cultural Disparities: French-speaking cantons (GE, VD, NE, JU, FR) tend to be on the repressive side, with conviction and pre-trial detention rates far exceeding those of German-speaking cantons.
  • Extremity of Pre-Trial Detention: The canton of Geneva holds up to 55% of all convicts in pre-trial detention, while the majority of other cantons remain below 10%. Over half of the convicts detained in Geneva are ultimately not sentenced to unsuspended prison terms.
  • The Efficiency Paradox of Sanctions: Case study results on drunk driving prove that recidivism rates across all cantons remain in the exact same range (8%–12%), regardless of whether the canton imposes severe prison sentences or merely issues fines [34–35].

Implementation

  • Impact of Article 41 CC Revision (2007): The obligation for judges to provide detailed written reasoning when imposing prison sentences of <6 months successfully reduced the number of prison verdicts from 16,000 (2006) to 6,500 (2007) [26–28].
  • Sanction Substitution: Shifting expensive and stigmatizing short-term prison sentences to monetary penalties and community service.

Policy Problems

  • Violation of the Principle of Equality: Defendants committing similar crimes receive vastly different sanctions depending on the canton in which the case is tried.
  • High Cost of Short-Term Imprisonment: Short-duration prison sentences have proven to be the most expensive and least efficient sanction in suppressing recidivism.

Reform / Change

  • Widespread dissemination of empirical findings by judicial administrators to all cantons to reduce reliance on pre-trial imprisonment.
  • Optimization of monetary sanctions as a socially and economically more advantageous penal instrument.
