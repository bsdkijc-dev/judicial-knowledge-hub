The Concept for Controlling at the Federal Supreme Court (Controllingverfahren des Bundesgerichts) is a policy framework and self-administration controlling system at the Swiss Federal Supreme Court (Schweizerisches Bundesgericht). It establishes a three-level reporting mechanism between the court, parliamentary commissions, and the public to balance the efficiency of judicial management with the principles of judicial independence and the separation of powers [1, 5, 6, 9–14].

Summary

Published and analyzed by Prof. Dr. iur. Andreas Lienhard (University of Bern) in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework was developed in response to the enactment of the Ordinance on Judges of the Federal Supreme Court 2006 by the Swiss Parliament. The Concept for Controlling document, jointly drafted by the Administrative Commission of the Federal Supreme Court and the Parliamentary Audit Commissions on March 5, 2007, establishes a three-level reporting governance structure. This system restricts the disclosure of individual judges' performance data to protect judicial independence, while simultaneously providing accurate caseload data to parliamentary commissions and the general public.

Legal Basis

  • Swiss Federal Constitution (Bundesverfassung - BV 1999):
    • Article 168(1) & Article 169(1) BV: The authority of the Parliament in the election of judges and the high oversight over the Federal Supreme Court.
    • Article 188(3) BV: The right of self-administration of the Federal Supreme Court.
    • Article 30 & Article 191c BV: The constitutional guarantee of the independence of the judiciary.
    • Article 126(1) & Article 170 BV: The principles of the economical use of budgetary resources and the evaluation of federal institutional effectiveness.
  • Swiss Federal Supreme Court Act (Bundesgerichtsgesetz - BGG / SCA, SR 173.110): Establishes the structure, jurisdictional authority, and self-governance of the Court.
  • Ordinance on Judges of the Federal Supreme Court (June 23, 2006, SR 173.110.1): Specifically Article 2, which mandates the controlling and reporting procedures.
  • Federal Act on the Federal Assembly (Parlamentsgesetz, SR 171.10): Articles 25, 26, and 40a regarding the oversight functions of the Parliamentary Audit Commissions and Court Commission.

Background

Applying modern management instruments (New Public Management) to a supreme judicial institution often raises concerns about the erosion of judicial independence. In Switzerland, the Parliament demanded transparency regarding workloads to determine budget allocations and the number of judges. To prevent institutional friction, the Federal Supreme Court and the Parliamentary Audit Commissions formed a joint working group to formulate an oversight concept that strictly respects the separation of powers.

Objectives

1. Fulfill the Parliament's oversight obligations without intervening in judicial decision-making. 2. Provide ICT-based internal management tools to optimize file allocation and reduce case delays. 3. Restrict parliamentary commissions' access to individual judge performance data to prevent the politicization of the judicial re-election process. 4. Enhance public trust through the transparency of the Annual Report.

Scope

This policy covers the administration and caseload controlling at the Swiss Federal Supreme Court (including the consolidation of the Federal Insurance Court), and serves as a reference model for first-instance federal courts (Federal Criminal Court & Federal Administrative Court) and Cantonal Supreme Courts.

Key Provisions

  • Three-Level Reporting Mechanism:
    • Level 1 (Public / Annual Report): Publication of aggregated national statistics including case duration, settlement rates, workload per division/legal field, and administrative reports on HR, finance, and ICT.
    • Level 2 (Parliamentary Commissions): Submission of detailed data to the Audit Commissions and Court Commission regarding pending cases, average case costs per division, the ratio of trial time vs. administration, and draft judgments.
    • Level 3 (Internal Court Controlling): Use of ICT tools by the Administrative Commission and Division Presidents to monitor daily case status, workloads of court clerks, and a special monitoring list (black list) for cases where drafts have been delayed for more than 3 months.
  • Protection of Judicial Performance Confidentiality: Individual judge performance data is not disclosed to the parliament; commissions only receive reports if there are objective, severe, and recurring performance flaws relevant for re-selection (Art. 40a para. 6 Parliament Act).

Implementation

  • ICT Restructuring Project: The modernization of IT-based statistical applications in Lausanne, completed by the end of 2007.
  • Average Case Cost Indicator: Calculating case costs based on the allocation of personnel costs, judicial operations, and infrastructure.

Policy Problems

  • The Judicial Re-Selection Dilemma: Determining the exact boundary of how far judicial efficiency data can be utilized by the Court Commission without triggering automatic re-selection metrics or political pressure.
  • Budgetary Balance: The tension between the freedom to exercise judicial functions and the obligation to practice national budget austerity.

Reform / Change

  • Institutionalization of a model for the co-operative handling of the separation of powers in Switzerland.
  • Replication of the reporting standards to first-instance federal courts and cantonal judiciaries.
