3. POLICY ANALYSIS

  • Background: Rapid global advances in FinTech, crowdfunding, and initial coin offerings (ICOs) provided alternative financing mechanisms to lower transaction costs and expand capital access. Following Presidential instructions and the Strategy for the Development of the Information Society, Russia developed a comprehensive legal model for digital assets.
  • Problem: Unregulated digital asset offerings created severe shadow economy risks, lack of investor protection against fraud or hacking, and inability of state authorities (Ministry of Finance, Bank of Russia) to monitor financial flows or prevent money laundering.
  • Legal/Policy Gap: Traditional Russian civil law lacked statutory definitions for virtual property and digital tokens. Furthermore, Russian statutory terminology ("digital rights", "DFA", "utility digital rights") diverged from standard international classifications (Security Tokens, Utility Tokens, Payment Tokens/Cryptocurrencies).
  • Objectives: Establish a clear legal environment for the digital economy, enable civil transactions and fundraising via digital technologies, protect investors, and bring token issuance under formal regulatory supervision.
  • Scope: Governs investment platform operators, DFA information system operators, exchange operators, natural person investors, and issuing entities within Russian jurisdiction, including mandatory conflict-of-laws rules for cross-border transactions involving Russian residents.
  • Implementation: Enacted via Federal Law No. 259-FZ on Investment Platforms (2 August 2019), Federal Law No. 259-FZ on DFA (31 July 2020), and Civil Code Amendments (Articles 128, 141.1; effective 1 October 2019). Oversight is managed by the Bank of Russia via official registers.
  • Evidence: Empirical statutory analysis, comparative review of EU market regulations (Prospectus Directive/Regulation, MiFID II, MAR, EU Digital Finance Package), and conflict-of-laws analysis under Russian private international law and EU Rome I Regulation.
  • Impact: Legally recognized "digital rights" as property objects in Russian civil law. Introduced statutory caps on retail investments (600,000 rubles/year cap for non-qualified natural persons across platforms) and strict registration requirements for operators.
  • Reform: Legal scholars recommend aligning national digital asset terminology with global standards, establishing explicit statutory consumer protection rules, creating dedicated regional legislation for federal subjects, and forming an international comity of national regulators to set uniform global ICO standards.
  • Unresolved Issues:
    • Excessive national isolationism resulting from restricting operator status exclusively to Russian legal entities.
    • Potential reduction in foreign investment due to unfamiliar legal terminology.
    • Lack of direct enforcement authority by the Bank of Russia over unlicensed offshore crypto entities.
    • Jurisdictional friction under international conflict-of-laws rules when classifying utility tokens.

5. MEDIAWIKI ARTICLE

The Russian Legal Framework for Initial Coin Offerings and Digital Assets is a statutory policy model in the Russian Federation governing initial coin offerings (ICOs), digital rights (*tsifrovye prava*), digital financial assets (DFA), and investment platforms.1

Lead

Adopted through a series of legislative reforms between 2019 and 2020, the framework establishes a "synthetic" regulatory model combining new federal statutes with targeted amendments to existing civil legislation.2 Legal analysis by scholar Marina Kasatkina highlights that while Russia established one of the world's most detailed legal structures for digital assets, its unique terminology ("digital rights" and "digital financial assets") diverges from international crypto-market standards.3

Summary

The regulatory framework rests upon two primary federal statutes and core Civil Code revisions:

  1. Civil Code Amendments (Arts. 128, 141.1): Effective 1 October 2019, recognizing "digital rights" as a distinct category of property rights under civil law.4
  2. Federal Law No. 259-FZ of 2 August 2019 (Law on Investment Platforms): Regulating crowdfunding and the issuance of "utility digital rights."5
  3. Federal Law No. 259-FZ of 31 July 2020 (Law on DFA): Regulating "digital financial assets," digital currency, and exchange operators.6

Legal Basis

  • Civil Code of the Russian Federation: Article 128 classifies digital rights as objects of civil rights under "other property"; Article 141.1 defines their exercise and transfer within designated information systems.7
  • Law on Investment Platforms (No. 259-FZ/2019): Governs contracts concluded via online investment platforms and defines utility digital rights.8
  • Law on Digital Financial Assets (No. 259-FZ/2020): Defines DFAs as digital rights encompassing monetary claims, equity security rights, and non-public JSC shares issued via distributed ledgers.9

Background

To prevent the expansion of shadow markets and protect investors from fraudulent schemes, the Russian President and Government issued directives in 2017 and 2019 ordering the creation of a legal framework for the digital economy.10

Objectives

  • Provide legal certainty for fundraising and civil transactions using digital technologies.11
  • Protect retail investors from unmitigated financial losses.12
  • Bring digital asset turnover under the supervision of state financial authorities.13

Scope

Applies to all digital rights issued, accounted for, or circulated within Russian jurisdiction, as well as cross-border transactions involving Russian resident investors or entities.14

Key Provisions

  • Digital Rights Definition: Property rights existing digitally in an information system meeting statutory criteria.15
  • Retail Investment Cap: Non-qualified natural persons are limited to investing a total of 600,000 rubles per calendar year across all investment platforms.16
  • Issuer Limit: Entities attracting investments through utility digital rights cannot exceed 1 billion rubles per calendar year (excluding public JSCs).17
  • Mandatory Operator Inclusion: Only Russian legal entities included in official Bank of Russia registers may operate investment platforms or DFA exchanges.18

Implementation

Supervised by the Bank of Russia (*Central Bank of Russia*), which maintains the registry of investment platform operators and DFA exchange operators.19

Policy Problems

  • Terminology Disconnect: Replacing international concepts (Security/Utility Tokens) with "DFA" and "Utility Digital Rights" creates confusion for foreign investors.20
  • National Isolationism: Restricting operator status to Russian legal entities limits foreign ICO project participation.21
  • Consumer Protection Gap: Lack of specific statutory dispute resolution rights for retail consumers compared to professional market participants.22

Reform

Scholars advocate harmonizing Russian terminology with global standards and forming an international regulatory comity to establish uniform global ICO guidelines.23
