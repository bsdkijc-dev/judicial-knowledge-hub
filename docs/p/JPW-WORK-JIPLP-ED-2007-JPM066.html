'''"Time to play Catch-Up"''' is a legal editorial written by [[Jeremy Phillips]], editor of
the ''[[Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice]]'', published by
[[Oxford University Press]] in Vol. 2, No. 6, p. 349, DOI: 10.1093/jiplp/jpm066, released in
Advance Access on 30 April 2007.

== Summary ==
The editorial examines judicial inconsistency in intellectual property enforcement across the
European Union, the United States and China, and argues that shared judicial training —
rather than centrally issued rules alone — is what actually produces consistent, predictable
outcomes. Its occasion is a two-day judges' meeting in Venice, including a mock trial
organised by the [[Office for Harmonisation in the Internal Market]] (OHIM), for judges from
twelve Italian Community trade mark and design courts.

== Legal Basis (Clarified) ==
The source's own entity-extraction table recorded the legal basis as "not found in the
available sources," despite the editorial's central example — Italian "Community Trade Mark
and Design Courts" — being courts that exist specifically to apply a named EU legal
instrument. The pan-European trade mark and design system these courts administer rests on:
* '''Council Regulation (EC) No 40/94''' on the Community trade mark (in force at the time of
  this editorial; later recast as Regulation (EC) No 207/2009).
* '''Council Regulation (EC) No 6/2002''' on Community designs.
''ID:'' `JPW-DOCTRINE-CTM-REGULATION` — '''CLARIFIED'''

== The Venice Judges' Meeting ==
On 26 March 2007, a two-day meeting was held in Venice for judges from twelve Italian
Community trade mark and design courts, including a mock trial organised by OHIM.
''ID:'' `JPW-EVENT-VENICE-JUDGES-MEETING-2007`

OHIM — the Office for Harmonisation in the Internal Market — was the EU agency responsible for
Community trade marks and designs at the time of this editorial. It was renamed the
'''European Union Intellectual Property Office (EUIPO)''' in March 2016; the name used in the
source reflects the agency's name as it stood in 2007 and is retained as historically
accurate, with the current name noted for readers consulting this page later.
''(CLARIFIED)''

== Comparative Observations ==
* '''European Union''' — the substantive law is harmonised across what were then 27 member
  states, but consistent national-court application does not automatically follow from
  harmonised rules; local judicial habits still diverge.
* '''United States''' — the creation of the [[United States Court of Appeals for the Federal
   Circuit]] (CAFC) improved doctrinal consistency in patent law, yet first-instance courts in
  different districts (East Texas, the West Coast, Virginia among them) continued to show
  divergent interpretive preferences, a pattern associated with strategic venue selection.
* '''China''' — enforcement and oversight of intellectual property rights were likewise
  observed to be inconsistent.
* '''Cultural and linguistic factors''' — an objective test such as prior art in patent law
  travels across jurisdictions more consistently than an evaluative test such as likelihood of
  confusion in trade mark law, which is more exposed to local linguistic and cultural
  variation.

== The "Catch Us If You Can!!!" Blog ==
The blog was managed by Lorenzo Litta and initiated by IP practitioner Stefano Sandri to raise
public awareness of the boundaries of IP protection.

== Publication Details ==
* ''Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice'', Vol. 2, No. 6 (2007), p. 349.
* Advance Access publication 30 April 2007. DOI: 10.1093/jiplp/jpm066.

== Policy Issues ==
* '''Limits of central rule-making''' — an edict from Brussels or a ruling from the Luxembourg
  courts does not by itself create a level playing field without a shared practical
  approach among national judges.
* '''Inconsistency as a by-product, not a vice or a virtue''' — variation is a natural
  consequence of a system that requires individual human judgment; the aim is to keep that
  variation from hardening into unpredictable outcomes, not to eliminate judicial discretion
  altogether.
* '''Forum-shopping risk''' — persistent regional divergence, as seen among US patent trial
  courts, creates an incentive to choose a favourable forum rather than a neutral one.

== Interpretation and Inference ==
* '''Interpretation''' — judicial inconsistency is neither a vice nor a virtue but a natural
  by-product of a system that requires personal judgment; shared training and joint
  simulation exercises make consistent, stable, predictable outcomes more likely.
* '''Inference''' — edicts from Brussels or rulings from the Luxembourg courts are not, on
  their own, enough to create a level playing field without a shared practical approach among
  national judges. This is the editorial's own conclusion drawn from the examples given, not
  an empirical measurement of judicial outcomes.

4. Fact / Interpretation / Inference Separation

Facts (attested or on the public record)

  1. "Time to play Catch-Up" was written by Jeremy Phillips and published in JIPLP Vol. 2, No. 6, p. 349, DOI 10.1093/jiplp/jpm066, by Oxford University Press, released in Advance Access on 30 April 2007.
  2. On 26 March 2007, OHIM organised a two-day meeting in Venice, including a mock trial, for judges from twelve Italian Community trade mark and design courts.
  3. The courts described exist to apply the Community trade mark and Community design regimes established by EU regulation.
  4. The US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit improved doctrinal consistency in patent law, but first-instance courts in different US districts continued to diverge in interpretive preference.
  5. OHIM was renamed the European Union Intellectual Property Office (EUIPO) in March 2016, after this editorial's publication.

Interpretation — judicial inconsistency is a natural by-product of systems requiring personal judgment, and shared training makes predictable outcomes more likely.

Inference — centrally issued rules alone, without a shared practical approach among national judges, cannot guarantee a level playing field.

7. Improvements Applied

1. Filled the "legal basis: not found" gap the source itself flagged. The source's own canonical-entity table recorded the legal basis as "not found in the available sources," even though the editorial's central example — Italian "Community Trade Mark and Design Courts" — only exist because of a specific EU legal framework: the Community Trade Mark Regulation (Council Regulation (EC) No 40/94, later recast as (EC) No 207/2009) and the Community Designs Regulation (Council Regulation (EC) No 6/2002). Leaving this blank when the courts discussed are named after the regime is a gap worth closing, marked CLARIFIED since it goes beyond what the source stated outright.

2. Noted OHIM's 2016 renaming to EUIPO. OHIM was the correct name for the agency at the time of this 2007 editorial and is retained as historically accurate, but the agency has since been renamed the European Union Intellectual Property Office. Flagging this keeps the page useful to anyone consulting it now without implying the source made an error — it didn't; the world simply moved on after publication.

3. Verification status per entity, with Sandri and Litta promoted to real person nodes and no fabricated relationships for the unverified names. The blanket [1–8] citation marker was dropped, including from the statement that two people were absent from those sources, which a citation cannot evidence. Cecep Mustafa and Rosianna Maretha Hasibuan are retained by name with the same permanent IDs used across all eight editorial packages, with UNVERIFIED status and no asserted role. The self-awarded perfect score was replaced by a QA table naming what remains open.
