Do government invitations to consultations shape stakeholder participation in public policymaking? is an empirical comparative political science and public administration research paper authored by Adriana Bunea and Idunn Nørbech of the University of Bergen (Norway), published in the European Journal of Political Research (EJPR) in 2025.

Summary

This study examines whether and how formal government invitations to online public consultations (OPCs) affect the volume and diversity of stakeholder participation in public policymaking. Drawing on a comprehensive dataset covering 251,153 stakeholder submissions (104,460 from citizens and 146,693 from organizations) across 4,062 public consultations held by Norwegian government ministries between 2009 and 2023, the study demonstrates that a higher number of government invitations leads to greater participation volume and greater diversity of interest groups involved.

  • Norwegian Public Consultation Scheme (*Høyringar*): Norway's executive administrative governance procedure requiring the collection of public input before a policy proposal is finalized.

Background

Online public consultations are used by government bureaucracies to involve citizens and organizations in policy formulation, aiming to reduce bias in interest representation and strengthen procedural legitimacy. In Scandinavian countries such as Norway, public consultation — historically rooted in traditional corporatist arrangements — has increasingly shifted toward pluralist patterns of representation. However, the effectiveness of bureaucratic outreach efforts through formal invitations in shaping public participation had not previously been tested systematically at scale.

Objectives

1. Test whether the number of government invitations increases both the total level of participation and the diversity of interests represented (Hypotheses 1.1 & 1.2). 2. Test the moderating effect of policy act type on the relationship between invitations and participation (Hypotheses 2.1 & 2.2). 3. Analyze how invitations directed at organizations can drive individual citizen mobilization (the "activation belt" effect).

Scope

The research covers all 4,062 online public consultations held by every executive ministry in Norway between 2009 and 2023.

Key Provisions / Findings

  • Hypothesis 1.1 Supported (Participation Volume): Each additional government invitation increases stakeholder participation by 0.5% (IRR = 1.005, p < 0.01). A one-standard-deviation increase in invitations (+84 invitations) yields an average additional +31.3 submissions for legislative proposals (+16.3 from organizations and +16.4 from citizens).
  • Hypothesis 1.2 Supported (Stakeholder Diversity): The number of invitations is significantly positively associated with the diversity index of interest types (β = +0.005, p < 0.01).
  • Policy Act Moderation Effects (Hypotheses 2.1 & 2.2): The positive effect of invitations on both participation volume and diversity is strongest for legislative proposals (*Lov*) and government reports (*NOU/Rapport*), while the effect is considerably more moderate for government regulations (*Forskrift*) and EU directives.
  • Organizations as an "Activation Belt" for Citizens: Although invitations are sent almost exclusively to organizations and institutions, an increase in organizational invitations significantly raises both the probability (p < 0.01) and frequency (p < 0.01) of individual citizen participation. For example, in a 2022 seabed mineral-mining consultation, the World Wildlife Fund (WWF) forwarded the ministry's invitation to its network, mobilizing 980 individual citizen submissions.

Implementation

The methodology was implemented through negative binomial regression models, hurdle models for citizen participation, and OLS regression for the Laakso-Taagepera diversity index [50–52, 85]. Replication data and code are stored openly on DataverseNO at `doi:10.18710/HZIBBH`.

Policy Issues

1. The risk of "consultation fatigue," in which a high volume of simultaneous invitations may strain the resources of stakeholder organizations. 2. High baseline participation inequality on technical or non-legislative issues (such as EU directives), which require additional bureaucratic outreach effort.

Reform / Implications

The study demonstrates that formal bureaucratic outreach through invitations is an effective, low-cost institutional instrument for democratizing public policymaking.
