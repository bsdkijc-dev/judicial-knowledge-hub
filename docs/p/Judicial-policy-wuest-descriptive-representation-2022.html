Voter preferences as a source of descriptive (mis)representation by social class is an empirical comparative political science and public policy research paper authored by Reto Wüest (University of Bergen) and Jonas Pontusson (University of Geneva), published in the European Journal of Political Research (EJPR) in 2022.

Summary

This study analyzes why democratically elected legislators consistently come from wealthier, more highly educated social strata than the citizens they represent (descriptive misrepresentation). Drawing on an online conjoint survey experiment involving 4,500+ Swiss citizens and an analysis of observational candidate data from the 2007 and 2011 Swiss National Council elections, the study tests whether this representational gap stems from voter demand or party supply. The findings show that voters generally reject routine working-class candidates and upper-middle-class candidates alike, favoring skilled working-class and lower-middle-class candidates instead.

  • Swiss National Council Electoral Rules (*Nationalrat*): The study is set within Switzerland's open-list proportional representation system, which allows voters to express direct candidate preferences through cumulation and list deletion.

Background

Unequal policy responsiveness toward lower-income groups has become a central issue in comparative politics. Across many democracies, legislators' occupational backgrounds have been shown to shape their voting behavior and legislative agenda-setting. In Switzerland, nearly one-third of parliamentarians in 2000 and 2010 were lawyers or entrepreneurs (compared to 13% of the active workforce), and 86% of elected MPs had household incomes above the national median.

Objectives

1. Test Hypothesis 1: on average, voters prefer skilled working-class (SWC) and lower-middle-class (LMC) candidates over routine working-class (RWC) and upper-middle-class (UMC) candidates. 2. Test Hypothesis 2: middle-class voters hold no bias against upper-middle-class (UMC) candidates relative to SWC or LMC candidates. 3. Analyze the roles of perceived competence ("competence bonus") and empathy ("understanding penalty") in candidate evaluation, based on Fiske et al.'s social psychology model.

Scope

The research comprises a conjoint experiment run on the LINK Institute's online panel with 4,500+ Swiss voters (May 2017), evaluating 8 candidate attributes (occupation, education, income, party, ideology, parliamentary experience, gender, and residence), alongside an analysis of observational candidate data from the FORS Selects surveys (2007 & 2011) [28, 33–37, 39, 75, 90].

Key Provisions / Findings

  • Hypothesis 1 Supported (Voter Preferences): Overall, voters give the highest support to skilled working-class (SWC) and lower-middle-class (LMC) candidates, while rejecting routine working-class (RWC) and upper-middle-class (UMC) candidates.
  • Hypothesis 2 Supported (Class-Divided Voter Preferences):
    • Working-class voters strongly reject UMC candidates, viewing them as out of touch with ordinary people's problems ("understanding penalty").
    • Middle-class voters show no bias against UMC candidates relative to SWC candidates, while strongly rejecting RWC candidates.
  • Competence Bonus vs. Empathy Penalty (Fiske Model): SWC, LMC, and UMC candidates all enjoy a significant "competence bonus" relative to RWC candidates. However, UMC candidates suffer an "understanding/empathy penalty," being perceived as disconnected from ordinary citizens' lives.
  • Turnout Inequality Mechanism: Survey data show a substantially higher turnout rate among middle-class citizens (81.4%–89.5%) than among working-class citizens (63.2%–79.3%). Because working-class citizens — who dislike UMC candidates — vote less often, middle-class voters, who are not biased against UMC candidates, end up dominating actual election outcomes.
  • Real-World Election Results: In real elections, routine working-class (RWC) candidates face very low chances of being elected, even when placed high on party lists. UMC candidates, by contrast, face no such penalty in practice, benefiting from significantly larger campaign budgets (an average of CHF 17,327 compared to CHF 2,230 for RWC candidates) and strategic party list placement (pre-cumulation).

Implementation

The methodology was implemented through Marginal Means (MMs) and Average Marginal Component Effects (AMCEs) estimated via OLS regression with respondent-clustered standard errors, along with multilevel logistic regression for the observational data. Replication data are held by FORS, with funding support from the SNSF (Grant No. 100017_166238) and the ERC (Grant No. 741538).

Policy Issues

1. Unequal responsiveness of public policy to low-income groups due to the scarcity of working-class representatives. 2. Systemic bias in candidate selection by party selectorates, which tend to favor highly educated, high-status candidates.

Reform / Implications

1. Increasing working-class voter turnout is the most effective way to reduce the dominance of upper-middle-class elites in parliament. 2. Campaign finance transparency reforms could help limit the advantage of wealthier candidates' larger campaign budgets.
