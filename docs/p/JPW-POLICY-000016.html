JPW-POLICY-000016

By Cecep Mustafa, Indonesian Judicial College (IJC) · revision 1353, 2026-09-17 · wiki source · Markdown

The Chinese Labour Dispute Settlement and Policy Framework (Stable ID: JPW-POLICY-000016) is a national statutory, administrative, and judicial policy framework evaluating the evolution, structure, procedures, social security integration, and reform of the labour dispute resolution system in the People's Republic of China. Documented under IDE Asian Law Series No. 22 by Wang Zhenqi (Ministry of Labour and Social Security), Prof. Wang Changshuo, and Assoc. Prof. Zheng Shangyuan (China University of Politics and Law) in collaboration with IDE-JETRO, the policy analyzes the "one-track, three-stage" dispute process (enterprise mediation, mandatory labour arbitration, and court litigation), social insurance pooling, Supreme People's Court Legal Interpretation 2001 No. 14, and proposed double-track reforms. Note: Named entities Cecep Mustafa and Reihan Abdan Syakur are Not found in the available sources.

Summary

The framework examines the restoration and modernization of China's labour dispute resolution machinery following the 1994 Labour Law and market reforms. It outlines six core structural pillars: (1) One-Track, Three-Stage Adjudication, requiring parties to proceed from Enterprise Mediation to mandatory Labour Dispute Arbitration Committees (LDAC) before accessing two-instance court litigation; (2) Social Insurance Legal System, integrating social pooling with individual accounts across pension, unemployment, medical, work injury, and maternity insurance; (3) Tripartite Arbitration Structure, composing LDACs with equal representation from labour administrative departments, trade unions, and enterprise associations; (4) Procedural Burden-Shifting, applying Supreme People's Court Legal Interpretation 2001 No. 14 to shift the burden of proof onto employers for termination/dismissal claims; (5) Collective Dispute Tripartism, operating joint coordination mechanisms between MOLSS, ACFTU, and CEC to manage strikes and mass incidents; and (6) Trade Union Legal Aid, delivering free legal representation to impoverished workers in arbitration and court suits.

Legal Basis

Authority and execution derive from constitutional, statutory, and judicial enactments:

  • Statutory Legislation: Labour Law of the People's Republic of China (adopted 5 July 1994, effective 1 January 1995); Legislation Law of the PRC (Art. 8); Civil Procedure Law of the PRC.
  • Administrative Regulations: Regulations on Settlement of Labour Disputes in Enterprises (State Council, 1993); Provisional Regulations on Settlement of Labour Disputes in State-run Enterprises (1987); Provisional Regulations on Collection of Social Insurance Contributions (1999); Regulations on Unemployment Insurance (1999); Regulations on Minimum Living Standard Security for Urban Residents (1999).
  • Judicial Interpretations & Ministerial Rules: Interpretations on Some Questions on Application of Laws and Regulations in Handling Labour Disputes (Supreme People's Court Legal Interpretation 2001 No. 14); Rule on Organisational Structure and Working Procedure of Enterprise Labour Dispute Mediation Committee (1993); Rules on Working Procedure of LDAC (1993); Methods on Settlement of Social Insurance-related Administrative Disputes (2001).

Background & Policy Problems

Background

Following the 1949 founding of the PRC, early labour dispute rules were established in 1950 but suspended in 1957. For 30 years under the planned economy, disputes were handled as administrative complaints without judicial recourse. Market reforms and state enterprise restructuring in the 1980s and 1990s triggered a surge in employment conflicts, prompting the State Council and National People's Congress to enact the 1993 Regulations and 1994 Labour Law.

Policy Problems

  • Protracted Procedural Chain: The mandatory "one-track, three-stage" chain (Enterprise Mediation → Mandatory Arbitration → First-Instance Court → Second-Instance Court) often takes over a year to reach a final judgment.
  • Overly Restrictive Statute of Limitations: Article 82 of the Labour Law imposed a strict 60-day filing limit starting from the date of dispute occurrence, causing many workers to forfeit their rights during negotiations.
  • Non-Final Arbitration Awards: LDAC decisions lack finality, operating with heavy administrative influence while routinely leading to de novo court litigation upon appeal.
  • Surge in Mass Collective Disputes: Rapid enterprise restructuring caused a sharp rise in collective disputes, particularly within state-owned enterprises undergoing conversion.
  • Reliance on Enterprise Mediation: Enterprise mediation committees are financially and organizationally dependent on employer management, compromising their structural neutrality.

Objectives

1. Protect the legitimate rights of workers and employing units under market economy conditions. 2. Pool social insurance risks across urban enterprises and employees. 3. Provide accessible mediation and arbitration for individual and collective labour disputes. 4. Prevent and resolve mass incidents through tripartite coordination. 5. Guarantee legal aid for impoverished workers in labour litigation.

Scope & Implementation

Scope

Applies across all urban enterprises, state-owned entities, foreign-funded firms, private businesses, individual entrepreneurs, trade unions, LDACs, and People's Courts in China.

Key Provisions

  • Mandatory Statute of Limitations (Art. 82 Labour Law): Requirement to file arbitration petitions within 60 days of dispute occurrence.
  • Burden of Proof Reversal (SPC Interpretation 2001 No. 14): Employer duty to adduce evidence in dismissal, demotion, and wage reduction claims.
  • Tripartite Arbitration Bench: Mandatory 3-member arbitration tribunal representing MOLSS, ACFTU, and CEC.
  • Thirty-Day Notice & Compensation (Art. 26 Labour Law): Requirement for 30 days' written notice and financial compensation for contract revocation due to changed objective circumstances.

Implementation

  • Arbitration Disposals: LDACs accepted 154,621 cases in 2001, settling 92% of disputes.
  • Enterprise Mediation Impact: Enterprise Mediation Committees resolved 1.32 million disputes by June 2002.
  • Judicial Case Volume: People's Courts tried 360,000 labour cases between 1997 and 2001.
  • Social Security Coverage: Pension pooling covering 142 million residents and medical insurance covering 83 million employees by 2001/2002.

Policy Problems

  • Protracted Procedural Chain: One-track, three-stage process taking over a year to reach final judgment.
  • Overly Short Statute of Limitations: 60-day filing window causing forfeiture of rights during negotiations.
  • Non-Final Arbitration Awards: Arbitration decisions lack finality, encouraging repetitive court litigation.

Reform

  • Enacting a double-track system allowing voluntary choice between arbitration and litigation.
  • Drafting a standalone Labour Dispute Settlement Law and Labour Contract Law.
  • Granting conditional legal binding force to mediation agreements.
  • Unifying arbitrator accreditation under the national judicial examination.
