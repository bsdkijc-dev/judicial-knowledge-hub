Policy Analysis

Background & Context

Commercial bankruptcy in Saudi Arabia was historically codified under Chapter 10 (Articles 103–135) of the Commercial Court Law of 1350H, derived from the Ottoman Commercial Code of 1863 and the French Commercial Code of 1807. Following economic growth, increased insolvency disputes, and modern legislative enactments—including the Execution Law (1433H) and the Sharia Civil Procedure Law (1435H)—the legal status of destitute merchants required clarification under Islamic Sharia principles.

Core Problems & Policy Gaps

In classical French-derived commercial law, bankruptcy centers on the cessation of payments (), viewing default as a systemic breach regardless of whether the debtor retains an estate. Conversely, Islamic jurisprudence draws a strict line between a bankrupt debtor who holds assets insufficient to satisfy debts (muflis) and a destitute debtor who owns no assets whatsoever (mu'sir). Subjecting an assetless debtor to liquidation provides no recovery for creditors and causes unjust harm.

A primary policy gap arose when Royal Decree M/1 of 1435H (Sharia Civil Procedure Law) repealed Articles 432–633 of the Commercial Court Law. This repeal eliminated Article 489—which expressly prohibited declaring destitute debtors bankrupt—without providing a substitute statutory provision, resulting in a temporary legislative vacuum () regarding destitute merchants.

Legal Basis & Statutory Baseline

  • Holy Qur'an (Surah Al-Baqarah 2:280): Directs creditors to grant time to debtors facing hardship ().
  • KSA Commercial Court Law 1350H: Articles 103–135 outline bankruptcy procedures; Article 489 protected destitute debtors by restricting judicial actions to debt confirmation without declaring bankruptcy.
  • KSA Execution Law 1433H (Royal Decree M/53): Article 78 governs insolvency petitions; Article 84 prohibits execution imprisonment () once insolvency is legally established.
  • Sharia Civil Procedure Law 1435H (Royal Decree M/1): Article 32(2) repealed former Commercial Court Law bankruptcy provisions; Article 35 assigns commercial bankruptcy cases to Commercial Courts.
  • KSA Judiciary Law 1428H (Royal Decree M/78): Article 11 empowers the Supreme Court to oversee adherence to Sharia principles and statutory decrees.

Implementation & Legal Conditions

Commercial courts must confirm four substantive conditions before entertaining a commercial bankruptcy application:

  1. Merchant Status (): The debtor must engage in commercial activities professionally and independently.
  2. Legal Capacity: The merchant must possess full legal capacity (age 18 under established judicial practice).
  3. Debt Commerciality: The underlying obligations must be verified, due, and commercial in nature.
  4. Asset Pool Verification: If court proceedings reveal that the debtor possesses no estate, the court must refuse a bankruptcy declaration and confirm the debt baseline.

Reforms & Unresolved Issues

Legal scholarship recommends that Saudi lawmakers explicitly codify a statutory clause in modern bankruptcy legislation exempting destitute merchants from liquidation. Further reforms advocate adopting unified insolvency frameworks (similar to English and German models) that apply consistent standards across merchant and non-merchant debtors alike.

What primary statutory laws and Sharia sources govern commercial insolvency in Saudi Arabia?

Which Saudi judicial bodies hold jurisdiction over commercial bankruptcy and insolvency verification?
Commercial Courts () hold jurisdiction over commercial bankruptcy filings, while Execution Courts () verify individual insolvency ().

Who authored the primary source study on Saudi commercial insolvent debtor policy?
Dr. Abdulhamid Aldisti Abdulhamid Shalabi (), Assistant Professor of Commercial Law at Umm Al-Qura University.

What is the difference between a Muflis and a Mu'sir in Saudi policy?
A Muflis owns assets that fail to cover total liabilities, making them subject to court liquidation. A Mu'sir is an assetless debtor who is protected from bankruptcy declarations and granted time under Sharia rules.

What substantive conditions must be met for a commercial bankruptcy declaration?
(a) The debtor must hold legal merchant status; (b) The debtor must possess full legal capacity; (c) Obligations must be verified commercial debts; (d) An asset pool must exist for distribution.

What legislative gap resulted from the Sharia Civil Procedure Law of 1435H?
Royal Decree M/1 of 1435H repealed Article 489 of the Commercial Court Law—which barred bankrupting destitute debtors—without enacting an explicit replacement clause, creating a statutory gap.

How does establishing insolvency affect execution imprisonment under Saudi law?
Article 84 of the KSA Execution Law strictly prohibits the execution imprisonment of a debtor once their insolvency () is legally verified.

What reform is recommended for the Saudi bankruptcy system?
Re-codifying an explicit statutory exemption for destitute merchants in the modern Bankruptcy Law and unifying merchant and non-merchant insolvency rules under a cohesive framework.

What binding principle was established by the Board of Grievances in Decision No. 139/T/4 of 1409H?
The Commercial Audit Panel ruled that "a destitute debtor cannot be declared bankrupt" ().
