'''Editorial: Killing the Orphans''' is a legal editorial written by [[Jeremy Phillips]] and published in the [[Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice]] (Vol. 2, No. 10) by [[Oxford University Press]] in 2007 [[Editorial Publication]] [1, 4, 5].

== Summary ==
This editorial analyzes the legal and economic dilemma surrounding the use of orphan works — works whose copyright owner cannot be identified or traced [[Orphan Works]] [6]. The author compares the concept of orphan drugs in patent law with orphan works in copyright law, and proposes a voluntary Code of Practice for the media industry as an interim solution [[Media Code of Practice]] [2, 4, 5].

== Legal Basis ==
* '''Patent Law (Orphan Drugs)''': The US and European legal regime that grants an extended period of commercial exclusivity to developers of drugs for rare diseases [[Orphan Drugs]] [2].
* '''Copyright Law (Orphan Works)''': The legal framework prohibiting the use of a copyrighted work without the rights holder's permission, which creates infringement liability risk for users of orphan works [[Copyright Infringement Liability]] [3, 6].

== Background ==
Many cultural and commercial industries (such as museums, archives, and filmmakers) face obstacles when they wish to reproduce or distribute older works whose copyright owners have disappeared [[Orphan Works Background]] [6].

== Objectives ==
# To explain the differing impact of the "orphan" concept between patent law and copyright law [[Impact of the Orphan Concept]] [2, 6].
# To assess the effectiveness of official licensing schemes and voluntary registration schemes in resolving the orphan works problem [[Scheme Effectiveness]] [3, 8].
# To propose a practical mechanism for the media industry to make lawful and fair use of orphan works [[Practical Mechanism]] [4, 5].

== Key Provisions ==
* '''Orphan Drugs vs. Orphan Works Analogy''': In patent law, orphan drugs are granted market exclusivity as an incentive [2]. By contrast, in copyright law, orphan works create legal fear for otherwise law-abiding users [6].
* '''United States & Canada Approaches''': The US proposed limiting sanctions for unauthorized users, while Canada has operated an official licensing scheme that has proven to be very rarely used [[US and Canada Schemes]] [3].
* '''Press Industry Stance''': The newspaper and periodical industry has taken a practical approach, continuing to use orphan works because the risk of detection or legal action is very low [[Press Industry Stance]] [7].
* '''Weakness of Voluntary Registration''': A voluntary registration scheme is ineffective because many creators of minor works (such as holiday photographs) will never think to register them [[Voluntary Registration]] [8].

== Implementation ==
* '''Official Publication''': Published in the *Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice*, Vol. 2, No. 10, pp. 633–634, DOI: 10.1093/jiplp/jpm170, by Oxford University Press [[Publication Detail]] [4, 5].

== Policy Issues ==
* '''Global Legal Uncertainty''': The absence of clear direction from governments or international bodies leaves commercial users operating in legal uncertainty [[Global Uncertainty]] [4].
* '''Risk of Paralysis in Cultural Industries''': Museums and archives are unable to make full use of their historical collections for educational or limited commercial purposes [[Paralysis of Cultural Industries]] [6].

== Reform ==
* Voluntary adoption of a Code of Practice by the media and entertainment sector, based on the principle of "practising use but providing for payment" of royalties [4, 5].

Fact / Interpretation / Inference Distinction

  • FACT:
    • The editorial "Killing the Orphans" was written by Jeremy Phillips (Editor, JIPLP) and published in 2007 in the Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice, Vol. 2, No. 10, pp. 633–634, DOI: 10.1093/jiplp/jpm170, by Oxford University Press [1].
    • In US and European patent law, the orphan drug concept grants an extended period of commercial exclusivity to drug developers for rare diseases, making the research investment financially viable [2].
    • In copyright law, an orphan work is a work whose copyright owner cannot be identified or traced [6].
    • Law-abiding commercial users (such as the film industry, museums, and archives) face obstacles in exploiting orphan works because of the risk of copyright infringement [3][6].
    • The United States proposed a compromise reducing sanctions for unauthorized users, while Canada has operated an official licensing scheme that has proven to be rarely used [3].
    • The newspaper and periodical sector has taken a practical stance, continuing to use orphan works because the copyright owners have generally disappeared and no protective organization (a "Society of Orphan Works Owners") exists [7].
    • A voluntary registration scheme is considered ineffective because many creators of minor works (such as holiday photographs or pottery) never think to register them [8].
    • The author recommends adopting a voluntary Code of Practice for the media and entertainment sector, based on the principle of "practising use but providing for payment" [4][5].
  • INTERPRETATION:
    • The absence of clear guidance from governments or international bodies forces the commercial world to operate in a "murky half-world of legal uncertainty" [4].
    • Using the human-orphan analogy has succeeded in stirring public emotion in the copyright debate, even though the analogy does not fully align with the commercial exploitation of orphan works [1][6].
  • INFERENCE:
    • If international bodies do not soon establish a global legal framework for orphan works, the media industry will remain divided between parties paralyzed by legal fear and parties that systematically disregard the rights of missing owners [4].

Policy Analysis Framework

  • Background: The inability of commercial users and cultural institutions to trace the copyright owners of historical works, archives, or old photographs (orphan works) [6].
  • Problem: Commercial use of orphan works risks triggering infringement claims, thereby hindering economic exploitation and academic/educational publication [6].
  • Legal/Policy Gap: The absence of a globally agreed legal framework, whether from an international body or government, to legitimize the use of orphan works [4].
  • Objectives: To provide legal certainty for users of orphan works while ensuring fair protection and royalty payment should a rights holder later come forward [3][5].
  • Implementation: A comparison between the US scheme (reduced sanctions), the Canadian scheme (an official but rarely used licensing system), and voluntary registration [3][8].
  • Evidence: The official publication in JIPLP Vol. 2, No. 10, pp. 633–634 (DOI: 10.1093/jiplp/jpm170), and practices across the film, museum, archive, and newspaper industries [5].
  • Impact: Legal fear paralyzes law-abiding sectors (museums/archives), while the press sector disregards licensing procedures due to the low risk of sanction [6][7].
  • Reform: Adoption of a voluntary Code of Practice for the media and entertainment industry, permitting use of a work while setting aside funds for royalty payment ("practising use but providing for payment") [4][5].
  • Unresolved Issues: The lack of any association representing orphan works owners (a "Society of Orphan Works Owners"), and the inability of voluntary registration to capture minor, everyday works [7][8].
