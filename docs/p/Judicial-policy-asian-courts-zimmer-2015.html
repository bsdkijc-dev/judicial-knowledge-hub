The Comparative Asian Judicial Systems Policy Framework is a comparative judicial governance policy framework reviewed by Markus Zimmer (Executive Editor of the IJCA, 2015) based on the book *Asian Courts in Context*, edited by Jiunn-Rong Yeh and Wen-Chen Chang (National Taiwan University, Cambridge University Press, 2015). It maps the institutional evolution, structure, and judicial administrative reforms across 14 Asian countries/economies (5 advanced and 9 rapidly developing) in response to globalization, the legacy of colonialism, and the dynamics of customary and religious laws [2–8, 16].

Summary

Published by Markus Zimmer in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework analyzes the 600+ page comparative anthology by Jiunn-Rong Yeh and Wen-Chen Chang. The study encompasses 14 Asian judicial systems divided into two groups: advanced economies (Hong Kong, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Taiwan) and rapidly developing economies (Bangladesh, China, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Mongolia, Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam). The policy highlights how Asian nations accelerated judicial modernization and established specialized business forums to gain the trust of global investors. Furthermore, the review uncovers the challenges of legal pluralism (the blending of common law, civil law, Sharia, and Hindu Shastra), the fragmentation of forums resulting in a 30-million case backlog in India, and the dynamics of judicial independence versus political indoctrination in China [8, 10, 13–15].

Legal Basis

  • Act on Basic Provisions of Judicial Power (1964): The legal foundation for the establishment of Indonesia's post-colonial national judicial system.
  • Asian Constitutions: The official promulgation of the Constitutions of China (1947), Japan (1947), South Korea (1948), India (1950), Singapore (1965), the Philippines (1987), and Mongolia (1992).
  • Taiwan Fair and Speedy Trial Act (2010): Regulations limiting endless cycles of criminal retrials.
  • Bengal Regulation (1772) & Singapore Constitutional Amendments (1971, 1979, 1993): The legal bases for appointing religious advisors in India and Judicial Commissioners in Singapore [10–11].
  • Not found in the available sources: Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text [FACT].

Background

The economic rise of Asia has driven nations across the continent to restructure their judicial systems. Unlike Western judiciaries that evolved slowly over centuries, Asian judiciaries were shaped rapidly post-decolonization to support global economic growth [2, 6–7].

Objectives

1. Build the trust of the international investment community through specialized commercial, intellectual property, and maritime courts. 2. Fulfill domestic social justice needs through specialized religious, labor, tax, and family courts. 3. Map the interactions between Western colonial law and local customary and religious laws across Asia.

Scope

This policy covers 14 Asian countries/economies situated in East Asia, Southeast Asia, and South Asia.

Key Provisions

  • Specialization of Business and Social Judicial Forums:
    • Establishing specialized forums (such as the *Obscene Articles Tribunal* in Hong Kong) to handle highly specific issues [5–6].
  • Temporary Judge Mechanisms in Singapore:
    • Appointing *supernumerary judges* and *judicial commissioners* from the ranks of legal practitioners to overcome judge shortages [10–11].
  • Limitation of Retrials in Taiwan:
    • Halting endless cycles of appeals and remands through the *Fair and Speedy Trial Act 2010*.
  • The Pattern of 5-Year Reform Plans in China:
    • A shift from judicial professionalism (2nd Plan, 2004–2008) toward emphasizing party leadership and social harmony (3rd Plan, 2009) [14–15].

Implementation

  • Publication of the Anthology (2015): The compilation of 14 scientific articles by Jiunn-Rong Yeh and Wen-Chen Chang published by Cambridge University Press [2–4].
  • Integration of Customary/Religious Law: The use of *pandit* and *kazi* advisors within the colonial Indian judicial system up until the 1860s.

Policy Problems

  • Backlog of 30 Million Cases in India: Overlapping jurisdictions across 4 tiers of judicial forums without unifying legislation [11–13].
  • Politicization of the Judiciary: The appointment of a party official without a formal legal background as the Chief Justice in China in 2008 [14–15].

Reform / Change

  • Unification of connecting legislation to eliminate overlapping jurisdictions [12–13].
  • Restricting the duration of criminal trials to guarantee the right to a speedy trial.
