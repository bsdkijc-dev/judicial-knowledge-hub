'''"In with the INN-crowd"''' is a legal editorial written by [[Jeremy Phillips]], editor of
the ''[[Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice]]'', published by
[[Oxford University Press]] in Vol. 2, No. 4, p. 191, DOI: 10.1093/jiplp/jpm003, released in
Advance Access on 1 March 2007.

== Summary ==
The editorial discusses the [[International Nonproprietary Names]] (INN) system, run by the
[[World Health Organization]] to give pharmaceutical substances a generic name that cannot be
monopolised as a trade mark — for example ''paracetamol'', alongside brand names such as
Tylenol, Panadol and Calpol. Outside pharmaceuticals there is no equivalent internationally
agreed word reserve, so the same term can be treated as monopolisable trade mark property in
one country and as generic common property in another. Phillips illustrates the problem with
the naming controversy between [[Apple Inc.]] and [[Cisco Systems]] over the word "iPhone,"
and proposes an INN-style approach — reserving common prefix-plus-descriptor combinations such
as "e-" or "i-" plus a generic noun — as a way to avoid this kind of dispute in technology
branding generally.

== The INN System ==
The World Health Organization administers the International Nonproprietary Names scheme,
assigning simple generic names to pharmaceutical substances — reducing something like
N-acetyl-p-aminophenol to "paracetamol" — which by design cannot be registered as a trade
mark, and which blocks confusingly similar terms from being monopolised for pharmaceutical
goods. Manufacturers remain free to market the same substance under their own trade marks.
''ID:'' `JPW-POLICY-WHO-INN-SYSTEM`

The World Health Organization and the World Intellectual Property Organization are both
specialised agencies of the United Nations system; the source's description of them as
"sibling" bodies is retained here as a general characterisation of that shared UN affiliation,
not as a claim of any formal institutional relationship between the two.

== The 'e-' and 'i-' Prefix Trend ==
The source attributed the rise of "e-" (electronic) and "i-" (information) prefixed terms —
email, e-commerce, iTunes, iPhone — to the establishment of CONTU (the US National Commission
on New Technological Uses of Copyrighted Works) in the 1970s. CONTU was a US body convened to
study copyright questions raised by computing and photocopying; it had no role in, and no
established connection to, either naming convention. The "e-" prefix's commercial currency
dates from the growth of electronic commerce in the mid-1990s, and the "i-" prefix is
generally associated with Apple's own branding beginning with the iMac (1998) and continuing
through iBook, iTunes, iPod and iPhone. ''ID:'' `JPW-CLAIM-PREFIX-ORIGIN` — '''CORRECTED'''

== The Apple–Cisco "iPhone" Naming Controversy ==
Cisco Systems held a prior US trade mark registration for "iPhone," acquired through its 2000
purchase of Infogear Technology Corporation, which had used the mark since the 1990s for a
internet telephony device, and later reinforced through Cisco's Linksys division. When Apple
announced its own iPhone on 9 January 2007, a naming conflict arose. The companies reached a
settlement on 21 February 2007 — before this editorial's Advance Access publication on
1 March 2007 — under which both were permitted to use the "iPhone" name, with an agreement to
explore product interoperability. The source described this as an unresolved dispute over
trade mark rights without noting that a settlement had already been reached by the time of
publication. ''ID:'' `JPW-EVENT-IPHONE-NAMING-DISPUTE-2006-07` — '''CORRECTED'''

== The INN-Style Proposal ==
Phillips's suggestion is that policymakers or WIPO could adopt an INN-like mechanism for
technology terms — reserving combinations of an indicative prefix ("e-" or "i-") with a
generic descriptor ("-phone," "-mail") as unmonopolisable common terms, similarly to how a
substance name is reserved in pharmaceuticals — while leaving room for genuine brand names
that carry real goodwill (Apple, Sony, Cisco) to remain protectable.

== Policy Issues ==
* '''Absence of a shared word reserve''' — outside pharmaceuticals, no international
  mechanism exists to keep common descriptive technology terms in the public domain across
  jurisdictions.
* '''Risk to genuine brand equity''' — any INN-style reservation scheme would need to avoid
  sweeping in terms that have already acquired real distinctiveness and goodwill, as opposed
  to purely descriptive prefix-plus-noun combinations.
* '''Institutional gap''' — no body outside WHO's health mandate currently has the standing
  or the mechanism to negotiate an equivalent word reserve for technology terms.

== Interpretation and Inference ==
* '''Interpretation''' — terms such as "iPhone," "iTunes" and "iTools" carry both a
  descriptive flavour and a brand allusion to a youthful, technology-oriented user identity
  associated with Apple's product line.
* '''Inference''' — applying an INN-style reservation to prefix-plus-descriptor technology
  terms could pre-empt disputes like the Apple–Cisco naming controversy without weakening
  protection for genuine brand names. This is Phillips's own proposal, not an adopted policy
  or a position taken by WHO or WIPO.

4. Fact / Interpretation / Inference Separation

Facts (attested or on the public record)

  1. "In with the INN-crowd" was written by Jeremy Phillips and published in JIPLP Vol. 2, No. 4, p. 191, DOI 10.1093/jiplp/jpm003, by Oxford University Press, released in Advance Access on 1 March 2007.
  2. The WHO's International Nonproprietary Names system assigns generic pharmaceutical names that cannot be registered as trade marks, alongside manufacturers' own brand names for the same substance.
  3. Outside pharmaceuticals, no equivalent internationally agreed word reserve exists, so the same term's legal status as monopolisable or generic can differ by jurisdiction.
  4. Cisco held a prior US trade mark registration for "iPhone"; Apple announced its own iPhone product on 9 January 2007; the two companies settled the resulting naming dispute on 21 February 2007, before this editorial's publication.

Interpretation — terms like "iPhone" and "iTunes" carry both descriptive content and brand-identity allusion for Apple's target user base.

Inference — an INN-style reservation scheme for prefix-plus-descriptor technology terms could reduce disputes like the Apple–Cisco naming controversy without weakening protection for genuinely distinctive brand names.

7. Improvements Applied

1. Corrected the CONTU attribution for the "e-"/"i-" naming trend. The source claimed the prefix trend "developed since the establishment of CONTU in the 1970s." CONTU — the US National Commission on New Technological Uses of Copyrighted Works — studied copyright questions raised by computing and photocopying and had no connection to product-naming conventions. The "e-" prefix's popularity tracks the growth of electronic commerce in the 1990s; the "i-" prefix is generally traced to Apple's own branding starting with the iMac in 1998. Presenting CONTU as the cause of a naming trend it had nothing to do with is a significant factual error, now flagged and corrected.

2. Corrected the framing of the Apple–Cisco "iPhone" dispute as unresolved. The source described this as a live dispute over trade mark rights. In fact Cisco held the prior US registration (via its 2000 acquisition of Infogear Technology Corporation), Apple announced its iPhone on 9 January 2007, and the companies settled — with both permitted to use the name — on 21 February 2007, roughly a week before this editorial's own Advance Access publication on 1 March 2007. Presenting a settled matter as an open conflict misdates the example the whole proposal is built around.

3. Verification status per entity, with named entities kept as addressable nodes and no fabricated relationships. The blanket [1–7] citation marker was dropped, including from the statement that two people were absent from those sources, which a citation cannot evidence. Cecep Mustafa and Rosianna Maretha Hasibuan are retained by name with the same permanent IDs used across all six editorial packages, with UNVERIFIED status and no asserted role.
