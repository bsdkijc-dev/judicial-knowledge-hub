International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA) Vol. 2 No. 1 is an official academic and professional journal, published electronically by the International Association for Court Administration (IACA), to facilitate international exchange on judicial governance, caseflow management, public trust, parliamentary oversight, court specialization, and delegation of judicial tasks.

Summary

This issue presents global judicial policy analysis covering the bridge of public trust in the Netherlands, criminal caseflow management in Kenosha County (Wisconsin), court management and output-based budgeting in the Netherlands, supervisory control and controlling systems at the Swiss Federal Supreme Court, the advantages and drawbacks of specialized courts, and the *European Rechtspfleger* Green Paper for the European Union.

Legal Basis

  • Not found in the available sources: The journal operates under the editorial policy of IACA's Executive Board and is not established under any specific statutory legislative instrument.
  • American Service-Members' Protection Act (ASPA 2002): A US law restricting cooperation with the International Criminal Court (ICC) [10–13].
  • Articles 168, 169, 188, & 191c of the Swiss Federal Constitution: The legal basis for parliamentary oversight and the courts' self-governing autonomy.
  • Council of Europe Recommendation No. R (86) 12: Guidance on reducing excessive court workloads [320–322].

Background

Judicial institutions worldwide face pressure to improve operational efficiency, manage case delays, respond to media coverage, and maintain budgetary accountability without sacrificing judicial independence.

Objectives

1. Facilitate international exchange of best practices in court administration across common law, civil law, and Shari'ah systems. 2. Provide practical solutions for reducing delays in criminal caseflow [68–71]. 3. Build transparent output-based budgeting models and judicial controlling systems [88–89, 196–198]. 4. Promote delegation of routine judicial tasks to *Rechtspfleger* at the EU level [315–317].

Scope

Topics covered include analysis of the public-vs-judicial punitiveness gap in the Netherlands, the Kenosha County caseflow workshop, the role of the court president, ten elements of good court management in Switzerland, a taxonomy of specialized courts in the US and Europe, and the *European Rechtspfleger* qualification framework.

Key Provisions

  • Media Logic vs. Legal Logic Gap: Public assessment of criminal sentencing is shaped by media dramatization ("media logic"), while judges rely on legal rational argument [35–37].
  • Kenosha Caseflow Recommendations: Prosecutor–public defender pre-trial meetings one week before the pre-trial hearing, limits on PSI reports, and use of videoconferencing [79–80].
  • Output-Based Budgeting: Financing of Dutch courts based on weighted caseload output (e.g., a three-judge panel case weighted at 1,052 minutes per judge) [88–89].
  • Swiss Three-Tier Controlling System: Division of public data (Annual Report, Level 1), parliamentary committee data (Level 2), and internal management data (Level 3) [196–201].
  • European Rechtspfleger Standard: An independent civil servant holding a Bachelor of Laws degree who handles routine judicial tasks to relieve judges' workloads [315–319].

Implementation

Implementation occurs through periodic journal publication, caseflow management workshops, resource agreements in Switzerland, and submission of the EUR Green Paper to the European Commission and CEPEJ in December 2008 [70–71, 184–186, 324–327].

Policy Problems

1. A gap in public perception caused by sensationalized media coverage that damages the judiciary's image [17–20, 35–37]. 2. Buildup of criminal case backlogs due to insufficient communication among law enforcement agencies [67–68, 74–78]. 3. Potential political interference by parliament through budgetary oversight of the judiciary [85–86, 128, 134–136]. 4. Risk of judicial isolation and "boundary law" arising from the excessive creation of specialized courts [235–238].

Reform / Change

Promotes a shift from passive judicial bureaucracy toward professional strategic leadership, automation of caseflow data, transparent public communication, and modernization of judges' support tasks [95–101, 121–124, 315–317].
