The ECtHR Judicial Performance Measurement Policy Framework is a governance and judicial performance evaluation framework concerning the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR). Formulated through the critical research of Elizabeth Lambert Abdelgawad (2017) in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), Vol. 8 No. 2, the framework evaluates the ECtHR's paradigm shift from individual human rights protection (pro victima) toward a quantitative, Results-Based Management (RBM/RBB) orientation. This managerial shift prioritizes efficiency, case disposal quotas, and the rapid rejection of single applications by single judges. The policy model identifies the negative impacts of tightening quantitative criteria, notably the erosion of the right of individual application (Article 34 of the ECHR), the decline of judicial legitimacy, and the neglect of qualitative standards regarding judicial reasoning [1–35].

Summary

Published by Elizabeth Lambert Abdelgawad (2017) in the IJCA, this policy framework critiques the application of the Results-Based Budgeting (RBB) managerial model at the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) [1–3, 6–7]. To overcome a massive case backlog (peaking at 151,000 pending cases in 2011) amid the Council of Europe's zero-growth budget policy, the ECtHR adopted a single-judge filtering mechanism under Protocol 14 and tightened the formal requirements of Rule 47. While these reforms successfully increased the quantitative output of case rejections—with 95% of applications declared inadmissible and 90% of those decided by a single judge without legal reasoning (unreasoned decisions)—Abdelgawad asserts that this obsession with quantitative productivity has eroded the constitutional right of individual petition (Article 34 ECHR) and blatantly ignored the qualitative justice criteria developed by the CEPEJ.

Legal Basis

  • European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR): Article 19 (the Court's mission to ensure the observance of the Convention), Article 6 (Right to a Fair Trial), Article 13 (Right to an Effective Remedy), and Article 34 (Right of Individual Application).
  • Protocol No. 14 to the ECHR: Provisions establishing the Single-Judge mechanism for the rapid rejection of clearly inadmissible applications.
  • Rule 47 of the Rules of Court: Court regulations imposing strict formal requirements for lodging new applications.
  • Intergovernmental Declarations (Interlaken 2010, Brighton 2012, Brussels 2015): Political agreements among state parties directing the managerial reform of the ECtHR.
  • Council of Europe Budget Rules (Results-Based Budgeting / RBB): The results-based budget management framework implemented since 1999–2000.
  • Not found in the available sources: Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text [FACT].

Background

The explosion of individual petitions following the expansion of the Council of Europe into Eastern Europe was not matched by budget increases. The Court became a "victim of its own success," leading major donor states to demand strict managerial accountability and the aggressive slashing of operational costs.

Objectives

1. Overcome the severe backlog crisis in Strasbourg. 2. Meet stringent productivity and efficiency targets under the Results-Based Budgeting (RBB) scheme. 3. Develop a balanced performance measurement framework that integrates both quantitative and qualitative indicators of justice.

Scope

This policy covers the evaluation of judicial governance and case administration at the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) for the 47 member states of the Council of Europe.

Key Provisions

  • Single-Judge Filtering Mechanism:
    • The authority of a single judge to rapidly disqualify inadmissible applications without a contradictory process.
  • Priority Policy:
    • Categorizing cases based on urgency (Categories I–III for core rights and matters of public interest).
  • Tightening of Rule 47 Formal Requirements:
    • The automatic dismissal of applications if they fail to meet the strict administrative requirements set by the Court.
  • Integration of Qualitative Justice Criteria:
    • The necessity of providing brief legal reasoning for rejections and conducting regular applicant satisfaction surveys.

Implementation

  • Operationalization of Protocol 14 (2011–2015): The processing of hundreds of thousands of applications by single-judge formations, significantly reducing the backlog.
  • Application of Brief Reasoning (2016): The adoption of formal judicial decisions with brief reasoning to replace registry letters for single-judge rejections, following the Brussels Declaration.

Policy Problems

  • Erosion of Individual Petition Rights: The transformation of the ECtHR from a victim-protecting court into an abstract standard-setting body.
  • Neglect of Quality and Transparency: The absence of adequate legal aid and the lack of judicial reasoning in mass rejections, triggering a severe crisis of legitimacy among applicants and lawyers.

Reform / Change

  • Adopting CEPEJ qualitative criteria and conducting periodic applicant satisfaction surveys.
  • Establishing Information Offices in the countries that contribute the most cases to filter and improve applications before they reach the Court.
  • Pressuring member states to fully execute leading judgments at the domestic level to stem the flood of repetitive cases [32–33].
