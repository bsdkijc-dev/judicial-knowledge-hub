'Framework for Development and Evaluation of Community Engagement' (Haslett et al., 2010)

Wiki Entity Page

The Framework for Development and Evaluation of Community Engagement at the Neighbourhood Justice Centre is a policy framework, a five-phase developmental model, and a community engagement evaluation model implemented at the Neighbourhood Justice Centre (NJC) in the City of Yarra, Melbourne, Australia [1–4, 18–20].

Summary

Published by Tim Haslett, Chris Ballenden, Louise Bassett, Saroj Godbole, and Kerry Walker in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework formulates the governance and evaluation of community engagement at Australia's first multi-jurisdictional community court. Utilizing a systems thinking approach (Causal Loop Diagrams - CLD), the framework maps the shift in resource allocation across five developmental phases to demonstrate the effectiveness of crime prevention and restorative justice interventions to the government.

Legal Basis

  • Department of Justice Victoria Policy Framework (2007): The Neighbourhood Justice Centre: Community Justice in Action in Victoria.
  • UK Ministry of Justice Report (2009): Engaging Communities in Criminal Justice (8 Principles of Community Justice).
  • Victoria Multi-jurisdictional Statutes: The consolidation of authority from the Magistrates' Court, Children's Court (Criminal Division), Victims of Crime Assistance Tribunal (VOCAT), and Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (VCAT).
  • Comparative Public Participation Models: Ladder of Citizen Participation (Arnstein, 1969), IAP2 Spectrum, and Varieties of Participation (Fung, 2006) [4–8].

Background

The NJC was established in 2007 to create a justice system that is more integrated, responsive, accessible, and effective in addressing the root causes of criminal behavior in the City of Yarra. However, after four years of operation, the NJC faced challenges, including the absence of a single agreed-upon definition of community engagement and demands from funding authorities for accountability in measuring qualitative impacts.

Objectives

1. Maximize the capacity of the Yarra community to prevent and manage the impacts of crime. 2. Enhance conflict resolution through Appropriate Dispute Resolution (ADR) and restorative justice. 3. Provide efficient, transparent, and inclusive judicial services at the local level. 4. Function as an "incubator" to transfer community justice practices to the mainstream justice system.

Scope

This policy applies to all NJC operations in the City of Yarra, Melbourne, encompassing four multi-jurisdictional judicial bodies, professional communities (Client Services / rehabilitation service providers), and local communities (Neighbourhood Houses and public housing estate committees).

Key Provisions

  • Three Dimensions of Integrated Justice: Integrating community justice, restorative justice, and therapeutic jurisprudence.
  • Two-Way Process: Community engagement evolves from the initial outreach of the court to the active participation of the community in determining how justice is administered.
  • Problem Solving Conferences: Deliberation sessions involving stakeholders and defendants to formulate recovery agreements as alternatives to imprisonment.
  • Respected Community Members Panels: Conflict resolution skills training for community leaders to proactively extinguish potential ethnic clashes.

Implementation (Five Phases of Development)

1. Phase 1 (Establishing the NJC Concept): Establishing initial contact with professional and local community organizations. 2. Phase 2 (Development of Communities): Organizing Problem Solving Conferences and formally engaging the community [24–26]. 3. Phase 3 (Independence and Resilience of Local Communities): Building the resilience and independent governance of local partner organizations. 4. Phase 4 (Systemic and Policy Related Interventions): Collaborating on crime prevention policies with Victoria Police, local government, universities, and the Office of Housing. 5. Phase 5 (Change within the Justice System): Disseminating NJC's best practices throughout the Victoria justice system.

Policy Problems

  • Difficulty in Qualitative Measurement: Crime prevention and community building activities are difficult to evaluate using conventional quantitative metrics.
  • Shifts in Political Support: A change in the Victoria government in 2010 resulted in the loss of its primary sponsor (Attorney General Rob Hulls) and brought demands to accelerate the transfer of practices to mainstream courts.

Reform / Change

  • Adoption of a developmental evaluation questions matrix at the end of each phase [23, 27–28, 31, 34, 38].
  • Institutionalization of systems thinking (Causal Loop Diagrams) to transparently manage the shifting allocation of resources.
