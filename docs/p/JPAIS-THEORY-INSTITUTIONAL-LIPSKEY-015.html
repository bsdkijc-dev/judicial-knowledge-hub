1. Regulatory Framework and Institutional Isomorphism

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: In Japan, in-court litigation settlement (Wakai dalam litigasi) is governed by the Code of Civil Procedure (CCP Art. 89, 264, 265, 267), granting Wakai protocols the exact same executorial force (kekuatan yang setara dengan putusan BHT) as a final, non-appealable court judgment. Civil Conciliation (Chōtei Perdata) is governed by a detailed special statute, the Civil Conciliation Act (UUCP / Minji Chōtei Hō Act No. 222/1951), establishing Conciliation Committees (Majelis Chōtei) comprising 1 Judge and at least 2 Lay Commissioners (Art. 5 & 6). The Act provides centralized jurisdiction for Intellectual Property Chōtei at Tokyo and Osaka District Courts (Art. 33-4), mandates pre-trial conciliation for family and land/building rent disputes (Art. 24-2), authorizes litigation remand to Chōtei with trial suspension (Art. 20 & 20-3), and empowers courts to issue ex-officio Orders in Lieu of Conciliation (Penetapan Pengganti Chōtei) under Article 17, which become final binding settlements if no party objects within 14 days (Art. 18, recognizing implicit/passive consent).

[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: In Institutional Theory, this dual statutory framework exerts coercive isomorphism over litigating parties. By embedding explicit statutory mechanisms—especially Article 17 orders subject to passive consent—the regulatory system prevents recalcitrant litigants from holding resolution hostage through pure inaction.

[ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS]: In the Indonesian reform context (RaPerMA / PERMA No. 1 of 2016 / JPAIS KCCP), court mediation suffers from institutional decoupling—where mediation is treated as a routine checklist item (formalitas belaka) before trial. Replicating the Japanese success requires institutionalizing 3-member Tripartite Conciliation Panels (Majelis Konsiliasi Tripartit) and introducing judicial Orders in Lieu of Settlement (Penetapan Pengganti Perdamaian) subject to a 14-day objection window to bridge the gap between formal procedure and substantive resolution.

2. Judicial / Institutional Structure and Organizational Capacity (5M Framework)

Applying the 5M Framework to Prof. Yamamoto's presentation illuminates the structural mechanics of judicial conciliation:

Man (Human Capital & Professional Roles)
[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Chōtei panels leverage 1 Judge (serving as panel chair) and minimum 2 Lay Commissioners (Anggota Majelis Chōtei) drawn from private sector legal, technical, and business backgrounds (Art. 5 & 6).

[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: In Organizational Sociology, pairing career judges with lay domain experts expands judicial organizational capacity without inflating the permanent judicial payroll, injecting specialized commercial knowledge directly into dispute evaluation.

Method (Procedural Methodology & Economic Model)
[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Prof. Yamamoto formulates the Economic Theory of Settlement:

PlaintiffExpectedGain = p1X − Z1

DefendantExpectedLoss = p2X + Z2

FeasibilityConditionforSettlement = Z1 + Z2 > (p1−p2)X

(where p1 = plaintiff's winning probability perception, p2 = defendant's losing probability perception, X = claim value, Z1, Z2 = litigation costs for plaintiff and defendant).

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: In Japan, litigation costs (Z1+Z2) are relatively low, making them a negative incentive for settlement compared to the US. Therefore, the primary driver of high settlement rates in Japan is minimizing the perception gap (p1−p2) → 0. This is achieved when an adjudicator/judge reveals their tentative judicial conviction (mengungkapkan keyakinan hakim) and presents a concrete settlement proposal reflecting predicted trial outcomes, aligning party expectations.

Material (Case Files & Procedural Protocols)
[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Wakai and Chōtei settlements are formally recorded in court protocols (Berita Acara), which carry immediate executorial force (CCP Art. 267 / UUCP Art. 16). Under 2022 CCP revisions, Wakai discussions can be conducted online via video conference.

Money (Trial Economy & DIPA Funding)
[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: Resolving cases via Wakai (31.6% in litigation) or Chōtei (60.2% success) delivers massive state budget savings by eliminating multi-tier appellate litigation and reducing judicial opinion drafting burdens.

Machine / Technology (Digital Case Systems)
[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: 2022 CCP revisions codified online procedures (CCP Art. 89), special written acceptance clauses (CCP Art. 264), and judge-determined settlement clauses (CCP Art. 265).

Legal Culture & Social Structure
[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Historically, Japanese judicial culture operated under the strict norm "Don't be a Wakai judge" ("Janganlah menjadi hakim Wakai"), believing a judge's sole duty was writing formal verdicts. This changed drastically in the 1970s due to an explosion of traffic accident and environmental pollution tort suits (perkara PMH), which caused severe court backlogs. Tokyo District Court's Traffic Division pioneered litigation Wakai, transforming judicial culture so that judges skilled in settlement are now highly evaluated.

Street-Level Bureaucracy vs. Judicial Discretion
[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: Applying Lipsky's Street-Level Bureaucracy framework requires distinguishing frontline court staff from judges:

  • Frontline Administrative Clerks (Panitera/Pegawai): Handle administrative intake, service of process, and tracking the 14-day objection clock under Article 18. Their discretion is purely procedural.
  • Judges (Hakim): Possess curial discretion and judicial independence. Judges exercise broad discretion regarding the timing and method of Wakai attempts (CCP Art. 89). During Chōtei and Wakai, judges exercise evaluative discretion by holding private caucuses (diskusi secara kaukus) and disclosing their judicial conviction (mengungkapkan keyakinan) to present concrete settlement proposals. To mitigate risks of information manipulation or party autonomy infringement, judges apply "Theory of Settlement Procedure" and "Theory of Settlement Technique" to structure creative, win-win solutions.

4. Implementation and Business Experience

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: In complex commercial litigation, Wakai serves as the primary resolution mechanism: 2024 data shows that litigation Wakai resolves 51.5% of medical malpractice suits and 62.2% of labor disputes. The average duration for litigation Wakai is 12.9 months (compared to 8.2 months for adjudicated judgments), demonstrating that complex commercial settlements undergo careful, thorough negotiation.

[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: For commercial enterprises operating in an economy, unexpected judicial rulings and protracted appellate delays represent severe business risks. By establishing clear judicial conviction disclosure, utilizing lay expert commissioners, and enforcing binding settlement deeds and Article 17 orders, the Japanese civil justice system provides high regulatory predictability, low administrative burden, and strong contract enforcement certainty.

C. Synthesis of Evidence vs. Epistemological Uncertainty

What is Firmly Supported by Source Evidence

  • Litigation Wakai (CCP Art. 89 & 267) carries full executorial force equivalent to a final court judgment (BHT).
  • Civil Chōtei (UUCP Act No. 222/1951) uses 3-member committees (1 Judge + 2 Lay Commissioners) under Articles 5 & 6.
  • UUCP Article 17 empowers courts to issue ex-officio Orders in Lieu of Conciliation (Penetapan Pengganti Chōtei), which become binding if no objection is filed within 14 days (Art. 18).
  • Japanese judicial culture shifted post-1970s from "Don't be a Wakai judge" to actively valuing settlement skill, driven by traffic and pollution tort backlogs.
  • In 2024, litigation Wakai resolved 31.6% of civil lawsuits (51.5% in medical, 62.2% in labor), while civil Chōtei achieved a 60.2% success rate across 30,209 cases with a 3.4-month average duration.
  • Prof. Yamamoto formulates the Economic Theory of Settlement, demonstrating that settlement feasibility depends on Z1 + Z2 > (p1−p2)X, where the primary driver in Japan is narrowing the perception gap (p1−p2) → 0 through judicial conviction disclosure.

D. Policy Recommendations for Indonesian Civil Procedure Reform (RaPerMA)

Based on Prof. Yamamoto's analysis of Japanese conciliation and economic settlement theory:

  1. Institutionalize Tripartite Conciliation Panels (Majelis Konsiliasi Tripartit): Amend Supreme Court Regulations (RaPerMA) to establish 3-member conciliation panels (1 Judge Mediator + 2 Non-Judge Expert Commissioners) for complex commercial, land, and property disputes exceeding Rp 500 million.
  2. Adopt Orders in Lieu of Settlement (Penetapan Pengganti Perdamaian): Codify an equivalent to Article 17 UUCP, authorizing panels to issue provisional evaluative settlement orders upon negotiation impasse, which convert into binding Settlement Deeds (Akta Perdamaian) if no party objects within 14 calendar days.
  3. Encourage Judicial Conviction Disclosure: Train judge mediators through BSDK Kumdil to actively disclose tentative judicial evaluations during court conciliation, narrowing party perception gaps (p1−p2) → 0 to drive rational economic settlement.
  4. Legitimize Digital Caucusing and Remote Settlement: Codify online video conference caucusing and digital settlement protocol execution within the e-Court framework.
