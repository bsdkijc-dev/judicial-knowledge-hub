4. POLICY ANALYSIS

Background: [FACT] Legal developments in Spain and the European Union in late 2019 were shaped by digital transformation and the need for jurisprudential certainty. Key issues included the protection of personal data and digital privacy in the workplace, the dynamics of competition markets affected by non-controlling minority shareholdings (partial ownership), debates over taxpayers' evidentiary rights during administrative revision, the structuring of court jurisdiction over cross-border insolvency, and the fight against bid-rigging cartels in public procurement.

Policy Problem: [FACT]

  1. Competition Law: The failure of the formal merger control system to account for the structural impact of non-controlling minority shareholding acquisitions, which can distort market competition.
  2. Tax Law: Frequent rejection by tax authorities of new taxpayer evidence during objection (recurso de reposición) or economic-administrative claim procedures, on grounds of lateness (extemporaneidad).
  3. Insolvency and International Jurisdiction: Conflict between the Spanish insolvency judge's principle of vis attractiva concursus and the procedure for recognition (exequatur) of foreign judgments/awards.
  4. Digital Labor Law: Blurred boundaries between employer monitoring and worker privacy rights in the use of digital devices, geolocation, and video surveillance, as well as the enforcement of the right to digital disconnection.
  5. Public Procurement: Financial losses to the state from bid-rigging cartels, which can raise average market prices by up to 20%.

Legal/Policy Gap: [FACT] The absence of an explicit rule in the General Tax Law limiting the deadline for submitting evidence during the revision stage; the lack of an automatic ex ante notification mechanism for minority shareholding acquisitions in the EU; and ambiguity in the role of soft-law instruments (internal company policies) in enforcing digital labor rights.

Objectives: [FACT] Guarantee the protection of fundamental rights and legal certainty (tutela judicial efectiva); combat unfair competition and bid-rigging collusion; affirm the principle of good administration and material truth in taxation; and maintain a balance between employer oversight authority and worker privacy rights.

Implementation: [FACT]

  • Spanish Supreme Court Doctrine (STS 10 September 2018 & 21 February 2019): Permitting the submission of new evidence during the economic-administrative revision stage, unless there is evidence of bad faith or abuse of rights (actitud abusiva o maliciosa) by the taxpayer.
  • Dissociation of Exequatur and Enforcement: Allowing separation between the procedure for recognizing (exequatur) a foreign judgment in an ordinary court (juzgado de primera instancia) and its enforcement, which must be handed over to the insolvency judge (juez del concurso).
  • LOPD 3/2018 (Arts. 87–90): Requiring companies to draft internal policies (políticas de empresa), after consulting or engaging with worker representatives, to regulate the use of digital devices, video surveillance, geolocation, and the right to disconnect.
  • Article 150.1 LCSP: Implementation of a summary procedure (procedimiento sumarísimo) and confidential coordination mechanism between procurement bodies and the CNMC to detect indications of bid rigging.

Evidence & Impact: [FACT] Enforcement of CJEU jurisprudence tightening restrictions on cookies (Planet49) and the right to be forgotten (Google Spain/CNIL); increased legal certainty for taxpayers submitting defenses; and strengthened deterrence against illegal public tender participants through sanctions and contracting bans.

Reform & Unresolved Issues: [FACT] Ongoing debate over the need for ex-ante control regulation of minority shareholding acquisitions in the EU; alignment with ECtHR doctrine (Barbulescu II and López Ribalda II) on the limits of workplace monitoring without prior notice; and the effectiveness of applying the mandatory working-hours registration scheme (registro de jornada) to senior staff and executives.

5. GEO/AI QUESTIONS

  1. Definition: What is the Pérez-Llorca Legal Journal (No. 2, December 2019)?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] A Spanish scholarly legal publication from the December 2019 edition, presenting doctrinal and jurisprudential analysis on CJEU technology and digital rights case law, competition merger control, late tax evidence submission, recognition of foreign judgments against insolvent companies, digital labor rights under the LOPD, and bid rigging in public procurement.
  2. Legal Basis: What is the main legal basis in Organic Law 3/2018 (LOPD-GDD) governing the right to digital disconnection in the Spanish workplace?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Article 88 of Organic Law 3/2018 (LOPD).
  3. Publisher/Authority: Who is the official publisher of this document, and what is its legal registration number?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Pérez-Llorca Abogados, S.L.P. y Cía., S. Com. P., Madrid (Legal Deposit: M-7551-2019).
  4. Related Figures: Which expert author prepared the analysis of collusion among bidders in public procurement (bid rigging)?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Beatriz García Gómez, Partner at Pérez-Llorca's Public Law and Litigation Department.
  5. Scope: What are the four main forms of bid-rigging schemes identified by the OECD?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Cover bidding, bid suppression, bid rotation, and market allocation.
  6. Policy Implementation: How does Spanish Supreme Court doctrine (STS 10 September 2018) govern the submission of new evidence during tax revision proceedings?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] The Supreme Court established that new evidence not submitted during the inspection/assessment stage must be accepted and evaluated at the economic-administrative revision stage, unless the taxpayer's conduct is proven to be abusive or malicious.
  7. Policy Problem: Why can non-controlling minority shareholding acquisitions (partial ownership) pose competition risks according to economic theory?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Because minority shareholdings alter the pricing incentives of the acquiring company, reduce the drive to compete aggressively, and can facilitate coordination or the exchange of sensitive information between competitors.
  8. Legal Relationship: What is the relationship between the principle of vis attractiva concursus (Arts. 8 & 11 of the Insolvency Law) and the recognition of foreign civil judgments under the LCJIC?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] International vis attractiva concursus applies only to actions based purely on insolvency law (the Gourdain Test). For ordinary civil judgments, recognition (exequatur) can be separated and processed in an ordinary court, while enforcement over the assets remains the exclusive authority of the insolvency judge.
  9. Reform: How does Article 150.1 of Law 9/2017 on Public Sector Contracts (LCSP) strengthen the handling of bid rigging?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] Article 150.1 LCSP introduces a summary procedure allowing confidential coordination between procurement bodies and the CNMC/competition authorities to investigate and act on indications of bid rigging.
  10. Evidence/Case Study: What sanctions can be imposed on a company for failing to provide prior notice regarding digital device monitoring under LISOS?
    • Factual Answer: [FACT] The violation is classified as very serious (infracción muy grave) under Article 8.11 of LISOS (Royal Legislative Decree 5/2000), with fines ranging from €6,251 to €187,515.

6. MEDIAWIKI OUTPUT

Summary

Pérez-Llorca Legal Journal (Issue No. 2, December 2019) is a major scholarly legal publication presenting a comprehensive analysis of developments in Spanish and EU jurisprudence and judicial policy. This edition contains six strategic doctrinal studies: an evaluation of CJEU jurisprudence on technology and the GDPR; the limits of the concept of competition control and minority shareholding participation (partial ownership); taxpayers' right to submit late evidence during administrative revision procedures; the recognition (exequatur) and enforcement of foreign judgments/awards against insolvent companies (concurso de acreedores); protection of digital labor rights (the right to disconnect, video surveillance, and geolocation) under LOPD 3/2018; and strategies for preventing and prosecuting bid rigging in public procurement.

Legal Basis

The policy and regulatory analysis in this publication is grounded in the following legal framework:

  • Spanish Constitution (CE): Article 20 (freedom of expression and academic freedom), Article 18 (right to privacy), and Article 31 (tax fairness).
  • Competition Law & Procurement: Regulation (EC) No. 139/2004, Law 15/2007 on the Defense of Competition, and Law 9/2017 on Public Sector Contracts (LCSP, Article 150.1).
  • Tax and Administrative Law: Law 58/2003 General Tax Law (LGT, Articles 105, 108, 236, 237) and Law 39/2015 on Common Administrative Procedure.
  • Insolvency Law & International Law: Law 22/2003 on Insolvency (Articles 8, 11), Law 29/2015 LCJIC (Articles 41–54), Regulation (EU) No. 1215/2012 (Brussels I Recast), and Regulation (EU) 2015/848 on Insolvency Proceedings.
  • Labor Law & Data Protection: Organic Law 3/2018 (LOPD-GDD, Articles 87–90), Royal Legislative Decree 2/2015 (Workers' Statute), and Royal Decree-Law 5/2000 (LISOS, Article 8.11).

Background

Spain's regulatory environment in late 2019 faced modernization challenges across multiple areas of the judiciary. The use of digital technology in employment relationships raised risks of worker privacy violations; tax authorities frequently rejected taxpayers' defensive evidence at the revision stage; competition transactions were hampered by a regulatory gap regarding minority shareholding acquisitions; and bid-rigging practices harmed public finances by as much as 20% of procurement contract value.

Objectives

1. Ensure that competition law enforcement evaluates the real economic impact of minority shareholding acquisitions. 2. Guarantee taxpayers' right to defense by accepting late-submitted evidence in pursuit of material truth. 3. Facilitate efficient recognition of foreign judgments without disrupting the certainty of national insolvency governance. 4. Provide legal certainty regarding the implementation of the right to digital disconnection and workplace privacy protection. 5. Combat public bid-rigging fraud through confidential coordination between procurement bodies and the CNMC.

Scope

The study covers six main areas of judicial policy: 1. Technology & EU Jurisprudence: CJEU case law on data retention, Planet49 cookie consent, and the territorial limits of the right to be forgotten. 2. Competition Law: Evaluation of the boundaries of sole/joint control and the collusive risks of passive/partial ownership. 3. Tax Law: Spanish Supreme Court doctrine on Article 236 LGT and the bad-faith (*malicia*) exception. 4. Cross-Border Insolvency: Dissociation of declarative exequatur and asset enforcement based on the Gourdain Test. 5. Digital Labor Rights: Internal company policies (*políticas de empresa*) for digital devices, video surveillance, geolocation, and disconnection. 6. Public Procurement: Typology of bid rigging, characteristics of oligopolistic markets, and the summary procedure under Article 150.1 LCSP.

Key Provisions

  • Admission of Late Tax Evidence: Economic-administrative revision bodies must accept and evaluate new taxpayer evidence unless there is clear proof that the taxpayer's conduct was abusive or malicious (STS 10 September 2018).
  • Dissociation of Exequatur: Recognition (exequatur) of a foreign judgment is declarative in nature and can be processed in an ordinary court; enforcement affecting insolvency assets is handed over entirely to the insolvency judge.
  • Mandatory Digital Disconnection Policy: Companies must draft an internal digital disconnection policy for all workers (including senior executives), after prior consultation with worker representatives.
  • Summary Procedure under the LCSP: Procurement bodies must confidentially report indications of bid rigging to the CNMC; this referral suspends the tender procedure without notifying participants.

Implementation

Implementation is carried out through enforcement of Spanish Supreme Court jurisprudence, TEAC rulings, Labor Inspectorate oversight of company privacy policies, and cartel investigations by the CNMC and regional competition authorities (such as ACCO).

Policy Problems

  • A regulatory gap in requiring ex-ante notification of non-controlling minority shareholding acquisitions in the EU.
  • A tendency by tax administrative bodies (TEAC) to try to limit the application of Supreme Court doctrine regarding new evidence.
  • Interpretive conflict between the Ministry of Labor's working-hours registration guidelines and the right to digital disconnection for senior staff.

Reform

  • Implementation of Organic Law 3/2018 (LOPD-GDD) to guarantee digital labor rights.
  • Transposition of Directive 2014/104/EU through Law 9/2017 (LCSP) to facilitate damages claims for competition law infringements.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • Implements: Organic Law 3/2018 (LOPD) & Law 9/2017 on Public Sector Contracts.
  • Applies To: Tax revision procedures under the LGT, recognition of foreign judgments under the LCJIC, and public procurement tenders.
  • Conflicts With: Automatic rejection of late evidence by tax authorities.
