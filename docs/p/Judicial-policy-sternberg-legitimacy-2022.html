The legitimacy-conferring capacity of constitutional courts: Evidence from a comparative survey experiment is an empirical comparative political science and judicial behavior research paper authored by Sebastian Sternberg (University of Mannheim), Sylvain Brouard (Sciences Po / CEVIPOF / CNRS), and Christoph Hönnige (Leibniz University Hannover), published in the European Journal of Political Research (EJPR) in 2021 (print edition Vol. 61, pages 973–996, November 2022).

Summary

This study examines whether a constitutional court's rulings can shift public opinion on a government policy (either conferring or stripping legitimacy from that policy), and tests the extent to which this effect depends on the court's level of institutional trust ("diffuse support"). Using a most-different-systems design comparing the German Federal Constitutional Court (GFCC, high public trust ~50%) and the French Constitutional Council (CC, moderate/low public trust ~28%), the study runs identical survey priming experiments on nationally representative samples from the German Internet Panel (N = 2,867) and the French National Election Study (N = 2,661). The findings show that a court with high public trust can significantly shift public opinion in the direction of its ruling, even among voters with strong pre-existing partisan attitudes [1, 2, 8, 42, 54, 58–59].

Background

Legitimacy is the primary source of power for constitutional courts, since courts lack any physical or executive means to enforce compliance with their rulings. The theory of "legitimacy-conferring capacity" (Dahl 1957; Easton 1965) holds that courts can draw on a "reservoir of goodwill" to drive public acceptance of government policy. However, earlier research focused almost exclusively on the U.S. Supreme Court and assumed courts are always among the most trusted institutions, whereas public trust in judiciaries varies widely across Europe.

Objectives

1. Test Hypothesis 1 (Aggregate Level): a constitutional court with higher diffuse support will shift public opinion more strongly in the direction of its ruling. 2. Test Hypothesis 2 (Pre-existing Partisan Attitudes): a high-trust court's legitimacy-conferring capacity is strong enough to shift opinion even among voters with strong prior attitudes. 3. Test Hypothesis 3 (Individual Level): respondents with higher trust in a court are more likely to follow its ruling than those with lower trust.

Scope

The research consists of survey priming experiments conducted on German and French populations regarding a hypothetical but credible "school security law," comparing the effect of a court ruling against that of national consultative control bodies.

Key Provisions / Empirical Findings

  • Hypothesis 1 Supported (German Court / GFCC): GFCC rulings significantly shift public opinion in the direction of the ruling (p < 0.05). GFCC disapproval increases the probability of public opposition by +5 percentage points (52% to 57%), while GFCC approval increases public agreement by +5 percentage points (34% to 39%).
  • Weak Effect for the French Court (CC): Endorsements by the French Constitutional Council move opinion in the expected direction, but the treatment effects are not statistically significant (p > 0.10) compared to the control group.
  • Hypothesis 2 Supported (Effect on Partisan Voters): GFCC approval reduces opposition to the bill among German Green Party voters by 8 percentage points (from 80% to 72%, p < 0.10). GFCC disapproval increases opposition among right-wing AfD voters by 15 percentage points (from 35% to 50%, p < 0.05). In France, CC rulings failed to move Socialist or National Rally voters.
  • Hypothesis 3 Supported (Pooled Individual-Level Model): Respondents with high trust in either court (Germany or France) showed a 6 percentage point opinion shift when exposed to a court disapproval treatment (p < 0.05), while low-trust respondents showed no significant change.

Implementation

The methodology was implemented through ordered probit regression models with Zelig-based probability simulations and partisanship interaction tests. The research was funded by the German Research Foundation (DFG / SFB 884) and Projekt DEAL.

Policy Issues

1. The limited ability of a low-trust constitutional court to serve as a legitimizer of controversial public policy. 2. The risk of judicial legitimacy erosion if a court issues a ruling that triggers backlash without sufficient reserves of public trust.

Reform / Implications

1. Constitutional courts must actively safeguard diffuse support and independence to preserve their authority to shape public opinion. 2. Policymakers cannot rely on the endorsement of a low-trust judicial body to boost public acceptance of unpopular legislation.
