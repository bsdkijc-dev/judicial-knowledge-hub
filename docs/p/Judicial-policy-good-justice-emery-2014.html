The Expectations of 'Good Justice' and Judicial Culture Policy Framework is a policy framework and empirical study of judicial culture formulated by Prof. Yves Emery and Lorenzo Gennaro De Santis (IDHEAP, University of Lausanne). It classifies 27 expectations of "good justice" in Switzerland based on the theoretical framework of Boltanski & Thévenot (1991) (Industrial, Civic, Commercial/Business, and Domestic Worlds) and analyzes the convergence and divergence of values between judges without managerial responsibilities and court managers in the context of New Public Management (NPM) and New Public Governance [1, 23–25, 27–36, 44].

Summary

Published in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA) Vol. 6 No. 1 (June 2014), this empirical research dissects Swiss judicial culture amidst the wave of New Public Management (NPM) reforms [1–2]. Based on 18 in-depth interviews across 10 courts in 7 cantons and 3 linguistic regions, the study identifies 27 expectations of "good justice" [21–23, 29–36]. The analysis reveals cultural hybridization where both judges and court managers agree on the paramount importance of speed (celerity) and client orientation, yet diverge significantly; judges focus on the human side of justice (humane/personalized), whereas managers emphasize functional systemic efficiency (pragmatic/efficacy) [29–37].

Legal Basis

  • Constitutional Principle of Judicial Independence (Swiss Federal Constitution / BV): The primary guarantee that judges work free from the influence of the executive, legislative, or external management pressures.
  • Swiss Procedural Codes Unification (Berne 2011): National procedural unification regulations that impact the internal organization of tribunals and the workload of judges.
  • Other Specific Formal Statutory Regulations: Not found in the available sources. This study is based on empirical socio-legal and public administration research under the Swiss National Research Project on Court Management (*www.justizforschung.ch*) [FACT] [20–21, 26].

Background

Since the 1990s, the New Public Management (NPM) movement has influenced public administration and the judiciary in Switzerland to improve efficiency, effectiveness, and public service orientation. However, the application of NPM in judicial institutions faces serious hurdles as it frequently triggers cultural shock and identity crises among judges. The failure of judicial reforms is largely due to the neglect of the judicial cultural dimension (court culture) and the clash of values between judges and court managers [8–9, 14–15].

Objectives

1. Empirically map the convergence and divergence of "good justice" expectations between judges and court managers. 2. Classify these expectations into the four reference worlds of Boltanski & Thévenot (1991) (Industrial, Civic, Commercial/Business, Domestic Worlds) to understand shifts in judicial culture [24–28]. 3. Provide a foundation for the development of New Public Governance (NPG) that aligns management values with judicial independence.

Scope

This policy covers the evaluation of the organizational culture of courts in the Swiss Confederation, specifically in the cantons of Vaud, Fribourg, Geneva, Luzern, Valais, Jura, Neuchâtel, and Ticino [1, 19, 21–22].

Key Provisions

  • 27 Expectations of Good Justice:
    • Top Shared Priorities (+++): Fast (Celerity) and Client-oriented [29–30, 36].
    • Judge-Specific Expectations (++): Emphasize the human side of justice (Humane, Personalized, Receptive) [31–32, 36].
    • Manager-Specific Expectations (++): Emphasize operational efficiency (Pragmatic, Efficacy, Inspiring Confidence) [34–36].
  • Four Reference Worlds of Boltanski & Thévenot (1991):
  1. Industrial World: Focuses on functional efficiency and operational performance (Fast, Efficient, Quality, Effectiveness).
  2. Civic World: Focuses on legal legitimacy, transparency, and public interest (Open, Independent, Transparent, Accountable, Impartial, Procedurally Compliant, Equal Treatment) [27, 38–39].
  3. Commercial/Business World: Focuses on market values, competition, and customer satisfaction (Client-oriented, Easy access, Financially affordable) [27–28, 38].
  4. Domestic World: Focuses on personal relationships, tradition, and community care (Humane, Personalized, Receptive, Close to the people) [27, 31, 37–38].

Implementation

  • Inductive Methodology & NVivo 10: Interview transcripts were analyzed using NVivo 10 software and coded into 27 knots (main expectation arguments), which were then classified into the four reference worlds [23–25, 29–36, 38–39].
  • Identification of Cultural Hybridization: The formation of a post-bureaucratic judicial culture that blends the Civic and Commercial Worlds, proving that the Swiss judiciary does not reject management, but adopts it selectively [37, 40–41].

Policy Problems

  • Tension Between Judicial Functions and Management: Potential value clashes between the professional ethos of judges (focused on material legal justice and adjudicative independence) and the expectations of court managers (focused on quantitative indicators, cost efficiency, and productivity) [2, 15–18].
  • Limits of Quantitative Performance Measurement: Excessive performance measurement based on quantitative standards risks pushing judges to "please the chiefs of courts" or sacrifice trial quality to meet case resolution quotas.

Reform / Change

  • Transition from New Public Management (NPM) toward New Public Governance (NPG) that integrates the perspectives of all internal and external judicial stakeholders.
  • The inclusion of judges in court management planning to prevent alienation and maintain the public ethos.

Multilingual Summaries (Swiss National Languages)

Deutsch (Schweizer Hochdeutsch)

Erwartungen an eine "gute Justiz" und die Gerichtskultur Dieser von Prof. Yves Emery und Lorenzo De Santis (IDHEAP, Universität Lausanne) im International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA, 2014) veröffentlichte Rahmen analysiert die Justizkultur in der Schweiz. Basierend auf Interviews in 10 Gerichten klassifiziert die Studie 27 Erwartungen an eine "gute Justiz" nach der Typologie von Boltanski & Thévenot. Sie zeigt, dass Richter und Gerichtsmanager bei Werten wie "Schnelligkeit" und "Kundenorientierung" übereinstimmen, sich aber unterscheiden, indem Richter die "menschliche" Seite betonen, während Manager "Pragmatismus" und "Effizienz" in den Vordergrund stellen. Dies deutet auf eine hybride Gerichtskultur im Zeitalter des New Public Management hin.

  • Rechtsgrundlage: Schweizerische Bundesverfassung (Richterliche Unabhängigkeit); Vereinheitlichung der Schweizerischen Prozessordnungen 2011.
