1. Regulatory Framework and Path Dependence

  • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: The history of issue and evidence management (Proses Penataan Isu dan Bukti) in Japanese civil procedure is explicitly characterized as a "Sejarah Kegagalan" (History of Failures). Japan initially transplanted the 1890 German model (adversarial party initiative), which led to prolonged trials. Subsequent transplants—the 1926 Austrian model (preparatory proceedings by inexperienced judges) and the 1956 US pre-trial model (separate "new case division")—both failed in practice due to systemic decoupling between preparation and trial judges.
  • [THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: In Institutional Theory, this exhibits profound path dependence and coercive/mimetic isomorphism. Transplanting foreign legal codes (German, Austrian, American) without aligning them with internal court routines resulted in administrative rejection. Only when internal practice innovated in the 1980s (Persidangan sekaligus Upaya Wakai) did genuine procedural adaptation occur, which was eventually codified in the 1996 New Code of Civil Procedure (CCP).
  • [ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS]: For Indonesia's civil procedure reform (RaPerMA), this history demonstrates that copying foreign pre-trial models without providing clear statutory authority for judges to manage evidence and interact with counsel will lead to administrative ritualization (formalitas belaka).

2. Judicial / Institutional Structure and Constitutional Safeguards

  • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: The 1996 CCP established three parallel options for issue management:
    1. Preliminary Oral Arguments (Art. 164 et seq., open court).
    2. Preparatory Proceedings (Art. 168 et seq., non-open chambers, main choice).
    3. Written Preparatory Proceedings (Art. 175 et seq., document exchange + teleconference).
  • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: In practice, Preparatory Proceedings under Art. 168 became the primary choice. However, this created two major constitutional/procedural tensions:
    • Open Court Principle (Asas Terbuka untuk Umum): Because preparatory proceedings are conducted in non-open chambers, CCP Art. 169(2) had to regulate spectator attendance to preserve public accountability. Furthermore, CCP Art. 173 requires that the results of preparatory proceedings be formally stated in open court (diungkapkan di sidang pemeriksaan).
    • Assigned Associate Judge (Hakim Anggota yang Ditunjuk) Authority: In three-judge panels (majelis hakim), single associate judges were frequently assigned to lead preparatory proceedings. Under the 1996 code, they were legally barred from examining written evidence (bukti surat) to prevent premature "judge conviction" (pembentukan keyakinan hakim) outside open court. In practice, judges could not manage issues without reading documentary evidence. Consequently, the 2003 CCP revision explicitly amended Art. 171(3) to grant assigned associate judges the legal authority to examine written evidence during preparatory proceedings.

3. Organizational Capacity and the 5M Framework

Applying the 5M Framework to the source evidence reveals the operational mechanics of Japanese court digitalization and case management:

  • Man (Human Resources):
    • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Inexperienced associate judges in early prep models (1926) caused unguided proceedings. Advocates (advokat) in practice tend to protect each other (saling melindungi) by not forcing opposing parties to explain delayed pleadings under Art. 167/174/178.
  • Method (Procedural Methodology):
    • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: The 1996 CCP envisioned an idealized 3-session trial model: Session 1 (case filtering/direction), Session 2 (issue clarification), and Session 3 (intensive witness evidence/verdict).
    • [EMPIRICAL EVIDENCE]: The 2024 empirical reality diverges significantly: the implementation rate of formal issue management stands at 41%, averaging 7.3 hearing sessions that absorb 70–80% of total trial duration. Individual prep sessions are often reduced to short 5–15 minute administrative check-ins.
    • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: The 2022 IT revision introduced a statutory alternative: Statutory Trial Period Litigation Proceedings (Persidangan dengan masa pemeriksaan menurut UU, Art. 381-2 et seq.). Upon mutual party consent, cases follow strict statutory deadlines: 5 months for preparation/evidence, 6 months for final arguments, and 7 months for verdict.
  • Material (Case Files & Evidence Matrices):
    • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Courts and parties jointly construct and update an Issue and Evidence Table (Tabel Penataan Isu dan Bukti) to map contested facts against documentary evidence.
  • Money (Financial & Trial Economy):
    • [THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: Early issue clarification reduces total trial costs for commercial litigants by eliminating unnecessary witness examinations.
  • Machine / Technology (Digitalization Infrastructure):
    • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: The 2022 IT revision integrated digital tools directly into issue management:
      • Web/Video Conferencing: Art. 169 allows online attendance for both litigating parties during preparatory proceedings.
      • Asynchronous Chat/Posting: Judges use the Chat (posting) function on Teams between scheduled hearing dates to request clarification on specific legal/factual points.
      • Cloud File Sharing: Real-time collaborative editing of Issue and Evidence Tables via shared cloud files.

  • Legal Culture & Social Structure:
    • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: The 1980s practice of Persidangan sekaligus Upaya Wakai was characterized by legal scholars as "Tidur sebantal, mimpi lain-lain" (Sleeping in the same bed, dreaming different dreams). Practitioners valued the informal flexibility of closed-door settlement caucuses, while legal theorists valued the formal strengthening of issue clarification.
    • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: In practice, revealing the results of preparatory proceedings in open court (Art. 173) frequently degenerates into a pure ritual: the judge asks "Is the result of the prep proceeding presented?" and counsel simply answer "Yes" without elaboration.
  • Street-Level Bureaucracy vs. Judicial Independence:
    • [THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: Lipsky's Street-Level Bureaucracy framework requires distinguishing frontline court staff from judges:
      • Frontline Administrative Clerks (Panitera/Pegawai): Handle routine filing, IT system permissions, and public gallery access under Art. 169(2). Their discretion is purely administrative.
      • Judges (Hakim): Possess curial discretion and judicial independence. Judges exercise discretion when choosing among the three preparatory proceeding modalities (Art. 164, 168, 175) based on case complexity. They also exercise discretion regarding whether to enforce preclusion sanctions (gugurnya hak mengajukan dalil dan bukti) under Art. 157 when parties submit late evidence after prep proceedings conclude.

5. Implementation and Business Experience

  • [SOURCE EVIDENCE]: The primary objective of issue and evidence management is to identify concrete facts relevant to legal claims, align perceptions between the judge and litigating parties, prevent unexpected judgments (mencegah putusan yang tidak terduga), and prevent frivolous appeals (mencegah pengajuan upaya hukum).
  • [THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: For commercial enterprises operating in an economy, unexpected judicial decisions represent a major risk. By establishing clear issue tables, utilizing asynchronous Microsoft Teams chat for interim clarifications, and enforcing statutory 5–7 month trial periods under Art. 381-2, the Japanese civil justice system provides high regulatory predictability and business certainty.

C. Synthesis of Evidence vs. Epistemological Uncertainty

  1. What is Firmly Supported by Source Evidence:
    • Japan's civil procedure history experienced multiple failed procedural transplants (1890 German, 1926 Austrian, 1956 US) before successfully codifying prep proceedings in 1996.
    • The 1996 CCP codified three parallel prep options (Art. 164, 168, 175).
    • The 2003 CCP revision amended Art. 171(3) to allow assigned associate judges to examine written evidence during prep proceedings.
    • Current Japanese District Court empirical practice shows a 41% implementation rate for formal issue management, averaging 7.3 sessions taking 70–80% of total trial duration.
    • The 2022 IT revision introduced asynchronous Teams chat clarifications, shared cloud file issue tables, and a Statutory 5–7 Month Trial Period (Art. 381-2 et seq.).

D. Policy Recommendations for Indonesian Civil Procedure Reform (RaPerMA)

Based on Prof. Yamamoto's analysis of Japan's historical lessons and recent digitalization efforts:

  1. Grant Statutory Authority to Assigned Judges: Ensure that Supreme Court Regulations (RaPerMA) explicitly authorize assigned associate judges (hakim anggota) to examine documentary evidence during pre-trial preparation, avoiding the pre-2003 Japanese bottleneck.
  2. Embed Asynchronous Digital Communication: Legalize secure asynchronous communication (e.g., e-Court chat/posting) between judges and litigating counsel between formal hearing dates to resolve interlocutory clarifications without requiring physical court appearances.
  3. Establish Shared Digital Evidence Matrices: Mandate the joint creation of an Issue and Evidence Table (Tabel Penataan Isu dan Bukti) uploaded directly to the court's digital portal to lock uncontested facts prior to trial.
  4. Avoid Procedural Formalism: Design pre-trial rules to prevent the open-court statement phase (Art. 173 equivalent) from becoming an empty ritual (formalitas belaka).
