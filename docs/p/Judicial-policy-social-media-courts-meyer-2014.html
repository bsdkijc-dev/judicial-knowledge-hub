The Social Media and the Courts Policy and Practical Guide Framework is a judicial administration governance policy and practical guide framework regarding the utilization of social media (Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, LinkedIn, etc.) by courts, judges, and court staff. Formulated by Norman H. Meyer, Jr. (Clerk of Court, US Bankruptcy Court for the District of New Mexico, 2014) in the *International Journal for Court Administration* (IJCA), it aims to enhance judicial transparency, accessibility, accountability, and efficiency ("open justice"), while mapping and anticipating ethical risks, media distortion, "Google mistrials", and security breaches, as well as establishing employee ethics guidelines and judicial ICT policy strategies [1, 4–8, 21, 50, 60–68].

Summary

Published by Norman H. Meyer, Jr. in the *International Journal for Court Administration* (IJCA), this policy framework provides a comprehensive guide for court administrators and officials in adopting social media. Amid the decline of traditional mass media and the convergence of ICT (*Nexus of Forces*), courts are required to open direct communication channels to the public [4–5, 7]. This policy maps the fundamental differences between the centralized/textual judicial culture and the decentralized/multimedia *new media* culture [14–17]. Meyer outlines strategies for utilizing three main platforms (Twitter, Facebook, YouTube), evaluates the risks of "Google mistrials", establishes 7 safe steps for employee recruitment, and formulates guidelines for drafting a *Court Social Media Policy* based on the Judicial Employee Code of Conduct [27, 50, 56–57, 66–68].

Legal Basis

  • U.S. Constitution (First Amendment & Due Process): Jurisprudence balancing the free speech rights of government employees (*Pickering v. Board of Education, 391 U.S. 563 (1968)*).
  • Code of Conduct for Judicial Employees (US Courts): Ethical conduct rules for court staff and *Advisory Opinion #112* (*The Use of Electronic Social Media by Judges and Judicial Employees*) from the Committee on Codes of Conduct of the Judicial Conference of the United States.
  • UK Criminal Justice Law (2014): An Act of the UK Parliament criminalizing jurors who conduct internet/social media research regarding a case or violate a judge's directions (carrying a penalty of up to 2 years in prison).
  • Courtroom Electronic Device Policies: Policies on the use of cell phones/laptops in the courtroom (e.g., US District Court for the District of Rhode Island 2014, and the Victoria Supreme Court Twitter Policy).
  • Not found in the available sources: Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text [FACT].

Background

The explosion in the use of mobile computing devices and social media worldwide is altering public expectations of public services, spawning demands for speed of service (*drive-through mentality*) and self-reliance (*DIY / self-represented litigants*) [11–13].

Objectives

1. Strengthen judicial transparency, accountability, and accessibility (*open justice*) [7, 18–20, 36]. 2. Provide practical guidance on using Twitter, Facebook, and YouTube platforms according to the target audience of the judiciary. 3. Prevent trial cancellations caused by internet research misconduct by jurors (*Google mistrials*) [50–51]. 4. Establish ethical guidelines, conduct manuals, and ICT controls to prevent confidential leaks and ethical violations by judges and court employees [60–63, 66–68]. 5. Prepare emergency notification strategies and media relations for high-profile cases [28, 33, 74–75].

Scope

This policy covers the governance of official court accounts, ethical personal and professional use by judges and court staff, employee recruitment, and relations with journalists and the general public at the national (US Federal & State Courts) and international levels (Australia, Norway, Dubai/DIFC, etc.) [1, 21–23, 54, 60].

Key Provisions

  • Three Cultural Differences between New Media vs. Judicial Culture:
  1. *Multidirectional/Decentralized vs Unidirectional/Institutional*: New media interacts multidirectionally ("many to many"), whereas courts operate unidirectionally ("one to many", issuing orders to be obeyed) [15–16].
  2. *Personal/Intimate vs Separate/Distant*: New media prioritizes personal intimacy, whereas courts maintain distance, formality (black robes, raised benches), and independence.
  3. *Multimedia vs Textual*: New media is audio-visual based (video, images, audio), whereas courts are dominated by text and words.
  • Seven Safe Steps for Employee Recruitment via Social Media:
  1. Use reputable job boards.
  2. Notify applicants in the job posting about internet background checks.
  3. Assign HR staff (not selecting officials) to conduct the background checks.
  4. Ensure protected information (race, religion, medical conditions) is not passed to the selecting officials.
  5. Conduct checks only at the final stage on 1–2 final candidates.
  6. Verify any adverse information found.
  7. Document the actions taken if a candidate is rejected due to adverse information.
  • Ethical Oversight Provisions and Social Media Policies:
    • The obligation to draft a *Court Social Media Policy* that prohibits the disclosure of confidential information, restricts "friending" between staff/judges and active litigators, and prohibits using official court e-mails for personal accounts [67–68, 73–74].

Implementation

  • Application of Main Platforms:
    • *Twitter*: Used for real-time trial updates for journalists, juror/journalist schedule notifications, and emergency announcements. Examples: Supreme Court of Victoria (Australia) [41–44], Norfolk Circuit Court, and Oslo District Court (Norway).
    • *Facebook*: Used as a general information portal, for public education, event announcements, recruitment, and curated informal public interaction. Examples: Maricopa County Superior Court (Arizona) [29–32] and US Bankruptcy Court District of New Mexico.
    • *YouTube*: Used to publish self-help videos, form-filling guides, juror service explainers, and internal/external training. Example: DIFC Courts (Dubai).

Policy Problems

  • The "Google Mistrials" Phenomenon and Juror Misconduct: Actions by jurors or witnesses seeking case information on the internet/social media, or posting trial status updates, which undermine the right to a fair trial and cause aborted trials or overturned verdicts [45–47, 50–51].
  • Ethical Risks for Employees and Judges: Personal or professional use of social media risks triggering ex parte communications, conflicts of interest, leaks of confidential information, breaches of independence, and defamation [60–63].
  • Social Media Recruitment Risks: Unstandardized background checks of job applicants on social media risk triggering discriminatory actions (religion, race, sexual orientation) and legal lawsuits [54–56].
  • Data Privacy and Security: Geotagging, encryption needs, and data breaches pose physical and digital security threats to the courts [84–85].

Reform / Change

  • Issuance of a Court Social Media Policy: Covering disclaimers, terms of service, comment participation rules, and "friending" restrictions [67–68, 73–74, 80].
  • Enforcement of Courtroom Electronic Device Policies: Balancing the principles of open justice with the protection of courtroom decorum [52–54, 75].
  • Adoption of Privacy and Security Measures: Utilizing encryption, data privacy apps, and geotagging controls to maintain judicial physical and data security [84–85].
