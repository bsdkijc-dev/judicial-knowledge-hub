4. POLICY ANALYSIS

  • Background: [FACT] The oldest European unification movement, the International Paneuropean Union, marked the 100th anniversary of its founding at an Extraordinary Congress in Vienna on November 17, 2022. The movement originated from the 1922 proclamation by Count Richard von Coudenhove-Kalergi in the newspaper Neue Freie Presse as a response to the threat of expanding Soviet communism and the danger of renewed warfare following World War I.
  • Policy Problem: [FACT]
    • Post-WWI Fragmentation: Division among 34 European states lacking a shared legal framework, solidarity, or unified strategic plan.
    • Totalitarian Suppression: Rise of Nazism and Sovietism led to the banning of Paneuropean organizations in 1933 in Germany and in 1938 in Austria following the Anschluss.
    • Modern Disintegration Risks: Internal territorial challenges (the 2017 Catalan secessionist crisis) and external institutional disintegration (Brexit in 2016).
  • Legal/Policy Gap: [FACT] Absence of an autonomous Common European Defense system, leaving European security heavily reliant on NATO structures and American political dynamics.
  • Objectives: [FACT] Prevent armed conflict in Europe through political and economic unification; safeguard democratic values and the Rule of Law; promote EU enlargement to Eastern European and Balkan states; and reinforce intra-European solidarity.
  • Implementation: [FACT] Progressive establishment of EU institutional frameworks starting from the ECSC (1950), the Treaty of Rome (1957) creating the EEC and Euratom, the Treaty of Maastricht (1992) establishing the modern EU structure, to the creation of the European Central Bank in 1998.
  • Evidence & Impact: [FACT] The fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 triggered Eastern EU enlargement waves in 2004 and 2007. In Spain, Paneuropa actively engaged in political dialogue and responded to the 2017 Catalan crisis through constitutional defense conferences.
  • Reform & Unresolved Issues: [FACT] Conclusions of the 2022 Vienna Congress set out future priorities: supporting democratic transition and Rule of Law in Russia, preserving Europe's Christian heritage, and facilitating EU integration for candidate countries (Ukraine, Moldova, Georgia, Kosovo, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Andorra, Albania, North Macedonia, Montenegro, Serbia, and Belarus).

6. MEDIAWIKI OUTPUT

Summary

100 Years of Paneuropa (100 años de Paneuropa) (Stable ID: JPW-WORK-000022) is an academic legal and historical policy essay written by Antonio Sáinz de Vicuña y Barroso, former General Counsel of the European Central Bank. Published in the December 2022 edition of Revista Jurídica Pérez-Llorca, the work commemorates the centenary of the International Paneuropean Union (1922–2022). The analysis traces the history of European integration from Coudenhove-Kalergi's vision, through its evolution into modern EU institutional frameworks (ECSC, EEC, Euratom, ECB), to contemporary challenges such as the Catalan secessionist crisis, Brexit, common European defense, and future EU enlargement.

Legal Basis

The historical and regulatory analyses presented in this publication reference the following primary legal pillars:

  • Spanish Constitution (CE): Article 20 (freedom of expression and academic publication).
  • Foundational Treaties of the European Union: Treaty of Rome (1957 - EEC and Euratom), Treaty on European Union / Treaty of Maastricht (1992), and Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU).
  • International Agreements & Withdrawal Treaties: Brexit Withdrawal Agreement (2020).

Background

On November 17, 2022, the International Paneuropean Union celebrated its 100th anniversary at an Extraordinary Congress held in Vienna. Founded in 1922 by Count Richard von Coudenhove-Kalergi following World War I, the movement served as the intellectual foundation for modern European supranational integration.

Objectives

1. Document the historical legacy and contributions of the Paneuropean Movement to European unification. 2. Map the legal evolution from early economic communities (ECSC, EEC) to a full political and monetary union. 3. Evaluate modern integration crises including Brexit and territorial secessionism. 4. Promote an autonomous Common European Defense policy (*Defensa común europea*) alongside future EU enlargement.

Scope

The analysis covers five main areas: 1. Early History: Kalergi's 1922 Manifesto, intellectual backing (Ortega y Gasset, Madariaga), and suppression under Nazi rule. 2. Post-WWII Period: The 1948 Hague Congress, the Marshall Plan, and the establishment of the Council of Europe. 3. Institutional Integration: EEC, Euratom, European Parliament, Treaty of Maastricht, and the establishment of the ECB (1998). 4. Crises & Challenges: Catalan separatism in 2017, Brexit negotiations, and common defense reliance on NATO. 5. Future Roadmap: Conclusions of the 2022 Vienna Centenary Congress and enlargement pathways for candidate nations.

Key Provisions

  • Origins of Unification: European integration began as a popular movement to prevent war and counter totalitarianism.
  • Institutional Evolution: Sectoral cooperation in coal, steel, and atomic energy evolved into full economic, monetary, and legal integration.
  • Defense Autonomy: Europe remains dependent on NATO structures and requires greater strategic autonomy in defense policy.

Implementation

Implementation is observed through the enforcement of EU treaties, European Commission negotiations, and ongoing policy advocacy by Paneuropean organizations across member states.

Policy Problems

  • Lack of an independent Common European Defense framework outside NATO.
  • Disintegration risks posed by populist movements and internal secessionist crises.
  • Slow accession timelines for candidate countries in the Western Balkans and Eastern Europe.

Reform

  • Ratification of the Treaty of Maastricht (1992) and establishment of the European Central Bank (1998).
  • Adoption of the 2022 Vienna Centenary Congress Roadmap.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • Implements: Treaty of Rome, Treaty of Maastricht, and Brexit Withdrawal Agreement.
  • Applies to: International Paneuropean Union, European Central Bank, and European Union Institutions.
