Italy-Libya Boat Migrants Pull-Backs and International Law Accountability Framework is an international judicial and policy framework governing the practice of pull-backs (push-backs by proxy)—specifically the interception and forcible return of migrants on the high seas by the Libyan Coast Guard under the coordination, funding, and technical assistance of Italy pursuant to the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) of 2 February 2017—as well as the assessment of legal accountability before the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) 1.

Overview

Authored by Giulia Ciliberto, Researcher in Economic Law at the University of Naples Federico II, the study examines the legal accountability gap engineered by European and Italian border externalisation strategies 2. Following the landmark ruling in Hirsi Jamaa and others v. Italy (2012), which prohibited direct high-seas push-backs, Italy shifted to an indirect model: financing, training, and equipping the Libyan Coast Guard (LCG) to execute pull-backs on Italy's behalf 3.

Legal Basis

The framework operates across national, international, and supranational legal instruments:

  • Memorandum of Understanding between Italy and Libya (2017): Bilateral agreement establishing cooperation to suppress illegal migration and enhance Libyan coast guard capabilities 4.
  • United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) & SAR/SOLAS Conventions: Mandates search and rescue obligations (UNCLOS Art. 98) and requires delivery of rescued persons to a designated "place of safety" 5.
  • European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR): Guarantees the right to life (Article 2), prohibition of torture and non-refoulement (Article 3), and prohibition of collective expulsion (Article 4 of Protocol No. 4) 6.
  • Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts (ARSIWA Article 16): Codifies customary international law governing state responsibility when aiding or assisting another state in an internationally wrongful act 7.

Background & Incident of 6 November 2017

On 6 November 2017, a vessel carrying 130 migrants encountered severe distress in international waters in the Central Mediterranean 8. The Italian Maritime Rescue Coordination Centre (MRCC Rome) relayed distress signals to the NGO rescue vessel Sea-Watch 3 and an LCG patrol boat 9. Aggressive intervention and miscoordination by the LCG resulted in at least 20 migrant drownings, 47 forced pull-backs to Libya, and 59 evacuations by Sea-Watch 3 to Italy 10. Subsequently, 17 surviving migrants lodged an application against Italy before the ECtHR 11.

Objectives

  • Assess whether Italy can be held internationally responsible for pull-backs carried out by Libyan authorities 12.
  • Establish that Libya does not qualify as a place of safety due to pervasive torture and inhumane treatment of detained migrants 13.
  • Prevent evasion of the non-refoulement principle executed through state border externalisation arrangements 14.

Scope

The policy covers International Law of the Sea, International Human Rights Law, International Refugee Law, State Responsibility doctrines, and ECtHR Jurisprudence 15.

Key Provisions & Accountability Analysis

  • Extraterritorial Jurisdiction (Al-Skeini / Medvedyev / Hirsi Jamaa): Italy exercises extraterritorial jurisdiction if it holds de facto control or decisive influence over the LCG pull-back operations 16.
  • Complicity Test under ARSIWA Article 16: Italy provided vital material support (patrol vessels, funding, MRCC command support) with actual knowledge (or willful blindness) of ongoing human rights violations in Libya 17.
  • Violations of Non-Refoulement: Returning migrants to Libya constitutes direct refoulement under Article 3 ECHR and triggers indirect refoulement through subsequent deportations to origin states 18.

Implementation

Executed through EU- and Italian-funded Libyan Coast Guard patrols, military training under Operation Sophia, and operational coordination managed via MRCC Rome 19.

Policy Issues

  • Criminalisation of Maritime Rescue NGOs: Implementation of restrictive Codes of Conduct and impoundment of NGO vessels, increasing mortality rates on maritime routes 20.
  • Monetary Gold Principle Obstacle: Procedural barriers preventing the ECtHR from determining Italian complicity without formally adjudicating the lawfulness of actions by Libya (a non-ECHR member state) 21.

Reforms & Recommendations

Calls for a comprehensive overhaul of EU border externalisation agreements to insert mandatory, judicially enforceable human rights compliance clauses 22.
