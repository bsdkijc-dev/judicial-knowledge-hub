JPW-SEMINAR-001

By Cecep Mustafa, Indonesian Judicial College (IJC) · revision 1101, 2026-09-16 · wiki source · Markdown

Fifth Judicial Seminar of the International Criminal Court is the ICC's fifth official judicial seminar, held on 20 January 2023 in The Hague in hybrid format, convened to discuss and strengthen implementation of the complementarity principle and the role of national courts in addressing serious international crimes.

Summary

The seminar carried the theme "Role of National Courts in the International Criminal Justice System." Held in hybrid format and funded by the European Commission, it brought together approximately 50 senior judges, court presidents, and practitioners from 27 national jurisdictions and 8 international, regional, or hybrid courts.

Legal Basis

  • Rome Statute: Article 17 (Admissibility), Article 18 (Preliminary Rulings Regarding Admissibility), Article 19 (Challenges to Admissibility), and Article 20 (Ne bis in idem).
  • Rome Statute: Article 28 (Responsibility of Commanders and Other Superiors).

Background

The Rome Statute's complementarity principle affirms that national jurisdictions hold the primary right and responsibility to prosecute international crimes (genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression). The ICC was never intended to be a "super court" handling every instance of mass atrocity worldwide. The seminar was convened to correct the widespread public perception of the ICC as the primary responder, and to encourage national courts to act as the frontline authority.

Objectives

  • Strengthen effective implementation of complementarity at the national level.
  • Facilitate networking and exchange of experience among courts.
  • Introduce supporting tools such as the ICC Case Law Database (CLD) and UN/UNAFEI capacity-building programs.

Key Provisions

  • Admissibility Test: A case is inadmissible before the ICC where it is genuinely being investigated or prosecuted by a state with jurisdiction (the "same conduct, same person" test), unless that state is unwilling or genuinely unable to do so.
  • Domestication of Law: The importance of incorporating Rome Statute crimes and modes of criminal responsibility into national law.
  • Complementarity Transition: A shift from a vertical (hierarchical) model toward a horizontal model based on burden-sharing and joint investigative teams across jurisdictions.

Implementation

The seminar was divided into two main sessions, each chaired by a division president of the Court. At the event, the ICC officially launched French- and Spanish-language versions of the ICC Case Law Database (CLD).

Evidence

  • Launch of French and Spanish versions of the ICC Case Law Database (CLD), containing over 8,000 legal findings extracted from approximately 10,000 ICC jurisprudential decisions.
  • The "same conduct, same person" admissibility test is now applied more flexibly according to the specific context of each situation.
  • A formal cooperation agreement was signed between the ICC and UNAFEI for capacity-building.
  • Joint investigative teams, such as in the situation in Ukraine, exemplify the shift in complementarity from a vertical to a horizontal, burden-sharing relationship.

Policy Issues

  • Limited legislative and technical capacity in many states to domesticate Rome Statute crimes.
  • Dual criminality requirements constraining the exercise of universal jurisdiction in some countries.
  • The need to harmonize international jurisprudence on command responsibility.
  • Witness protection challenges and the risk of "culture shock" in cross-border witness examination.
  • Limited accessibility of national court judgments due to translation constraints.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • International Criminal Court: Principal organizer of the seminar.
  • European Commission: Official financial sponsor.
  • United Nations (Office of Legal Affairs): Partner supporting judicial capacity-building.
  • UNAFEI (United Nations Asia and Far East Institute for the Prevention of Crime
