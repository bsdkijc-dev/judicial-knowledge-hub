The Electronic Courts and Evidence Management at the International Criminal Court Policy Framework is an international judicial administration and electronic evidence (e-evidence & big data) management policy framework at the International Criminal Court (ICC, The Hague). Formulated by Mark Dillon (Head of Information and Evidence ICC) and David Beresford (Registry/e-Court Specialist ICC) in 2014, it establishes electronic court (e-Court) standards, technical e-Court protocols and the E-Court User Group, evidence registration chain of custody oversight (Pre-Registration Form / PRF, Bates / ERN / EVD numbering), the integration of the Ringtail & iTranscend systems, and solutions to 5 primary challenges regarding the rise of Big Data, Electronically Stored Information (ESI), infrastructure costs, staff turnover (institutional knowledge drain), and procedural legal compliance (Regulation 26 of the Regulations of the Court & the Rome Statute) [1–25].

Summary

Published by Mark Patrick Dillon and David Beresford in the *International Journal for Court Administration* (IJCA), this policy framework reviews the first 11 years of experience of the International Criminal Court (ICC) in The Hague in managing electronic courtrooms (*e-courtrooms*) and massive digital evidence [1–3, 20]. Inheriting the experiences of past tribunals such as the *International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg* and the ICTY, the ICC developed a digitized evidence workflow (*scanned softcopy*, *Bates Stamp* / ERN numbering) [2, 7–10]. This policy emphasizes the importance of legal compliance with Regulation 26 of the *Regulations of the Court*, the operationalization of the *E-Court User Group*, and the application of three technology evaluation criteria: *procedural compliance*, *fitness for purpose*, and *cost-benefit analysis* [16, 21–23].

Legal Basis

  • Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court & Rules of Procedure and Evidence: Regulates international criminal procedural law, combining civil law and common law traditions.
  • Regulations of the Court (ICC Judicial Provisions):
    • *Regulation 26 (Electronic Management)*: The legal basis for the management of electronic proceedings.
    • *Regulation 26(2)*: Requires the Registry to guarantee the authenticity, accuracy, and confidentiality of electronic evidence, and to maintain a verbatim record of proceedings [21–22].
    • *Regulation 26(4)*: Requires evidence to be presented in electronic form, while establishing that the original physical document (*original copy*) remains legally binding (*authoritative*) should a dispute over authenticity arise.
  • Council of Europe Electronic Evidence Guide (May 2013): Guidelines on handling electronic evidence for law enforcement and judicial authorities [18–19].
  • Not found in the available sources: Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text [FACT].

Background

The evolution of international criminal evidence shifted from the large-scale arrangement of physical paper documents during the Nuremberg era (1945) toward an integrated digital archive system at the ICTY (1993) and the permanent ICC in The Hague [2, 7–10].

Objectives

1. Guarantee the integrity, authenticity, and security of electronic evidence at the ICC [21–22]. 2. Overcome the technical hurdles of managing *Big Data* and *Electronically Stored Information* (ESI) [23–24]. 3. Facilitate cross-party coordination (OTP, Defense, Victims, Registry) through the *E-Court User Group* [22–23]. 4. Ensure that judicial ICT investments meet rational cost-benefit analyses.

Scope

This policy applies to all trials at the International Criminal Court (ICC - The Hague), the panel of judges (*Chambers*), the Office of the Prosecutor (*OTP*), the *Registry*, and serves as a global reference for e-Court administration [1–3, 20].

Key Provisions

  • Three Criteria for Evaluating Judicial Technology Investments:
  1. *Procedural Compliance*: ICT applications must comply with procedural law and judicial guidelines.
  2. *Fitness for Purpose*: Technology must demonstrably provide real added value and not complicate usability (e.g., the lessons learned from the failed virtual reality simulation in the *Bloody Sunday Inquiry*) [16–17].
  3. *Cost-Benefit Analysis*: The benefits of implementing ICT must outweigh its total investment and maintenance costs.
  • Main Components of ICC e-Courtrooms:
    • *Ringtail e-Court System*: An integrated evidence management and presentation application.
    • *iTranscend Real-Time Transcripts*: A service providing live transcription feeds from the stenographer directly to the monitors of judges and counsels.
    • *30-Minute Broadcast Delay*: A 30-minute delay in the internet broadcasting of trials to protect witnesses and sensitive evidence.
  • E-Court User Group Governance:
    • A practical technical coordination forum among system users to harmonize data structures, update technical protocols, and simplify evidence numbering (such as the elimination of the *EVD number*) [22–23].

Implementation

  • **Evidence Management Workflow**:
  1. OTP investigators collect evidence and fill out a *Pre-Registration Form* (PRF).
  2. Registration of claims data and metadata into the investigation database.
  3. Handover of evidence files to the Registry via the *Court Management Section* (CMS).
  4. Uploading the data into the *Ringtail* system for real-time access by the parties during the trial.

Policy Problems

  • Institutional Knowledge Drain: High international staff turnover risks the loss of practical knowledge regarding the initial configuration of the e-Court system [1, 24–25].
  • Hidden Storage Costs: The decline in hardware prices is often eclipsed by the exponential surge in *Big Data* volume that must be stored and processed [23–24].

Reform / Change

  • The planned creation of a *single generic e-court protocol* for all future ICC cases.
  • The embedded integration of e-Court technology directly into the physical infrastructure of the new permanent ICC building in The Hague from day one.

Relationships with Other Entities

  • International Criminal Court (Registry / CMS): The executive body managing e-courtrooms and digital evidence.
  • ICTY (International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia): The predecessor tribunal that pioneered e-Court technology [7–10].

3 Most Important Improvements
