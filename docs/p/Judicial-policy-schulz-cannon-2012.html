Trial by Tweet? Findings on Facebook? Social Media Innovation or Degradation? The Future and Challenge of Change for Courts is a policy research paper by Dr. Pamela D. Schulz and Dr. Andrew J. Cannon examining the impact of the social media explosion on court administration, judicial independence, and judicial communication strategy.

Summary

The paper analyzes how the growth of social media (such as Facebook and Twitter) has dismantled the information monopoly once held by nation-states and traditional media. The authors argue that courts must not remain isolated within their traditional "habitus," but instead should adopt "Push and Pull" communication strategies to counter "law and order" rhetoric and "Discourses of Disrespect."

Legal Basis

  • Not found in the available sources: This paper is an academic policy study and is not established under any specific statutory legislative instrument [1–53].
  • Standard Protocol on Hand-held Electronic Devices (England and Wales): Governs permission for using handheld devices to tweet in courtrooms.
  • Revised Jury Instructions (US Federal Courts): Governs prohibitions on jurors' use of social media during trials.

Background

Social media has become a new "public square" in cyberspace. Uncontrolled and rapid public discussion online frequently triggers "Trial by Tweet" — trial by social media — which risks undermining the right to a fair trial.

Objectives

1. Encourage judicial institutions to adopt social media as a strategic communication tool. 2. Safeguard the integrity and independence of the courts from false information or public prejudice online. 3. Provide practical guidance on virtual service of process, digital evidence, and jury management.

Scope

The study covers communication theory analysis (Spiral of Silence, Panopticon, Contested Space), case studies from Australia, England, the United States, Malaysia, and Ireland, and practical policy recommendations for courts.

Key Provisions

  • Push & Pull Strategy: Sending concise information via Twitter/RSS (*Push*) to draw users to the official court website containing full judgments (*Pull*).
  • Jurors and the Internet ("Googling Juror"): The challenge of jurors independently researching information on Wikipedia/the internet, requiring clear rules and juror education.
  • Virtual Service of Process: Use of Facebook/Twitter to deliver court documents following initial physical identity verification.
  • Video Recording of Evidence: Use of early video recordings of witnesses to preserve the integrity of oral testimony.

Implementation

Implementation occurs through the creation of official court Twitter accounts (e.g., Family Court of Australia), providing plain-language summaries alongside major judgments, appointing dedicated court communications specialists, and using "retired judges" as intermediaries to clarify media controversies [28, 36–39].

Policy Problems

1. Threats to *sub judice* rules and fair trial rights due to publication of a defendant's criminal history on Facebook prior to jury trial. 2. The "Discourses of Disrespect" phenomenon, in which politicians and commercial media exploit crime issues for sensationalism (*infotainment*). 3. Courts' inability to compel global platforms to remove harmful content.

Reform / Change

  • A shift from passive one-way communication toward an interactive, two-way model.
  • Recognition of digital map evidence (Google Maps) and live video recording at crime scenes.
  • Appointment of media officers and discourse-analysis specialists within court environments.
