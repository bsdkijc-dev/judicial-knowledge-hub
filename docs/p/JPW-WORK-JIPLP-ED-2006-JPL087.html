Editorial: IP litigation, the new money-spinner is a legal editorial authored by Jeremy Phillips and published in the Journal of Intellectual Property Law & Practice (Vol. 1, No. 8) by Oxford University Press in 2006 Editorial Publication [1–10].

Summary

This editorial reviews the commercial phenomenon where intellectual property (IP) litigation yields substantial financial returns and media exposure for both winning and losing parties (*win-win situation*) IP Litigation Benefits [1–10]. The author cites as prime examples *The Da Vinci Code* copyright dispute and Tesco's parallel import trade mark clash with Levi Strauss IP Case Examples [1–10].

Legal Basis

  • Fair Dealing (UK Copyright Law): A legal doctrine permitting excerpts from litigated texts and judicial decisions in academic commentaries or trial review books without infringing copyright Fair Dealing [1–10].
  • Regional Trade Mark Exhaustion Policy (European Union): Regulations prohibiting unauthorized parallel imports of branded goods from outside the European Economic Area (EEA), as enforced in Levi Strauss v Tesco (Joined Cases C-414/99 to C-416/99) Trade Mark Exhaustion [1–10].

Background

IP litigation is widely recognized for its exorbitant costs, which place heavy financial demands on litigants Litigation Costs [1–10]. However, intensive media interest throughout proceedings frequently generates promotional value far exceeding the legal fees incurred Judicial Publicity [1–10].

Objectives

  1. Demonstrate how IP litigation provides direct and indirect economic rewards for participating parties Financial Returns [1–10].
  2. Analyze the impact of trial-generated publicity on consumer product sales Product Marketing [1–10].
  3. Evaluate corporate reputation outcomes within EU trade mark exhaustion disputes Corporate Image [1–10].

Key Provisions

  • Baigent and Leigh v Random House Case: A copyright infringement suit brought by the authors of The Holy Blood and the Holy Grail against the publisher of The Da Vinci Code was dismissed, yet sales for both books surged dramatically Da Vinci Code Dispute [1–10].
  • Judicial Encrypted Code (Smithy Code): Mr Justice Peter Smith inserted a secret code within his judgment text, attracting widespread public and media fascination Smithy Code [1–10].
  • Levi Strauss v Tesco Case: Although Tesco lost its suit over gray-market Mexican jeans, it established a favorable public reputation as a "Champion of the Consumer" opposing European differential pricing Tesco Levi Dispute [1–10].

  • Burden of Judicial Expenses: The need for procedural streamlining in court litigation, despite secondary promotional benefits Judicial Efficiency [1–10].
  • Consumer Welfare vs Trade Mark Monopolies: Conflict between brand owners' rights to control supply channels and consumer demands for competitive pricing Distribution Conflict [1–10].
