'Reconceptualizing Security Strategies For Courts' (Wallace, Blackman & Rowden, 2013)

Wiki Entity Page

Reconceptualizing Security Strategies For Courts: Developing A Typology For Safer Court Environments is a judicial policy study and court design framework authored by Prof. Anne Wallace (Edith Cowan University), Prof. Deborah Blackman (University of Canberra), and Dr. Emma Rowden (University of Technology Sydney), published in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA, Vol. 5, No. 2, October 2013, ISSN 2156-7964).

Summary

This study reconceptualizes court security strategies by moving beyond traditional perimeter security approaches (such as X-ray scanners, metal detectors, and CCTV cameras). Utilizing the results of a three-year research project funded by the Australian Research Council (ARC Linkage Project LP0882179), the authors developed a four-quadrant typology that combines "Place vs. Process" dimensions and "Hard vs. Soft Systems" analysis to ensure physical, psychological, cultural safety, and accessibility for all court users [1, 4, 5, 14–17].

Background

Over the past 30 years, Australian courts experienced heightened security concerns following violent courtroom incidents, the assassination of Judge David Louis Opas at his home, and post-9/11 and Bali Bombings terrorism threats. Government responses focused on massive funding for reactive, "airport-style" perimeter security. However, this approach is limited and neglects the psychological safety, cultural safety, and accessibility needs of court users [2–4].

Policy Objectives

1. Distinguish clearly between the concept of "Safety" (a holistic, broad state of the user) and "Security" (technical/procedural mechanisms). 2. Develop a four-quadrant typology to analyze and design holistic court safety strategies [14–17]. 3. Promote the "co-creation of safety" through collaboration among court administrators, architects, and court users.

Scope & Methodology

The research involved 87 semi-structured interviews with judges, magistrates, security staff, lawyers, and victim advocates, as well as 66 "user jury" participants in focus groups evaluating court buildings across 5 jurisdictions in Australia and New Zealand.

The Four Quadrants of Court Safety Typology

The typology combines two classification axes [14–18]: 1. Inflexible Environments (Hard Place): Architectural layouts, perimeter screening points, and physical segregation zones. 2. Regulatory Systems (Hard Process): Official rules, security personnel presence, breach reporting procedures, and queuing rules. 3. Flexible Environments (Soft Place): User-centric waiting spaces, clear wayfinding signage, witness support rooms, and accessible drinking water and toilets. 4. Supportive Practices (Soft Process): Intelligence gathering and integrated case scheduling, pre-hearing briefings, non-verbal intimidation detection, and collaborative practices.

Implementation & Domestic Violence Case Study

The study analyzes the dilemma of domestic violence victims in courts who often feel like "sitting ducks" in open waiting rooms monitored by CCTV, or risk being "cornered" and intimidated in hidden public corners out of sight of security. The best solution combines hard approaches (perimeter screening) with soft approaches (special scheduling for domestic violence cases, pre-hearing briefings, and the detection of non-verbal intimidation signals) [8, 18–21].

Policy Problems

1. The dominance of a "fortress approach" that triggers anxiety and psychological stress for victims and witnesses [2–4, 23]. 2. A lack of training for security personnel to detect non-verbal intimidation codes.

Reform / Change

Transitioning from reactive perimeter security to flexible, adaptive, and collaborative user-based court safety design.
