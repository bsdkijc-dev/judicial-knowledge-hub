Supreme Court Decision No. 1176 K/Pdt/1986 is an Indonesian civil-law precedent concerning the procedural consequences of filing a partnership-loss claim before the financial statements required by the partnership's constitutive documents have been properly prepared, signed, and approved.

The decision establishes that where the partnership agreement requires a balance sheet and profit-loss statement to be prepared by a designated accountant and approved by both partners, a claim based upon an unapproved financial statement is premature and may be declared inadmissible (Niet Ontvankelijke Verklaard / NO).

Executive Summary

The dispute arose between [Party A] and H. [Party C] in connection with the business activities of Fa. Kalimantan Development Corporation (Fa. KDC).

[Party A] originally claimed timber-cutting commissions of US$2.80 per cubic metre, valued at IDR 16,406,613.60, under a Cooperation Statement dated 10 September 1972 concerning an approximately 8,000-hectare forest concession area in Melak.

H. [Party C] responded with a counterclaim seeking recovery of 50% of the alleged losses of Fa. KDC as of 31 August 1970. The counterclaim relied upon a balance sheet and profit-loss statement prepared by accountant Drs. [Party B].

The lower courts granted the counterclaim. The Supreme Court, however, held that the financial statement had not satisfied the contractual formalities prescribed by the partnership's constitutive documents because it had not been signed or approved by [Party A].

Accordingly, the partnership-loss counterclaim had not yet become procedurally ripe for adjudication and was therefore inadmissible.

The Supreme Court granted the cassation petition, annulled the decisions of the lower courts, rejected the conventional claim, and declared the counterclaim inadmissible.

Case Background

[Party A] brought an action before the Samarinda District Court concerning timber-cutting commissions allegedly payable under a Cooperation Statement dated 10 September 1972.

The claimed commission rate was US$2.80 per cubic metre, with the claim calculated at IDR 16,406,613.60. The underlying transaction concerned an approximately 8,000-hectare forest concession area in Melak.

H. [Party C] contested the claim and filed a counterclaim relating to losses allegedly incurred by Fa. KDC.

The counterclaim sought 50% of the partnership losses as of 31 August 1970, consisting of:

  • IDR 11,478,725.78 in partnership losses; and
  • IDR 4,757,723 in personal-account amounts;

for a total claimed amount of IDR 16,236,448.78.

The counterclaim relied upon a Balance Sheet and Profit-Loss Statement prepared by accountant Drs. [Party B].

Procedural timeline

Legal Issues

The case raised the following principal legal issues:

  1. Whether a partnership-loss claim may be judicially enforced before the partnership's required Balance Sheet and Profit-Loss Statement has been validly prepared and approved.
  2. Whether Article 7 of the Deed of Establishment of Fa. KDC required the relevant financial statement to be prepared by a designated accountant and signed by both partners.
  3. Whether an unsigned or unapproved balance sheet could constitute a sufficient legal basis for determining the amount of partnership losses.
  4. Whether the counterclaim was premature and therefore inadmissible (Niet Ontvankelijke Verklaard / NO).
  5. Whether the lower courts had correctly applied the partnership agreement and applicable procedural law.

Partnership Agreement and Financial Statement Requirement

The Supreme Court's reasoning centered on Article 7 of Deed No. 5 concerning the establishment of Fa. KDC, read together with the Joint Statement dated 15 January 1971.

Under these arrangements, the partnership's financial statements were subject to formal requirements. The relevant Balance Sheet and Profit-Loss Statement had to:

  1. be prepared by the designated accountant; and
  2. be signed or approved by both partners.

These requirements were legally significant because the financial statement was being relied upon to establish the partnership's financial position and allocate losses between the partners.

Supreme Court's Reasoning

1. Contractual Requirements Governed the Financial Statement

The Supreme Court treated the partnership's constitutive documents as establishing the formal requirements for determining the partnership's financial position.

Article 7 of the Deed of Establishment, together with the Joint Statement of 15 January 1971, required the relevant financial statement to be prepared and approved according to the agreed procedure.

2. The Balance Sheet Had Not Been Properly Approved

The Balance Sheet and Profit-Loss Statement prepared by Drs. [Party B] had not been signed or approved by [Party A].

Therefore, the financial statement had not acquired the contractual status necessary to establish the partnership's final financial position between the partners.

3. The Partnership-Loss Claim Was Premature

Because the financial statement had not been completed and validated in accordance with the partnership agreement, the alleged loss allocation had not yet reached the stage at which it could properly be enforced through judicial proceedings.

The counterclaim was therefore considered premature or not yet ripe (niet rijp).

4. The Lower Courts Misapplied the Law

The Supreme Court concluded that the Judex Facti had misapplied the relevant legal requirements by granting the counterclaim despite the absence of the required approval of the financial statement.

The lower-court decisions therefore could not stand.

5. Consequence of Procedural Immaturity

The appropriate procedural consequence was not an adjudication of the partnership-loss amount on the merits, but a declaration that the counterclaim was inadmissible (Niet Ontvankelijke Verklaard / NO).

Ratio Decidendi

The core ratio decidendi may be stated as follows:

Where the partnership's constitutive documents require a Balance Sheet and Profit-Loss Statement to be prepared by a designated accountant and signed or approved by both partners, a partnership-loss claim based upon a financial statement that has not received the required approval is premature and may be declared inadmissible.

The Supreme Court therefore distinguished between:

  • the substantive existence of an alleged partnership loss; and
  • the procedural ripeness of a judicial claim seeking allocation or recovery of that loss.

Until the contractual financial-statement requirements had been satisfied, the alleged loss allocation could not properly serve as the basis for the counterclaim.

Holding

The Supreme Court:

  1. Granted the cassation petition of [Party A].
  2. Annulled the East Kalimantan High Court Decision No. 33/Perd/1985/PT.KT.Smda.
  3. Annulled the Samarinda District Court Decision No. 109/1983/Pdt.G/PN.Smda.
  4. Adjudicated the case itself (mengadili sendiri).
  5. In the conventional claim, rejected the plaintiff's claim.
  6. In the counterclaim, declared the counterclaim inadmissible (Niet Ontvankelijke Verklaard / NO).
  7. Ordered the Cassation Petitioner / Original Plaintiff to bear court costs at all judicial levels, including cassation costs of IDR 20,000.

Legal Significance

This precedent is significant for Indonesian partnership and civil procedural law because it demonstrates the relationship between contractual financial-accounting requirements and procedural ripeness of litigation.

The decision illustrates several principles:

  • A partnership agreement may prescribe formal procedures for establishing the partnership's financial position.
  • Courts should take those contractual requirements into account when determining whether a claim based upon partnership accounts is procedurally ripe.
  • A financial statement that has not received the approval required by the partnership agreement may be insufficient to establish an enforceable allocation of losses.
  • A claim that is filed before the contractual preconditions have been satisfied may be declared inadmissible rather than adjudicated on its substantive merits.
  • The Supreme Court may annul Judex Facti decisions where the lower courts have misapplied the legal consequences of contractual requirements.
  • The distinction between rejection on the merits and inadmissibility for procedural prematurity is central to the case.
