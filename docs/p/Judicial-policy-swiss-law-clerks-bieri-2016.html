The Swiss Law Clerks and Judicial Assistants Policy Framework is a judicial governance and caseload management policy framework in Switzerland that utilizes law clerks and judicial assistants (Gerichtsschreiber / greffier / cancelliere) as key-players within the judiciary. Formulated by Peter Bieri (2016) in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), it evaluates the expansion of law clerks' functions from mere trial recorders to drafters of judgment proposals, authors of judgment reasoning, and holders of advisory votes. The framework analyzes the implications of this expansion on the right to a lawful judge (Article 30(1) of the Swiss Federal Constitution), judicial independence, the phenomenon of Gerichtsschreiberjustiz (clerk-driven justice), and the implementation of performance targets [1–30].

Summary

Published by Peter Bieri in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework reviews the crucial role of law clerks (Gerichtsschreiber) in addressing the surging judicial caseloads in Switzerland. Unlike in many other European nations, Swiss law clerks are highly qualified lawyers who actively participate in the adjudication process, including drafting the legal reasoning of judgments and casting an advisory vote [12–14]. The massive growth in the number of law clerks (e.g., at the Federal Supreme Court, increasing from 2 clerks in 1875 to 132 clerks for 38 professional judges in 2016) has raised concerns about Gerichtsschreiberjustiz (clerk-driven justice). This policy examines the constitutional limits of delegating judicial tasks, the risks of anchoring bias, and the effectiveness of establishing quantitative performance targets [16, 21, 27–30].

Legal Basis

  • Federal Constitution of the Swiss Confederation 1999 (Article 30(1)): Constitutional guarantee ensuring the right to a lawful, independent, and impartial judge (Recht auf den gesetzlichen Richter).
  • Swiss Federal Supreme Court Act 2005 (BGG, SR 173.110), Article 24: Regulations defining the core functions and status of law clerks at the Swiss Federal Supreme Court.
  • Regulations on the Federal Supreme Court 2006 (BGerR, SR 173.110.131), Articles 3 & 39: Detailed technical regulations regarding the duties and the advisory vote rights of law clerks.
  • Swiss Federal Personnel Act 2000 (BPG, SR 172.220.1): The statutory basis for performance-related pay for law clerks.
  • Cantonal Regulations (Lucerne, Zurich, Glarus, Thurgau, Basel-Landschaft): Establishments such as the 1:1 clerk-to-judge ratio in Lucerne (§ 62 JusV) and other specific cantonal authorities.
  • Not found in the available sources: Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text [FACT].

Background

Constantly increasing caseloads amid tight financial constraints have forced Swiss courts to hire more law clerks instead of appointing more judges. This shift has transformed the role of the judge from a direct case examiner to a team manager relying heavily on delegated work.

Objectives

1. Manage caseload backlogs efficiently and cost-effectively without compromising the quality of justice. 2. Maintain the strict constitutional boundary between delegable tasks and non-delegable core judicial tasks. 3. Preserve judicial independence and the constitutional guarantee of the right to a lawful judge [16, 19–24]. 4. Enhance the accountability and efficiency of law clerks through indicator-based performance targets [26–30].

Scope

This policy covers all levels of the federal judiciary (Federal Supreme Court, Federal Administrative Court, Federal Criminal Court, Federal Patent Court) and the courts of the 26 Swiss cantons [8–11].

Key Provisions

  • Extensive Functions and Authority of Law Clerks:
    • Instructing cases, drafting pre-trial memos, formulating judgment proposals, writing the final legal reasoning, and co-signing the judgments alongside judges.
  • Advisory Votes and Special Rights:
    • The right to cast an advisory vote during deliberations, the right to request oral deliberations at the Federal Criminal Court, and the right to register a dissenting opinion in the Canton of Zurich.
  • Upper Limit on the Clerk-to-Judge Ratio:
    • The obligation for judges to independently review case files; merely conducting a passive "plausibility check" on a clerk's draft violates Article 30(1) of the Federal Constitution.
  • Establishment of Performance Targets:
    • The application of quantitative benchmarks (e.g., 40–60 cases per year at the Federal Supreme Court) which directly influence the annual performance-related pay of law clerks.

Implementation

  • Operationalization at the Swiss Federal Supreme Court: Utilizing 132 law clerks to draft 9 out of 10 judgment proposals under the supervision of 38 permanent professional judges.
  • Empirical Survey of Performance Targets (2013): Confirmation of the application of performance targets for law clerks across 14 out of 18 surveyed cantons and federal courts.

Policy Problems

  • Gerichtsschreiberjustiz (Clerk-Driven Justice): The de facto dominance of law clerks in drafting legal reasoning, which risks eroding the core adjudicative role of judges.
  • Risk of Anchoring Bias: A clerk's draft proposal acts as a cognitive "anchor" that subconsciously influences the independent reasoning of the reviewing judge.
  • Dependence of Lay Judges: Lay judges often lack formal legal backgrounds, making them highly dependent on the legal advice provided by the professional law clerk in the courtroom.

Reform / Change

  • Establishing formal upper limit ratios for clerks to judges (e.g., the 1:1 ratio in the Canton of Lucerne) to guarantee that judges have sufficient time for independent file review.
  • Differentiating the handling of routine cases (which can be heavily delegated) from leading cases (which require the full and direct examination of the judge).

Deutsch (Schweizer Hochdeutsch)

Rahmenwerk für Schweizer Gerichtsschreiber und Justizassistenten Dieser von Peter Bieri im International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA, 2016) veröffentlichte Rahmen analysiert die zentrale Rolle der Gerichtsschreiber bei der Bewältigung der wachsenden Fallzahlen an Schweizer Gerichten. Gerichtsschreiber in der Schweiz übernehmen weitreichende Aufgaben, einschliesslich des Verfassens von Urteilsentwürfen und der Ausübung einer beratenden Stimme. Die massive Zunahme des Verhältnisses von Gerichtsschreibern zu Richtern (z. B. 132:38 am Bundesgericht) birgt jedoch die Gefahr der "Gerichtsschreiberjustiz". Der Rahmen untersucht die verfassungsrechtlichen Grenzen der Aufgabendelegation (Recht auf den gesetzlichen Richter), die Risiken kognitiver Verzerrungen (Anchoring Bias) und die Auswirkungen quantitativer Leistungsziele auf die richterliche Unabhängigkeit.

  • Rechtsgrundlage: Bundesverfassung der Schweizerischen Eidgenossenschaft (Art. 30 Abs. 1); Bundesgerichtsgesetz (BGG); Bundespersonalgesetz (BPG).

Français

Cadre politique des greffiers et assistants judiciaires suisses Publié par Peter Bieri dans l' International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA, 2016), ce cadre analyse le rôle crucial des greffiers (Gerichtsschreiber) dans la gestion de l'augmentation du nombre de dossiers dans les tribunaux suisses. En Suisse, les greffiers assument des fonctions étendues, notamment la rédaction de propositions de jugement et l'exercice d'un vote consultatif. Toutefois, l'augmentation massive du ratio greffiers/juges (par exemple 132:38 au Tribunal fédéral) soulève des inquiétudes quant à la « Gerichtsschreiberjustiz » (justice dirigée par les greffiers). Ce cadre examine les limites constitutionnelles de la délégation des tâches (droit à un juge légal), les risques de biais d'ancrage et l'impact des objectifs de performance quantitatifs sur l'indépendance de la justice.

  • Base Légale: Constitution fédérale de la Confédération suisse (Art. 30 al. 1) ; Loi sur le Tribunal fédéral (LTF) ; Loi sur le personnel de la Confédération (LPers).

3 Most Important Improvements

1. Establishing a National Constitutional Upper Limit for the Clerk-to-Judge Ratio: Formalize a capped ratio of law clerks to judges (such as the 1:1 standard in Lucerne) across all cantons to prevent the unchecked dominance of Gerichtsschreiberjustiz. 2.
