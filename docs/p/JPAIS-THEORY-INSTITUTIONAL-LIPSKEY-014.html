1. Regulatory Framework, Bottom-Up Innovation, and Institutional Isomorphism

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Around 1990, Japanese civil litigation was constrained by an archaic interpretation of the Code of Civil Procedure, which required that all document exchanges and formal statements be conducted in open courtrooms. This resulted in piecemeal 5-minute hearings ("Kami menyatakannya") and fragmented witness examinations spread across months. In response, frontline judges in Tokyo, Osaka, and regional courts independently developed bottom-up procedural innovations—specifically "Persidangan sekaligus Upaya Wakai" (Simultaneous Trial and Wakai Effort) and Pemeriksaan Bukti Intensif (Intensive Evidence Examination)—despite initial academic criticism that these methods lacked explicit statutory grounding.

[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: In Institutional Theory terms, this evolution represents a classic transition from institutional decoupling (where formal open-court rules were decoupled from practical dispute resolution needs) to bottom-up normative adaptation and eventual coercive codification. The Supreme Court of Japan did not impose these reforms top-down; rather, judge-led field innovations demonstrated such overwhelming operational success (reducing trial duration from 2.5 years to under 6 months) that parliament codified them in the 1996 Code of Civil Procedure Revision (enforced 1998) and funded nationwide court infrastructure overhauls.

[ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS]: In the Indonesian judicial reform context (RaPerMA / PERMA No. 1 of 2016 / JPAIS KCCP), court-annexed mediation often suffers from procedural ritualization. Adapting the Japanese trajectory requires empowering Indonesian judges to conduct structured pre-trial issue triage and oral evidence narrowing, transforming formal court hearings from 5-minute administrative check-ins into substantive dispute resolution sessions.

2. Judicial / Institutional Structure & Organizational Capacity (5M Framework)

Applying the 5M Framework to Prof. Tani Yuko's operational narrative illuminates the structural mechanics of judicial productivity and capacity management:

Man (Human Capital & Professional Roles)

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Japanese District Court judges work in shared multi-judge offices (3 to 6 judges per room), facilitating active peer consultation and mentorship between senior presiding judges (Judge B, 20 yrs exp), mid-career judges (Judge C, 12 yrs exp), and junior assistant judges (Judge D, 2 yrs exp). Assistant judges cannot handle single-judge litigation ending in a verdict during their first 5 years.

[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: In Organizational Sociology, shared physical working environments foster normative knowledge transfer and reduce individual cognitive isolation, enabling junior judges to master complex issue management protocols.

Method (Procedural Methodology & Four-Way Triage)

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Judge C operationalized a 4-way case triage model at the first hearing:

  1. Default judgments (Putusan Verstek) for absent defendants;
  2. Fast-track trials for uncontested facts without settlement potential;
  3. Fast-track Wakai sessions for uncontested facts seeking quick settlement; and
  4. "Persidangan sekaligus Upaya Wakai" for contested facts.

Furthermore, Judge B's Intensive Evidence Examination required:

  1. Pre-identifying core issues;
  2. Restricting witnesses strictly to core issues;
  3. Examining principal parties (X and Y) first;
  4. Submitting advance written statements (surat pernyataan); and
  5. Submitting detailed question lists (daftar pertanyaan terperinci).

Material (Infrastructure & Round-Table Courtrooms)

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Following the 1996 CCP revision, the Supreme Court funded extensive physical renovations: providing dedicated pre-trial chambers equipped with IT/telephones for every judge, and constructing round-table courtrooms (ruang sidang meja bundar) where judges and litigating parties sit as equals at a central table to encourage active discussion.

Money (Trial Economy & Systemic Efficiency)

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Completing witness examinations in a single 3-hour session and resolving cases via Wakai produces substantial systemic savings: judges avoid spending weeks drafting formal written verdicts, court clerks are freed from compiling lengthy witness examination minutes, and parties avoid multi-year appellate litigation costs.

Machine / Technology (Digitalization & Remote Hearings)

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: The procedural framework evolved from 1990 physical attendance to 1998 telephone/IT prep rooms, 2003 statutory trial speed targets, and 2026 fully online civil litigation (online filings, digital evidence access, and remote video hearings with a 7-month statutory trial period).

Legal Culture & Social Structure

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Public dissatisfaction with 1980s civil justice was extreme: 86.4% felt trials were too slow, 84.9% too costly, and 88.3% viewed litigation as a last resort. Popular culture criticized trials through three famous metaphors:

  1. "Dentist Treatment" (5-minute monthly visits);
  2. "May Rain" (piecemeal, intermittent filings); and
  3. "Rudderless Ship" (unpredictable, drifting proceedings).

Crucially, public critique targeted slowness and inefficiency, NOT judicial corruption or illegal rulings—the public trusted that judges applied the law faithfully.

Street-Level Bureaucracy vs. Judicial Discretion

[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: Lipsky's Street-Level Bureaucracy framework requires distinguishing frontline court administrators from judges:

  • Court Secretaries & Clerks (Sekretaris & Panitera): In conventional practice, court secretaries exercised significant frontline discretion over daily call orders, scheduling 6 cases in 30-minute blocks and placing contentious attorneys last. Clerks managed minute preparation and expressed frustration when piecemeal cross-examinations required endless transcript drafting.
  • Judges (Hakim): Possess curial discretion and judicial independence. In the Japanese model, judges exercise curial discretion to actively structure issues, reallocate burden of proof, disclose their tentative judicial conviction (pengungkapan keyakinan hakim), and propose fair Wakai terms. Because the judge conducting issue structuring and witness exams is the same judge conducting Wakai, settlement proposals carry immense persuasive authority grounded in the evidence.

4. Implementation & Business Experience

[SOURCE EVIDENCE]: Proper pre-trial issue structuring allows rational commercial parties to accurately predict trial outcomes. When judges clarify burden of proof and conduct intensive evidence exams, parties choose Wakai not out of frustration, but as the most rational business solution.

[THEORETICAL INFERENCE]: For commercial enterprises, piecemeal litigation spanning years creates crippling business uncertainty and high legal overheads. Implementing oral issue structuring, single-day intensive witness examinations, and judge-led Wakai delivers high regulatory predictability, low administrative burden, and strong contract enforcement certainty.

Synthesis of Evidence vs. Epistemological Uncertainty

What is Firmly Supported by Source Evidence

  • Around 1990, Japanese single judges handled ~200 cases, relying on 5-minute open court hearings and piecemeal witness examinations spanning 2.5 years.
  • Public dissatisfaction in 1985 was high regarding speed (86.4%) and cost (84.9%), but public trust in judicial integrity remained intact.
  • Frontline judges independently pioneered "Persidangan sekaligus Upaya Wakai" and "Pemeriksaan Bukti Intensif", reducing trial duration to under 6 months.
  • The 1996 CCP revision codified these bottom-up innovations into formal law, driving nationwide court renovations (dedicated prep rooms, round-table courtrooms).
  • In Japan, the judge conducting issue structuring and evidence exams is the same judge conducting Wakai, making settlement proposals highly persuasive.

Policy Recommendations for Indonesian Civil Procedure Reform (RaPerMA)

Based on Prof. Tani Yuko's analysis of Japan's judicial evolution:

  1. Institutionalize Pre-Trial Issue Triage: Amend Supreme Court Regulations (RaPerMA) to require judges at the first hearing to triage cases into four distinct procedural tracks (Default, Fast-Track Trial, Fast-Track Mediation, and Intensive Pre-Trial Issue Structuring).
  2. Adopt Intensive Evidence Examination Rules: Require litigating parties to submit advance written witness statements (surat pernyataan) and detailed question lists (daftar pertanyaan terperinci), concentrating witness cross-examinations into single-day sessions.
  3. Unify Judge Roles for Pre-Trial and Settlement: Align court rules so that the judge who manages pre-trial issue structuring and evidence examination is authorized to conduct evaluative conciliation (Wakai), leveraging formed judicial conviction to propose realistic settlement terms.
  4. Modernize Courtroom Infrastructure: Transition courtroom design from traditional high-bench adversarial layouts to collaborative round-table courtrooms (ruang meja bundar) and dedicated digital pre-trial suites to foster active oral communication.
