JPW-POLICY-000018

By Cecep Mustafa, Indonesian Judicial College (IJC) · revision 1359, 2026-09-17 · wiki source · Markdown

The Taiwanese Constitutional Development and Human Rights Framework (Stable ID: JPW-POLICY-000018) is a national constitutional law, judicial review, democratization, and human rights policy framework evaluating the six constitutional revisions (1991–2000), semi-presidential governance, Grand Justice constitutional review, and millennium human rights policies in Taiwan. Documented under IDE Asian Law Series No. 24 by Assoc. Prof. Jau-Yuan Hwang (NTU), Dr. Fort Fu-Te Liao (Academia Sinica), and Assistant Prof. Wen-Chen Chang (NTU) in collaboration with IDE-JETRO, the policy outlines electoral legitimization, the dual-executive system, landmark Grand Justice interpretations (Nos. 261, 325, 387, 499), the National Human Rights Commission bill, and the domestication of the International Bill of Rights. Note: Named entities Cecep Mustafa and Reihan Abdan Syakur are Not found in the available sources.

Summary

The framework analyzes Taiwan's constitutional transformation from post-WWII martial law rule to a consolidated democracy. It outlines six core structural pillars: (1) Six Constitutional Revisions (1991–2000), terminating emergency martial provisions, introducing 100% direct legislative and presidential elections, and reducing the National Assembly to an ad hoc body; (2) Semi-Presidential Governance, establishing a dual-executive system where the President appoints the Premier without legislative confirmation, balanced by legislative no-confidence and conditional dissolution powers; (3) Constitutional Judicial Review, empowering the Council of Grand Justices to enforce abstract/concrete review, non-delegation, due process, and judicial deadlines; (4) Landmark Judicial Jurisprudence, terminating lifetime parliamentarians (Interpretation No. 261) and voiding unconstitutional self-term extensions (Interpretation No. 499); (5) National Human Rights Infrastructure, establishing the Presidential Human Rights Advisory Group and Executive Yuan Human Rights Committee to draft National Action Plans and create the National Human Rights Museum; and (6) International Treaty Domestication, enacting domestic statutory incorporation for the ICCPR and ICESCR alongside the Human Rights Basic Law bill.

Legal Basis

Authority and execution derive from constitutional charters, amendments, and procedural statutes:

  • Constitutional Charters & Amendments: 1947 Constitution of the Republic of China; Six Sets of Constitutional Additional Articles (1991, 1992, 1994, 1997, 1999, 2000).
  • Emergency & Security Statutes: Temporary Provisions Effective During the Period of National Mobilization for the Suppressing of the Communist Rebellion (1948, repealed 1991); Emergency Martial Law Decree (May 1949 – July 1987); National Security Law (1987, as amended 1992).
  • Judicial & Organic Laws: Law on Adjudication of Cases by the Council of Grand Justices (1958, as amended 1993); Organic Law of the Judicial Yuan; Presidential and Vice Presidential Election and Recall Law (1995); Local Autonomy Act.
  • Human Rights & Gender Statutes: Domestic Violence Prevention Law (1999); Law of Equal Employment between Two Sexes (2002); Aborigine Status Act, Aborigine Education Act & Aborigine Employment Rights Protection Act.

Background & Policy Problems

Background

Following 50 years of Japanese colonial rule and post-1945 ROC administration, Taiwan was subjected to 38 years of martial law and political suppression under the White Terror era. Democratic protests led to the 1987 lifting of martial law, the 1991 termination of the MSCR, and judicial mandates ordering competitive elections.

Policy Problems

  • Executive-Legislative Gridlock: Minority President facing hostile legislative majorities without discretionary dissolution powers.
  • UN Treaty Deposit Obstruction: Diplomatic blocking of formal UN treaty deposits by Beijing.
  • Stalled Human Rights Bills: Legislative delays on the National Human Rights Commission Act.
  • Retention of Capital Punishment: Mandatory death penalty sentences across special criminal statutes obstructing complete human rights compliance.

Objectives

1. Consolidate constitutional democracy through full direct elections. 2. Enforce checks and balances through active Grand Justice judicial review. 3. Establish an independent National Human Rights Commission. 4. Incorporate the ICCPR and ICESCR into domestic law. 5. Promote human rights education, historical archives, and victim compensation.

Scope & Implementation

Scope

Applies across all central government Yuans, courts, local governments, educational institutions, civil service academies, and human rights bodies in Taiwan.

Key Provisions

  • Direct Presidential Election (1994 Amendment): Requirement for direct popular election of the President and Vice President by plurality vote.
  • Semi-Presidential Appointment (1997 Amendment): Presidential authority to appoint the Premier without legislative consent, subject to legislative no-confidence votes.
  • Super-Majority Relaxation (1993 Grand Justice Law): Reducing decision quorums to two-thirds for constitutional interpretations.
  • Burden of Proof Non-Delegation: Mandatory statutory specificity for administrative restrictions on fundamental rights (Interpretation No. 313).

Implementation

  • Grand Justice Output: Invalidating unconstitutional laws/regulations in 98+ cases across the 5th and 6th Councils.
  • Victim Compensation: Awarding NT$117.8 million+ in compensation to White Terror victims.
  • Civil Service Training: Training 6,000+ civil servants annually in human rights and gender equality.

Policy Problems

  • Executive-Legislative Gridlock: Minority President facing hostile legislative majorities without discretionary dissolution powers.
  • UN Treaty Deposit Obstruction: Diplomatic blocking of formal UN treaty deposits by Beijing.
  • Stalled Human Rights Bills: Legislative delays on the National Human Rights Commission Act.

Reform

  • Unifying the Judicial Yuan court structure.
  • Passing the Human Rights Basic Law.
  • Completing the National Human Rights Action Plan.
  • Replacing mandatory death penalties with discretionary sentences.
