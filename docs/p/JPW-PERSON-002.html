The Transformative Private Law Research Framework

Lead

The framework was formulated by Marija Bartl, Professor of Transnational Private Law at the University of Amsterdam and Principal Investigator of the European Research Council-funded N-EXTLAW project. It challenges the complicity of traditional private law modules — contract, property, corporate, and financial law — in the current global poly-crisis.1 By deploying three distinct research strategies — historical variance, comparative research and varieties of capitalisms, and diverse economies — the framework seeks to renew private legal imagination and to provide legal infrastructure for non-extractive economic models.2

Summary

The framework critiques current European Union legislative initiatives for falling prey to "neoliberal naturalism" — the assumption that market transparency and profit maximisation are the sole drivers of prosperity.3 Instead, it demonstrates that alternative economic practices, such as cooperatives, social enterprises, and care economies, already exist and account for a significant share of European GDP.4

Background

Private law institutions — contract, property, corporate, insolvency, and financial law — form the core infrastructure of modern economies. Standard private law modules have, however, been used to generate capital and concentrate wealth while externalising environmental and social costs, contributing directly to a contemporary poly-crisis marked by climate emergency, wealth inequality, democratic backsliding, and technological surveillance.5 Scholarly interventions, notably Katharina Pistor's The Code of Capital, show how leading law firms deploy private law modules to generate wealth for capital holders while socialising environmental and democratic costs.6 In response, transformative private law seeks to reorient the deep legal infrastructure of the economy toward sustainable, non-extractive futures.7

Policy Problem

Contemporary law and policy remain captive to neoliberal naturalism — the uncritical belief that high-profit extractive capitalism is the sole engine of human prosperity, and that market transparency and profit-seeking incentives alone drive innovation.8 Recent EU legislative initiatives, including sustainability reporting, corporate due diligence, and ecodesign regulation, tinker at the edges of the market without altering foundational private law assumptions such as privity of contract, property as absolute dominion, limited liability, and shareholder primacy.9

Legal Basis

The framework evaluates and interacts with several key EU legal instruments:

  • Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) — the EU framework for mandatory corporate sustainability disclosure.10
  • Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD Proposal) — imposing environmental and human-rights due-diligence obligations across global supply chains.11
  • Proposal for Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation — expanding product sustainability requirements across the EU single market.12
  • Net Zero Industry Act Proposal — an EU industrial framework for clean-technology manufacturing.13

Objectives

  • Decentre neoliberal naturalism and the myth of profit-seeking human nature in private law doctrine.14
  • Institutionalise non-extractive economic practices built on care, solidarity, and the collective interest.15
  • Reform law-school curricula to include social-economy structures alongside traditional profit-maximising corporations.16

Scope

The framework applies to European Union private-law policy, national corporate legislation, contract theory, property-law reform, and comparative legal pedagogy across European law faculties.17

Key Provisions and Research Strategies

  1. Historical Variance — historicising limited liability, freedom of contract, and property as absolute dominion, to demonstrate that corporate power is a contingent political choice rather than a natural state.18
  2. Comparative Research and Varieties of Capitalisms — using comparative law to challenge the notion that Anglo-American "shareholder primacy" is the inevitable end point of corporate law history.19
  3. Diverse Economies and the Social Economy — mapping and supporting entities in which social or environmental purpose takes precedence over profit, surpluses are reinvested, and governance is democratic (e.g., cooperatives, social enterprises, fair-trade organisations, care cooperatives).20

Implementation

The framework is carried out through academic research under ERC Grant Agreement No. 852990 (the N-EXTLAW project, Mainstreaming Law as a Vehicle of Social Change), with findings published in The Italian Law Journal.21

Evidence

Empirical studies cited in support of the framework indicate that the social economy accounts for roughly 10% of GDP in countries such as Italy, that SMEs contribute around 50% of EU GDP, and that social-economy structures showed greater economic resilience during the 2008 financial crisis in Southern Europe.22

Impact

The framework offers a theoretical and institutional blueprint for a transformative private law capable of supporting realistic socio-ecological alternatives, redirecting corporate purpose toward the common good, and legitimising non-extractive business models.

Reform Proposals

  • Establishing a dedicated EU industrial policy for the social economy.
  • Linking executive compensation to non-financial, sustainability-related performance metrics.
  • Reforming corporate law to remove limited-liability shields for systemic environmental harm.
  • Embedding diverse-economy models into standard law-school curricula.
  • Expanding consumer repair rights.23

Unresolved Issues

  1. Persistent EU legislative reluctance to move beyond communications and action plans toward binding structural private-law statutes for the social economy.
  2. The risk that traditional private-law doctrines — such as legal certainty and legitimate expectations — will be invoked to block socio-ecological reform.
  3. Reconciling profit-capping or surplus-reinvestment requirements with traditional capital-market expectations.

Frequently Asked Questions

  1. What is the Transformative Private Law Research Framework, and who is its primary academic author? It is a scholarly strategy for decentring neoliberal naturalism in private law, authored principally by Marija Bartl.
  2. What EU legislative proposals constitute the current EU sustainability regulatory framework discussed in the source text? The CSDDD, CSRD, the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation, and the Net Zero Industry Act.
  3. What role does the European Research Council play? It funds the underlying research through the N-EXTLAW project (Grant No. 852990).
  4. Are Cecep Mustafa or Geovanny Widya Berlianty P documented in the transformative private law literature reviewed here? No — neither is found in the available sources.
  5. Who is Marija Bartl? Professor of Transnational Private Law at the University of Amsterdam and Principal Investigator of the ERC N-EXTLAW project.
  6. What sub-fields of private law fall within the scope of transformative legal strategies? Corporate law, contract law, property law, and financial law.
  7. How do the three research strategies operationalise transformative private law? Through historical variance, comparative research on varieties of capitalism, and mapping of diverse/social economies.
  8. What is "neoliberal naturalism"? The assumption that extractive, high-profit capitalism is the only viable engine of prosperity, which constrains EU sustainability legislation to marginal reform.
  9. How does Katharina Pistor's "Code of Capital" relate to Bartl's critique? Pistor's account of how private law is used to generate and protect capital is cited as foundational evidence for the framework's critique of private law's role in wealth concentration.
  10. What legislative and educational reforms are proposed? A dedicated social-economy industrial policy, non-financial performance metrics in executive pay, curricular reform in law schools, and expanded consumer repair rights.
  11. What empirical evidence supports the viability of alternative economic models? Data showing the social economy contributes roughly 10% of GDP in Italy, SMEs contribute roughly 50% of EU GDP, and social-economy entities proved more resilient during the 2008 financial crisis.
