Understanding unequal representation is a symposium introductory article and comparative political science analytical framework authored by Brian Burgoon (University of Amsterdam), Noam Lupu (Vanderbilt University), Jonas Pontusson (University of Geneva), and Wouter Schakel (University of Amsterdam), published in the European Journal of Political Research (EJPR) in 2022.

Summary

This article presents the analytical framework for an EJPR Symposium examining income and class biases in political representation across liberal democracies. The paper conceptualizes unequal representation through two central approaches — congruence and responsiveness — and identifies the mediating conditions and moderating conditions that link wealth to political influence. The study demonstrates that affluence bias in representation is not merely a distinctively American phenomenon, but a common feature across many democracies.

Background

Since the 1980s, elected governments in OECD countries have rarely taken new initiatives to compensate low- and middle-income citizens for the concentration of wealth at the top, with state redistribution actually declining across the US and Western Europe. Populist rhetoric claiming that policymaking favors elite interests has found strong resonance among working-class and lower-middle-class citizens.

Objectives

1. Advance a comparative approach to the study of unequal political representation. 2. Map the chain of political representation into three stages: pre-electoral (candidate recruitment and party platforms), electoral (campaigns and voter choice), and post-electoral (legislative bargaining and lobbying). 3. Distinguish the causal mechanisms between mediating conditions and moderating conditions.

Scope

The research synthesizes six comparative studies drawing on cross-national data (33–39 countries), longitudinal public opinion survey data (>800 policy proposals in Germany), historical cabinet minister data (18 democracies since 1945), and conjoint candidate choice experiments.

Key Provisions & Symposium Findings

  • Ideological Congruence: Elected representatives across 33 countries are consistently more closely aligned with the preferences of wealthy citizens than poor citizens.
  • Policy Responsiveness: Public policy outputs (as observed in Germany and across 18 democracies) consistently respond more to the preferences of business owners and high-status groups than to those of unskilled workers.
  • Agenda-Setting Bias: Legislative activity is significantly higher in policy domains prioritized by high-status occupational groups.
  • Descriptive Misrepresentation & Candidate Recruitment: Middle-class voters tend to disfavor working-class candidates, contributing to the shortage of working-class representatives in parliament.
  • Party Platforms: Political parties' budgetary commitments (especially among right-wing parties) more closely reflect the spending preferences of wealthy citizens.
  • Moderating Conditions: Corruption, clientelism, and economic growth moderate representation bias. However, proportional electoral systems and economic globalization were not found to eliminate representation bias.

Implementation

The article outlines a methodological framework based on comparative macro-statistical analysis, machine learning techniques, and conjoint experiments. The research was funded by the European Research Council (ERC Advanced Grant No. 741538), NWO (Grant No. 406-15-089), and the Amsterdam Center for Inequality Studies (AMCIS).

Policy Issues

1. The deficit of political influence among low-income and working-class citizens in shaping redistributive policy. 2. The hidden influence of high-income elite groups through legislative agenda-setting and cabinet composition.

Reform / Future Research Directions

1. Integrating measurement of policy positions with issue salience. 2. Broadening class comparisons to include skilled versus unskilled workers and intersections with gender and ethnicity. 3. Measuring citizens' awareness of representation inequality and its effect on populist voting behavior.
