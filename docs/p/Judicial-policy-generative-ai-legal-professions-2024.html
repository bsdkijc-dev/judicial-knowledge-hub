By Cecep Mustafa, Indonesian Judicial College (IJC) · revision 1425, 2026-09-18 · wiki source · Markdown

The Governance and Impact Framework for Generative AI in the Legal Professions (Unboxing Generative AI for the Legal Professions) is a policy governance and impact evaluation framework regarding the use of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) and Large Language Models (LLMs) in judicial administration systems and the legal professions [1, 2, 10–16].

Summary

Published by Dr. Francesco Contini in the International Journal for Court Administration (2024), this framework analyzes the transformation of the judiciary following the rapid penetration of GenAI since the launch of ChatGPT [1, 6–7]. The policy maps the technical functions of GenAI, evaluates the risks of hallucinations and data confidentiality breaches, and compares two primary regulatory approaches: the bureaucratic/certification approach (CEPEJ/FBE) and the professional responsibility and human oversight approach (Judiciary of England & Wales) [14–16, 65–70, 87–97, 102–107].

Legal Basis

  • European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act): Classifies LLMs as general-purpose AI / foundation models.
  • CEPEJ Guidelines on the Use of Generative AI by Judicial Professionals (February 2024) [23, 67, 75–77].
  • Courts and Tribunals Judiciary AI Guidance for Judicial Office Holders (England & Wales, December 2023) [23, 66, 72, 77–79].
  • European Lawyers in the Era of ChatGPT Guidelines (Fédération des Barreaux d’Europe / FBE, June 2023).
  • Charte pour une utilisation responsable de l'IA générative (Predictice, 2023).
  • Practical Guidance for the Use of Generative AI in the Practice of Law (California State Bar, November 2023).
  • Guidelines for Use of Generative AI in Courts and Tribunals (Courts of New Zealand, December 2023).

Background

For over 30 years, legal AI focused on predictive systems (predictive justice). However, its development was hindered by the high costs of data cleaning and the limited availability of jurisprudential databases in Europe [3–6]. The emergence of GenAI in November 2022 dramatically disrupted the judicial sector through the availability of public chatbots and direct integration into daily office applications (such as Microsoft 365 Copilot and Google Workspace Gemini) [6–7, 33–34].

Objectives

1. Harness the efficiency of GenAI for document processing and expanding access to justice [10, 39, 49–52]. 2. Prevent judicial risks caused by AI hallucinations and the submission of fictitious evidence/jurisprudence [15, 19, 60–61, 104]. 3. Guarantee data confidentiality, privacy protection, and professional secrecy [20, 73–78]. 4. Reaffirm the principles of human oversight and personal accountability in court proceedings [2, 15, 85–86, 104–106].

Scope

This governance policy encompasses three levels of analysis [10–13]:

  • Activity System Level: Changes in the practical workflows of judges, lawyers, and pro se litigants.
  • Organizational & Judicial Governance Level: Implications for self-governing bodies and soft-law regulations.
  • Economic Level: The shift of judicial budgets from local staff to LLM platform license providers (digital feudalism / techno-feudalism).

Key Provisions

  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) & Domain Systems: The use of RAG architecture and dedicated APIs/GPTs to absorb specific legal knowledge and suppress hallucination rates [25–33, 41–46].
  • Data Confidentiality & Paid Licenses: Prohibition on uploading private data or case files to free public chatbots, and the necessity of contractual clauses forbidding the use of user data for AI model training [20, 26, 73–79].
  • Output Control & Human Oversight: The mandatory requirement for users to verify factual and legal accuracy, as well as the absence of bias, before documents are submitted to the court [15, 81–86, 104–106].

Implementation

  • Law Firms & Legal Tech: Adoption of dedicated systems (such as Lexis+ AI, CoCounsel/Casetext) for summarizing files, drafting contracts, and conducting legal research [38–46].
  • Public Access to Justice: The launch of the Guia Prático da Justiça (GPJ) chatbot by the Portuguese Ministry of Justice to provide free information on divorce and business registration [51–55].
  • Judicial Institutions: The use of AI by the Spanish Ministry of Justice for document clustering; Italy's development of the National Jurisprudential Database (BDP) abstracts [56–58]; and anecdotal usage by judges in Colombia, India, Pakistan, the UK, and the US [59–64].

Policy Problems

  • High Hallucination Rates: A Stanford University study (Magesh et al., 2024) found that even leading specialized legal AI systems still produce hallucinations at rates of 17% to 33% [47–48, 85].
  • Divergence in Governance Models:
    • Certification/Authorization Model (CEPEJ/FBE/Predictice): Demands pre-use authorization and mandatory disclosure, which risks stifling AI use due to the absence of formal authorizing bodies [91–94, 102].
    • User Accountability Model (UK Judiciary): Emphasizes the personal responsibility of the judge/lawyer for material accuracy without requiring certification or explicit disclosure [91–96, 103–106].
  • Information Asymmetry & Moral Hazard: Inexperienced users or pro se litigants lack the capacity to verify the accuracy of GenAI outputs, triggering principal-agent problems [86–87, 115–116].

Reform / Change

  • Prioritizing the enforcement of ethical and deontological professional obligations (human empowerment & critical thinking) over bureaucratic certification.
  • Encouraging periodic independent empirical benchmarking regarding the accuracy and hallucination rates of legal GenAI systems.
  • Mandating the use of protected licenses (Enterprise/Paid API) that guarantee data privacy protection.
