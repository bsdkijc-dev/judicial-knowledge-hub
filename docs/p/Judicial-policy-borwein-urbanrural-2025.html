Urban–rural policy disagreement is an empirical comparative political science and public policy research paper authored by Sophie Borwein (University of British Columbia), Jack Lucas (University of Calgary), Tyler Romualdi, Zack Taylor, David A. Armstrong II, and Katharine McCoy (University of Western Ontario), published in the European Journal of Political Research (EJPR) in 2025.

Summary

This study tests whether the sharpening urban–rural electoral divide since the 1990s is driven by widening disagreement over public policy preferences between urban and rural residents. Based on a synthesized dataset covering 456 unique policy issue questions (asked 973 times) drawn from 10 Canadian Election Study surveys (1993–2021), totaling N = 5.3 million individual-level responses, the study finds that although urban and rural residents hold genuinely large disagreements on cultural policy issues (such as gun control, immigration, and Indigenous affairs), the urban–rural policy issue divide has not widened at all over the past 30 years. Consequently, growing policy disagreement cannot explain the widening urban–rural electoral cleavage.

Background

Since the 1990s, the electoral cleavage between urban and rural areas has re-emerged with growing intensity across North America and Europe. Political scientists frequently assume that this widening electoral gap is driven by diverging policy attitudes and preferences between urban and rural residents. However, earlier studies examined only specific issues at single points in time, without tracking longitudinal trends.

Objectives

1. Test whether urban–rural policy disagreement is greater on cultural issues than on economic issues (Hypothesis 1). 2. Test the "progressive cities hypothesis" — whether urban voters consistently hold more progressive/left-leaning views than rural voters (Hypothesis 2). 3. Test whether the urban–rural policy preference divide has widened over recent decades (Hypothesis 3a vs. Hypothesis 3b).

Scope

The research covers an analysis of 5.3 million responses from 10 Canadian national election surveys (1993–2021), spanning 20 policy topic domains (gun control, immigration, Indigenous affairs, health, social welfare, taxation, foreign trade, and the environment) [1, 5, 35, 37, 42–44].

Key Provisions / Findings

  • Cultural vs. Economic Issue Divide (Hypothesis 1 Supported): More than half (51%) of all policy issue questions show a statistically significant urban–rural divide. The largest divides occur on cultural policy issues (gun control, Indigenous affairs, immigration), while divides on economic and social-welfare issues are considerably smaller.
  • Support for the Progressive Cities Hypothesis (Hypothesis 2 Supported): Urban residents hold progressive/left-leaning views on 75% of all questions and 90% of ideologically divided questions (the sole exception being free trade, due to strong support among highly educated urban workers) [1, 6, 25, 55–58].
  • Static Nature of the Policy Divide (Hypothesis 3b Supported): Across 10 elections (1993–2021), the urban–rural policy disagreement gap did not widen at all. The estimated time-trend line remains consistently flat across all models (p = 0.84) [59–63, 80].
  • Substantive Significance: The urban–rural policy preference divide proved larger than divides based on university education, gender, or union household membership.

Implementation

The methodology was implemented through 973 separate Bayesian multilevel linear regression models, with dependent and independent variables rescaled to 0–1, along with topic and repeated-question fixed-effects analysis [47, 59–62, 76, 77, 79]. Replication code and data are published openly on Scholars Portal Dataverse at `https://doi.org/10.5683/SP3/YNZMH8`.

Policy Issues

1. The mistaken assumption that geographic political polarization is caused by widening disagreement over public policy preferences. 2. The risk that political elites may exploit cultural issues to stoke place-based resentment for electoral gain.

Reform / Implications

The study recommends that political science shift its focus away from policy issue divergence toward alternative, non-issue mechanisms: 1. Affective Place Polarization: Rising emotional animosity between urban and rural groups. 2. Partisan-Ideological Sorting: Urban and rural voters increasingly sorting into parties aligned with pre-existing ideological preferences, without any underlying shift in policy attitudes. 3. Strategic Elite Issue Activation: The strategic activation of prominent cultural flashpoint issues by political elites.
