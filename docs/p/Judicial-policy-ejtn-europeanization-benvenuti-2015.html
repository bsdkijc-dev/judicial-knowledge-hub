The European Judicial Training Network Policy Framework for the Europeanization of National Judges is a transnational judicial training and governance policy framework regarding the Europeanization of national judges. Formulated by Simone Benvenuti (University of Rome "La Sapienza" & iCourts Copenhagen, 2015) in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), it analyzes the structure, funding, and operationalization of the European Judicial Training Network (EJTN / Réseau Européen de Formation Judiciaire). The framework is based on four core principles—Co-operation, Decentralization, Complementarity, and Targeting—as well as the concept of European Added Value (EAV), the Aiakos Exchange Programme, and knowledge management instruments (EU Law Court Coordinators such as Eurinfra in the Netherlands and European Gaius in Italy) to build the European Area of Freedom, Security and Justice [1–6, 14–16, 23–24, 28–31, 45–46].

Summary

Published by Simone Benvenuti in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework reviews the evolution of the European Judicial Training Network (EJTN) as the primary platform for judicial training in the European Union. Based on Articles 81 and 82 of the Lisbon Treaty and the 2011 European Commission Communication, the EU targeted training for 50% of legal practitioners by 2020. Benvenuti demonstrates that while the European Commission's massive scale targets were somewhat unrealistic, the EJTN has successfully executed its transnational coordination function effectively through a decentralized model based on 4 principles (Co-operation, Decentralization, Complementarity, Targeting) and the concept of European Added Value (EAV) [2, 28–31, 38–39]. Beyond the Aiakos exchange program, the long-term success of the Europeanization of judges is heavily determined by the development of transnational judicial networks and the establishment of EU Law Court Coordinators (such as the Eurinfra project in the Netherlands and European Gaius in Italy) [24, 43–46].

Legal Basis

  • Articles 81 & 82 TFEU (Lisbon Treaty): The formal legal foundation of the European Union for the development of judicial training in civil and criminal matters.
  • 2011 European Commission Communication: The policy document titled "Building trust in EU-wide justice: a new dimension to European judicial training."
  • Council Decision 2007/126/JHA: The basis for providing operating grants to the EJTN from the European Union budget [14–15].
  • Bordeaux Charter (2000): The foundational document for the establishment of the Réseau de Bordeaux network.
  • European Parliament Resolutions 2008 & 2012 (2012/2864(RSP)): Mandates supporting EU law coordinators in national courts [31, 45–46].
  • Not found in the available sources: Other specific formal statutory regulations are not mentioned in the source text.

Background

The creation of the European Area of Justice requires a uniform understanding of EU law among national judges. The EJTN was established to coordinate national judicial training institutions without eroding the legal autonomy of member states [2, 8, 30–31].

Objectives

1. Foster the creation of a common European judicial culture. 2. Coordinate judicial training standards and needs based on the principle of European Added Value (EAV) [4, 38–39]. 3. Organize cross-border judicial exchange programs (Exchange Programme & Aiakos) [23–25]. 4. Build knowledge management instruments through the network of EU Law Court Coordinators at the local court level [45–46].

Scope

This policy covers the governance of judicial training across the 28 EU Member States, national judicial schools (e.g., ENM France, CSM Italy, Escuela Judicial Spain), the Academy of European Law (ERA Trier), and transnational judicial associations.

Key Provisions

  • Four EJTN Governance Principles:
  1. *Co-operation*: Close collaboration between the EJTN, the European Commission, and professional judicial networks [28–29].
  2. *Decentralization*: The planning and execution of training remain in the hands of national judicial schools [29–31].
  3. *Complementarity*: European training complements rather than replaces the primary responsibilities of member states [31, 37–38].
  4. *Targeting*: Determining specific and effective target activities, such as the exchange programs.
  • Funding Mechanisms and Organizational Structure:
    • EU operating grants cover up to 95% of the EJTN’s total budget (€7.88 million in 2015) [15–16].
    • The Permanent Secretariat is located in Brussels with 3 Working Groups and specialized sub-working groups (Civil, Criminal, Administrative, Linguistics, Trainers) [14, 19–20].
  • EU Law Court Coordinators Instrument:
    • The establishment of specialized judges acting as EU law coordinators in local courts (the Dutch Eurinfra & Italian European Gaius models) to disseminate knowledge at the grassroots level.

Implementation

  • Aiakos Exchange Program: Facilitates reciprocal 2-week exchanges for young and future judges [24–25].
  • Coordinator Network Development: The institutionalization of EU law coordinator judges in the Netherlands, Italy, Slovenia, Croatia, and Hungary.

Policy Problems

  • Unrealistic Commission Targets: The imbalance between the European Commission's massive targets and the actual capacity for large-scale training [2, 36–37].
  • Risks of Executive Intervention: Attempts by executive agencies (such as EASO) to train judges, which face opposition for threatening judicial independence [32–33].

Reform / Change

  • Establishing the European Added Value (EAV) criteria as the primary filter for transnational training budget allocation [38–39].
  • Forming strategic partnerships with transnational judicial associations (such as the AEAJ) and institutionalizing court coordinators [43–46].

3 Most Important Improvements

1. National Institutionalization of the EU Law Court Coordinators Network: Adopt the Dutch Eurinfra and Italian European Gaius models across all member state courts to ensure the horizontal dissemination of EU legal knowledge at the local level [45–46]. 2.
