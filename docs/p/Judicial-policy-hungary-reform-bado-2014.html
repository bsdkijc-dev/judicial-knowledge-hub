The Hungarian Judicial Reform and Fair Trial Policy Framework is a judicial reform and judicial independence model of Hungary reviewed by Thomas de Weers (Utrecht School of Law, 2014) in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA) based on an edited volume by Prof. Attila Badó (2014). It analyzes the politico-legal impacts following the 2010 elections in Hungary—specifically the centralization of judicial administration under the National Office for the Judiciary (NOJ) and the National Judicial Council (NJC), the controversial right of the NOJ president to reassign cases, the politicization of judicial selection, and the judiciary's over-reliance on prosecutors (with a conviction rate of 96–97%). These factors are scrutinized for conflicting with the standards of judicial independence and the right to a lawful judge (fair trial) as outlined by the Venice Commission of the Council of Europe.

Summary

Published by Thomas de Weers in the International Journal for Court Administration (IJCA), this policy framework evaluates the book Fair Trial and Judicial Independence – Hungarian Perspectives, edited by Prof. Attila Badó. The landslide victory of Victor Orbán’s party in the 2010 elections triggered a constitutional overhaul and a radical restructuring of the Hungarian judicial system. The 2011 reforms centralized judicial administrative authority in the National Office for the Judiciary (NOJ), led by a president with sweeping powers, while simultaneously weakening the oversight functions of the National Judicial Council (NJC). The Venice Commission strongly criticized the NOJ President’s authority to reassign cases between courts as a violation of the right to a lawful judge. Furthermore, comparative studies exposed anomalies in Hungary's politically influenced judicial selection system and an excessively high criminal conviction rate (96–97%) stemming from an over-reliance on public prosecutors.

  • Judicial Reform Acts 2011: A package of judicial reform laws establishing the NOJ and NJC.

Background

Following the 2010 elections, the Hungarian Government utilized its qualified parliamentary majority to restructure the judiciary. This move sparked international criticism asserting that judicial independence in Hungary was under severe threat.

Objectives

1. Provide a comparative analysis of the Hungarian judicial reforms against international fair trial standards. 2. Assess the impact of centralizing power in the NOJ President and the weakened oversight functions of the NJC. 3. Compare the judicial selection system and evidence evaluation in Hungary with European and non-European jurisdictions. 4. Establish an academic foundation for merit-based judicial reform.

Scope

This policy encompasses the evaluation of judicial governance in Hungary and provides a comparative analysis of judicial systems in the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Russia, China, Azerbaijan, and Africa.

Key Provisions

  • Centralization of Administration Under the NOJ:
    • Transfer of central administrative power to the President of the NOJ, granting highly dominant executive authority.
    • Strict boundaries set by the Venice Commission rejecting the NOJ President’s authority to unilaterally reassign cases.
  • Weakening of NJC Oversight:
    • The National Judicial Council (NJC) was initially deprived of meaningful oversight authority over the actual decisions made by the NOJ.
  • Politicization of Judicial Selection and Evidence Evaluation:
    • Deviation of the judicial selection system from merit-based standards.
    • Excessive judicial reliance on public prosecutors, reflected in conviction rates reaching 96–97%.

Implementation

  • Establishment of the NOJ and NJC: The creation of two primary bodies managing the Hungarian judiciary based on the 2011 Judicial Reform Acts.
  • Partial Amendments: The execution of limited revisions to the judicial laws in response to Venice Commission recommendations to restrict the NOJ's authority and empower the NJC.

Policy Problems

  • Violation of the Right to a Lawful Judge: The practice of case reassignment by the NOJ President undermining the legal certainty of citizens.
  • Risk of Illiberal State Practices: The shift in the Hungarian judiciary mirroring the power dynamics found in illiberal states.

Reform / Change

  • Implementation of the Venice Commission's recommendations to limit the subjective authority of the NOJ President and strengthen the NJC.
  • Transitioning toward a merit-based judicial selection process free from political intervention.

3 Most Important Improvements

1. Complete Abolition of the Right to Reassign Cases: Ensure absolute compliance with the principle of the right to a lawful judge by strictly prohibiting the unilateral determination of judicial panels by the President of the NOJ. 2. Strengthening the Autonomy and Oversight Authority of the NJC: Grant the National Judicial Council (NJC) veto power and binding oversight authority over all strategic policies and personnel decisions made by the NOJ. 3. Implementation of an Open, Merit-Based Judicial Selection System: Replace politically influenced judicial selection mechanisms with an independent, transparent, and measurable selection process.
